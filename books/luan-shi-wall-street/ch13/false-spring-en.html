CHAPTER THIRTEEN · FALSE SPRING

After Bear: Lehman's Monday scare

The previous chapter’s fire-sale had just closed: on 16 March 2008, the Fed helped JPMorgan catch Bear Stearns, and the first domino seemed steadied. Bond trader Yuyang remembers that the real scare was the next day—Monday 17 March. Lehman Brothers, fourth among Wall Street’s “bulge-bracket five,” plunged more than 50% intraday before a partial rebound into the close.¹

Investors were spooked animals: any rumor could trigger withdrawals and a run. The liquidity threat was no longer one firm—it was the whole dealer funding chain: repo, customer cash, counterparty trust, all withdrawable overnight.

¹ 17 Mar 2008: Lehman’s intraday collapse scare Contemporary reports and crisis chronologies agree: on the first trading day after the Bear rescue package, fear rotated to “who’s next.” Lehman—smaller, highly leveraged, independent—fell more than 50% at the worst print before recovering somewhat into the close. The day turned a single-firm spiral into a system narrative.
Why it matters: the false spring begins only after fear is fully visible—without “who’s next,” there is no alphabet soup and no brief thaw.
Source: contemporaneous press on 17 Mar 2008 Lehman trading; BIS / crisis chronologies

Alphabet soup: TAF, TSLF, PDCF

Stabilization again fell to the central bank. Beyond rate cuts, the Fed rolled out facilities the press nicknamed alphabet soup—awkward acronyms that tracked how each new fracture forced a wider response.²

TAF (Term Auction Facility), from December 2007, auctioned term funds to “generally sound” depository institutions against discount-window-like collateral, with borrower identity confidential—avoiding discount-window stigma.³ Pure investment banks still could not tap TAF.

TSLF (Term Securities Lending Facility), announced 11 March 2008, let primary dealers pledge less-liquid securities to borrow Treasuries from the Fed, easing collateral scarcity in repo.⁴ The Fed stepped past its traditional bank-only perimeter.

PDCF (Primary Dealer Credit Facility)—sometimes rendered PDLF in Chinese accounts—launched 16 March beside the Bear deal: primary dealers could borrow overnight cash from the New York Fed against eligible collateral—an investment-bank discount window in all but name.⁵ The mask came off; the Fed became Wall Street’s emergency wet nurse.

Each new lettered medicine confessed that the last dose was not enough.
Section notes
² Alphabet soup and crisis escalation Across 2007–09 the Fed and Treasury introduced TAF, TSLF, PDCF, AMLF, CPFF, TALF and more. Market slang called the stack alphabet soup: longer names usually meant traditional tools had failed and eligible counterparties/collateral kept widening.
Why it matters: the book teaches institutional history through medicine labels—you need not memorize every acronym, but you should map each dose to a broken layer.
Source: Federal Reserve facility explainers; New York Fed speeches and Current Issues papers
³ TAF (announced 12 Dec 2007) The Term Auction Facility auctioned 28-day (later also 84-day) credit to sound depository institutions. Collateral resembled the discount window, but anonymity reduced stigma. Investment banks without a deposit franchise could not borrow—until March 2008’s TSLF/PDCF broke that wall.
Why it matters: marks the last “banks only” phase—dealers drink from the bottle next.
Source: federalreserve.gov Term Auction Facility overview
⁴ TSLF (11 Mar 2008) The Term Securities Lending Facility let primary dealers swap less-liquid collateral for up to ~28-day Treasuries from the New York Fed, then repo those Treasuries privately. It improved “good collateral” supply without directly expanding bank reserves—and reduced forced fire sales.
Why it matters: first systematic term securities lending to primary dealers (including investment banks)—critical scaffolding the week before Bear.
Source: NY Fed Current Issues on TSLF; FRBNY terms pages
⁵ PDCF (16 Mar 2008; also called PDLF in some Chinese texts) The Primary Dealer Credit Facility let primary dealers borrow overnight cash at the primary credit rate against eligible securities—functionally a dealer discount window. Official English acronym: PDCF; some Chinese narratives use PDLF for the same facility. Eligible collateral later widened with the crisis.
Why it matters: announced the same day as Bear’s rescue—markets read “the Fed will lend cash straight to dealers.”
Source: NY Fed Current Issues on PDCF; Fed press releases

Thaw: real-money buyers and the false spring

Liquidity plus a signal of Fed resolve lifted confidence. Capital markets that had been frozen for months began to thaw: auction-rate securities (ARS) failures eased, muni and corporate issuance revived, and credit spreads fell from March peaks.⁶

The pivotal buyers were not levered arb desks but real-money investors—pensions, insurers, mutual funds, wealthy individuals—using their own cash, not overnight repo. When they rotated out of money-market and Treasury “havens” into crisis-cheap munis, corporates, and equities, spring 2008’s false spring / Indian summer (小阳春) took shape.⁷

After leverage leaves, the people who save markets are often those holding cash and willing to mark to market slowly.
Section notes
⁶ ARS freeze and partial thaw Auction-rate securities reset rates via periodic auctions meant to mimic cash-like liquidity. In February 2008, after dealers stopped supporting auctions, failures cascaded and “cash-like” paper became long bonds. Into spring, some issuers shifted to conventional fixed-maturity munis and failures eased—a thermometer of thaw—even as ARS holders spent years in liquidity fights and regulatory settlements.
Why it matters: the desk story’s doorway—ARS failure rewrote short-muni product mix and client mix.
Source: SEC 2008 ARS settlement releases; contemporaneous muni-market coverage
⁷ Real money vs levered arbitrage Pre-crisis, cheap funding let arb traders dominate spread compression; in crisis they delevered and sold. Real-money accounts bought “cheap” paper with own funds—becoming temporary market saviors when the funding window cracked open. The BIS 2008 annual report notes post-Bear spread tightening even as interbank markets failed to heal fully.
Why it matters: explains the micro-mechanism of the false spring—not vanished risk, but a change of buyer type.
Source: BIS 78th Annual Report, financial markets chapter; crisis microstructure accounts

Desk view: munis go back to basics

For Yuyang, false spring was not an index abstraction. After the ARS “high-carry” episode, his remit widened across short bonds. Housing paper stayed quiet; short munis got busy—issuers replaced failed ARS with ~5-year fixed deals, and supply surged.

On the phones he watched the market “go back to basics”: structures simplified—plain fixed-rate, fixed-maturity bonds returned; fancy floaters and formula rates vanished. Buyers shifted from arb shops to households and institutions with real allocation needs. Cheapness was real; courage belonged to a different crowd.

Crisis teaches one lesson fast: complex structures can lose liquidity overnight; simple cash flows can still clear.

False spring: markets warm, real economy cools

From April to June 2008, U.S. equities and credit bounced; oil and soft commodities strengthened; “decoupling” talk returned—as if BRICs could tow the rich world out of the mud. At home, crisis lag effects hit the real economy: unemployment rose, house prices and consumption dragged each other down.⁸

The author’s interlocking metaphor (unemployment, consumption, house prices, housing investment) is the point: financial false spring and real-economy slide happened together. Screens went green while Main Street cooled.

Money-market gauges warned too: LIBOR–OIS and TED spreads never fully normalized; banks still hated lending to each other. Equity “happy talk” and money-market caution did not agree.⁹

False spring fools no weather station—it fools people who mistake a funding window for the end of the crisis.
Section notes
⁸ Real-economy lag: jobs and housing Public macro series show U.S. unemployment rising through the first half of 2008 while house prices and residential investment stayed weak. Consumption is ~70% of GDP; housing chains amplify via materials, durables, and jobs—financial shocks “come ashore” as recession with a lag of quarters.
Why it matters: nails the false spring to macro fact—screen rallies ≠ economic healing.
Source: BLS / public macro narratives; standard crisis timelines
⁹ LIBOR–OIS / TED: unhealed interbank markets After Bear, equities and some credit spreads tightened, yet LIBOR–OIS and TED stayed elevated versus pre-crisis norms; April 2008 also brought reporting controversies about lowball LIBOR quotes. BIS noted that by mid-May, interbank markets had not truly recovered and the credit improvement’s durability was unclear.
Why it matters: the trader’s thermometer—false spring is risk appetite, not restored funding trust.
Source: BIS annual report; Geithner NY Fed speech Apr 2008; contemporaneous money-market commentary

Funding window: Merrill cuts, Lehman stalls

For wounded dealers, the false spring was a precious, short funding window. The book runs Merrill versus Lehman as a controlled experiment: same storm, different captains, different willingness to cut flesh.

Merrill Lynch, after Stan O’Neal’s CDO-and-leverage disaster, hired NYSE’s John Thain. A parachuted CEO owed no loyalty to “crops he planted”: he sold toxic inventory (and some crown jewels) for cash while raising equity. A later public landmark: ~$30.6B notional of super-senior ABS CDOs sold for about $6.7B (~22¢ on the dollar) to a Lone Star affiliate—painful, but better than feeding the poison.¹⁰

Lehman’s long-serving chief Dick Fuld was battle-hardened and intolerant of dissent. 2007 hedges made Lehman look clever—and made him complacent enough to add some mortgage exposure into rebounds. In 2008, capital raises often felt market-forced; problem assets were “hide if you can, delay if you must”—book marks sometimes lagged observable CMBS-style indices by a wide margin.¹¹

Better to lance the boil than nurse the toxin—Thain chose pain; Fuld chose delay.
Section notes
¹⁰ Thain and Merrill’s CDO dump (public mid-2008) After becoming CEO in late 2007, John Thain pushed writedowns, sales, and capital raises. Around 28 July 2008 Merrill announced the sale of ~$30.6B notional U.S. super-senior ABS CDOs to a Lone Star affiliate for ~$6.7B, alongside a large equity raise. The deal sits at the end of the false-spring window—public evidence of “did you cut while you still could?”
Why it matters: foil to Lehman—same window, one strategy de-toxes and recaps, the other delays and denies.
Source: Merrill Lynch / SEC exhibit (Jul 2008); CNBC / contemporaneous coverage
¹¹ Fuld and Lehman’s delay narrative Reporting and later inquiries describe Fuld’s long reign: 2007 hedges limited early writedowns relative to peers, but 2008 pace on marks, capital, and asset sales was widely seen as slow; commercial-real-estate-linked books often trailed observable indices. Critics likened it to rearranging deck chairs after the iceberg.
Why it matters: personality and governance enter the balance sheet—one-man rule delays get levered ~30× into survival risk.
Source: MarketWatch and other contemporary profiles; post-crisis investigation summaries

Einhorn vs Callan: seeds of distrust

From April 2008, Greenlight Capital’s David Einhorn publicly challenged Lehman’s CDO and problem-asset disclosure, capital adequacy, and leverage—and said he was short.¹² CFO Erin Callan sparred with him in the press. Two rising stars made great TV—but for a high-leverage dealer that drinks capital-markets milk daily, attention itself is risk.

In early June, Lehman reported a large quarterly loss and raised on the order of $6 billion; Fuld replaced Callan and other executives—but not the captain. The ship had hit the reef; the deck chairs were rearranged again.

You can argue with a hedge fund’s short thesis; once trust cracks, repo counterparties do not read your press release.
Section notes
¹² Einhorn’s short and public challenges (incl. Ira Sohn) David Einhorn discussed financials risks from late 2007 and intensified Lehman critiques in spring–summer 2008, including Ira Sohn remarks, while openly short; sparring with Erin Callan was widely covered. After June’s loss and capital raise, markets filed “Einhorn called it” into memory—seeds sown in the false spring, germination after autumn’s storm.
Why it matters: information warfare accelerates liquidity crises—narrative prices funding spreads.
Source: public Greenlight / Ira Sohn materials as reported; contemporary financial press

Quiet before the storm

The chapter ends in dangerous comfort: spreads on screens have tightened, munis can clear again, Fed alphabet soup still pours—yet unemployment rises, Lehman argues, Merrill cuts, and the tsunami of the next chapter (“Two Beauties in Distress”) still gathers in deep current.

From the bond desk, false spring is the sound of real-money orders—and a thermometer of Indian summer. After Indian summer, what comes is often not autumn but a colder winter.

The false spring’s poison is convincing people they still have time to cut slowly—until the window slams.
