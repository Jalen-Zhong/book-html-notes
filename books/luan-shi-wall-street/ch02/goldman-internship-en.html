CHAPTER TWO · GOLDMAN INTERNSHIP

Special-forces vibe: autumn in IBD Strategies

In autumn 2005 the author walked into Goldman Sachs Investment Banking Division Strategies as an intern. The culture did not feel like a normal corporation: it moved like special forces — smart, hungry, willing to carry weight. Much of that temperament traced, in firm lore, to J. Aron, the commodities-and-FX house Goldman bought in 1981, which seeded a generation of risk-tolerant traders¹. Lloyd Blankfein rose on that line: precious-metals salesman at J. Aron, then currencies and commodities leadership, then the top of the firm; by 2006 he would be chairman and CEO. Around 2005, the firm’s appetite for risk was being lifted by exactly that trading bloodline².

For someone fresh from Berkeley’s financial-engineering program and finally sitting near the front office, the culture was both intoxicating and crushing: you were not here to “sample a workplace”; you were here to prove you belonged at the table.

Section notes
¹ J. Aron: Goldman’s commodities and FX lineage In 1981 Goldman acquired client J. Aron & Company — a family commodities house with New Orleans roots in coffee, later precious metals and FX. Public firm history frames the deal as a way to deepen international commodities and currency reach. After the purchase, J. Aron’s trading culture and talent pipeline filtered into Goldman and helped shape the later FICC powerhouse.
Why it matters: the “special forces” and risk appetite in this chapter are not slogans; they map onto a documented 1981 acquisition and Blankfein’s promotion path.
Sources: Goldman Sachs history moment “1981 J. Aron”; New York Times, 30 Oct 1981
² Blankfein: from metals salesman to CEO Lloyd Blankfein joined J. Aron in 1982 (already under Goldman) as a precious-metals salesperson, made partner in 1988, later led currencies and commodities, became president and COO in 2003–04, and succeeded Hank Paulson as chairman and CEO in 2006. Public narratives cast him as the trader who remade Goldman’s risk appetite and profit mix.
Why it matters: the internship sits on the eve of trading DNA taking the corner office — the firm’s “dare to wager” mood had a concrete personnel backdrop.
Sources: Goldman Sachs history “2006 Blankfein”; Wikipedia / NYT DealBook public career summaries

Because we are Goldman: bonuses and the bottom cut

Goldman’s first lesson is often compensation, not models. In public filings and press, 2005 was a record year: compensation and benefits on the order of $11.7 billion — a rough per-employee average near half a million dollars, with trading and partner pay far higher and intern pay a different story³. Beside the money sat the knife: roughly the bottom 5% were pushed out. Those who stayed pep-talked each other with a near-creed: Because we are Goldman.

That mix of incentive and fear sat on top of the post-1999 identity: still called “partners,” already a public company — the title kept its Holy-Grail glow while the capital structure had already changed generations⁴. For an intern, the bonus myth and the cull knife swung in the same room.

Section notes
³ 2005: a publicly reported record pay year Goldman’s 2005 results put compensation and benefits near $11.69 billion, about 47% of net revenue, across roughly 22,000 employees. Contemporary media hyped year-end bonus pools; managing directors could clear millions in salary-plus-bonus. The figures explain both the magnet of the brand and why a bottom-cut culture could still hold under pressure.
Why it matters: the later “bonus season” and Christmas-party mood only make sense inside this compensation boom.
Sources: Goldman 2005 results / SEC EX-99.1; eFinancialCareers, Feb 2006
⁴ The 1999 IPO: partnership halo, public shell On 4 May 1999 Goldman listed at $53 a share (ticker GS), raising about $3.66 billion — then one of the largest U.S. financial IPOs. The prospectus cited permanent capital, broader employee ownership, and acquisition currency. Decades of partnership debate had feared cultural dilution. After listing, the partner / MD ladder and rituals remained, but the legal form was The Goldman Sachs Group, Inc.
Why it matters: chasing the “partner track” in this chapter means competing for old-partnership status badges inside a public company.
Sources: Goldman Sachs history “1999 IPO”; CNN Money, 3 May 1999; SEC prospectus summaries

Intern work: SecDB, narrative, and Jerry

Days were not all client dinners. The author did heavy financial analysis and brushed against internal tool names: SecDB, plus UFO and YAMS as the book’s desk slang — opaque to outsiders, the actual desktop for Strategies⁵. Turning models into client-ready stories often fell to professors-turned-MDs: people who could both run the math and sell the narrative.

A manager named Jerry — J. Aron track, on the partner path — enforced a ruthless deliverables culture: on time, clean versions, numbers that would survive the next client meeting. For an intern, that beat any slogan: your worth was whether tonight’s slide was table-ready.

Strategies felt like a hidden assembly line: quant minds make it add up, narrative minds make it land, MDs sell it. Interns sit in the middle and serve both ends.
Section notes
⁵ SecDB: the public story of Securities Database Securities Database (SecDB) grew in the early 1990s out of J. Aron’s FX and commodities risk needs, launched around 1992–93, then spread across equities and fixed income as firmwide risk plumbing. Press long called it an internal “secret weapon”; in the 2010s pieces of capability reached clients via Marquee. Names like UFO and YAMS appear in the author’s internship context as internal tools/process codes with little independent public documentation — treat them as desk toolchain labels, not invent technical detail.
Why it matters: explains why a Strategies internship smelled so technical — Goldman long treated software as a trading weapon, not mere IT support.
Sources: Goldman Sachs history “1993 SecDB”; Fortune / WSJ on Marquee & SecDB; public SecDB / Michael Dubno overviews

Elevator gossip and the insider-trading lesson

One day, unfinished-deal gossip drifted through an elevator. Compliance and colleagues moved fast afterward: what you may hear, what you must never do, and how information can harden into material nonpublic information (MNPI) — the raw material of insider-trading cases⁶. The same season, markets argued over the NYSE–Archipelago merger: Goldman advised both sides, held Archipelago stock, and had historical ties to exchange leadership — lawsuits and judicial commentary flayed that multi-hat role in public⁷.

The practical advice was pure Wall Street: for sensitive talk, prefer the phone to email — email leaves a forwardable trail. For a newcomer this was not a how-to on cutting corners; it was a warning that information boundaries here are sharper than any campus ethics seminar.

Section notes
⁶ Insider trading and MNPI: the boundary course Under U.S. securities law, trading on material nonpublic information — or tipping for personal benefit — can be illegal; bankers, lawyers, and other temporary insiders are in scope. Compliance training hammers walls, watch lists, restricted lists, and the duty to report what you hear without trading or tipping family. Elevator gossip is dangerous precisely because the source is informal yet the content may still be MNPI.
Why it matters: this chapter turns “overhearing gossip” into a career life-or-death line — Keen’s later fate is the cautionary payoff.
Sources: SEC / U.S. insider-trading primers; public IB compliance training summaries
⁷ 2005 NYSE–Archipelago: the dual-advisor storm In April 2005 the NYSE announced a combination with electronic venue Archipelago on the path to becoming a public company. Goldman’s public role was tangled: facilitator/advisor to both sides, holder of roughly 15% of Archipelago, NYSE seat owner; NYSE CEO John Thain was a Goldman alumnus. Seat holders sued over conflicts; a New York judge later still blasted Goldman’s multiple roles while clearing related settlements. The deal advanced, but “can a bank serve both sides?” became that year’s open seminar.
Why it matters: the book uses the deal to show how “clever” and “clean” tear apart under public light — what the intern heard was the industry’s reputation stress test.
Sources: NBC / LA Times / NY Post 2005 coverage; Archipelago SEC 8-K; Higgins litigation public summaries

Keen’s later path: do not follow

Keen — the Berkeley interviewer from Chapter 1 who grilled him for hours and smiled when every answer landed — later drifted into an insider-trading ring. The author writes it almost as a warning label: the shortcut looks like “information edge”; the destination is regulators and prison. For readers, the elevator lesson upgrades from policy to a human wreck — do not follow.

The internship teaches two weapons: SecDB and models, and reverence for information boundaries. Without the second, the first only helps you fail faster.

Bonus season 2005, Christmas party, NYU fair

Year-end, Wall Street bathed in 2005’s record-bonus air. Inside Goldman, acronyms like SSG and Triple Play floated at parties — fireworks for an intern: bright, not yet fully decoded. The Christmas party glittered; between toasts the author felt outgunned — surrounded by people closer to revenue accounts and smoother at the story.

So he went to a New York University recruiting fair — not to discard Goldman’s glow, but to open another door: if the full-time seat was not on this Strategies line, a trading, prop, or rates seat might be closer to the table he had wanted since the casino years.

Section notes
⁸ Chinese bank reform and the Goldman–ICBC line (same-era public facts) 2005–06 was peak season for Chinese big-bank restructuring and strategic investors. Public records show that in January 2006 a Goldman-led consortium (with Allianz and American Express) signed to invest about $3.78 billion for roughly 8.45% of ICBC, Goldman itself near 5.75%, plus governance and risk-management cooperation ahead of ICBC’s IPO. For a Chinese observer interning at Goldman, such deals were live textbooks on mutual need between China and Wall Street — even when this chapter’s plot is a personal job hunt.
Why it matters: pins the smallness of a Christmas party against the same year’s Asian financial-reform table — gilding was not only in the champagne.
Sources: ICBC English press releases (Jan 2006 strategic investment); Wharton case PDF summaries

Meeting Rick: “Lucy Bank” prop / rates

At the NYU fair he met Rick — from the book’s aliased “Lucy Bank,” on a proprietary-trading / rates-related team. They talked bonds, rates, and the CFA: the charter path as a common signal in fixed income and portfolio work⁹. The résumé line Goldman intern was already a door-knocker in the 2005 market.

Prop desks then were still banks’ relatively free corners for betting the house’s capital: high payouts, real autonomy, not yet remade by the later Volcker Rule¹⁰. To someone who had trained expected value at blackjack and poker, the seat smelled more like a true table than client narrative decks. The chapter closes on that pull — the Goldman internship opened a door; the next table might not be in the same building.

Section notes
⁹ CFA: the three-level investment passport The Chartered Financial Analyst program (CFA Institute) typically runs three exam levels: Level I tools and terms, Level II valuation application, Level III portfolio management and integration (with newer pathway options at III). After exams and work experience, candidates may become charterholders. For rates and bond seats, the CFA often signals “I seriously studied investments” — the author had already finished Level III at Berkeley, so Rick’s bond talk synced immediately.
Why it matters: explains how “CFA + bonds” builds instant common language at a fair — and echoes Chapter 1’s signaling story.
Sources: CFA Institute public program overviews
¹⁰ Bank proprietary trading circa 2005 Before the crisis, large banks commonly ran prop desks that traded the firm’s own capital, with successful traders taking a slice of P&L. The 2005 hiring market still sold prop as a playground for the sharp. Post-2008 regulation, especially the U.S. Volcker Rule under Dodd-Frank, sharply limited deposit-taking banks’ proprietary trading; many desks moved to hedge funds or shut. Read this chapter knowing “Lucy Bank” prop is a pre-Volcker species.
Why it matters: without that era marker, you miss why prop felt like the casino skill set promoted into a real job.
Sources: eFinancialCareers 2004–05 prop pay/culture pieces; Volcker Rule background primers
