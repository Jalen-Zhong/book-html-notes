      <h2 id="s2">Because we are Goldman: bonuses and the bottom cut</h2>
      <p>Goldman’s first lesson is often compensation, not models. In public filings and press, 2005 was a record year: compensation and benefits on the order of $11.7 billion — a rough per-employee average near half a million dollars, with trading and partner pay far higher and intern pay a different story<a class="fn-link" href="#fn3"><sup class="fn">³</sup></a>. Beside the money sat the knife: roughly the bottom 5% were pushed out. Those who stayed pep-talked each other with a near-creed: Because we are Goldman.</p>
      <p>That mix of incentive and fear sat on top of the post-1999 identity: still called “partners,” already a public company — the title kept its Holy-Grail glow while the capital structure had already changed generations<a class="fn-link" href="#fn4"><sup class="fn">⁴</sup></a>. For an intern, the bonus myth and the cull knife swung in the same room.</p>

      <h2 id="s3">Intern work: SecDB, narrative, and Jerry</h2>
      <p>Days were not all client dinners. The author did heavy financial analysis and brushed against internal tool names: SecDB, plus UFO and YAMS as the book’s desk slang — opaque to outsiders, the actual desktop for Strategies<a class="fn-link" href="#fn5"><sup class="fn">⁵</sup></a>. Turning models into client-ready stories often fell to professors-turned-MDs: people who could both run the math and sell the narrative.</p>
      <p>A manager named Jerry — J. Aron track, on the partner path — enforced a ruthless deliverables culture: on time, clean versions, numbers that would survive the next client meeting. For an intern, that beat any slogan: your worth was whether tonight’s slide was table-ready.</p>

      <div class="scene">
        Strategies felt like a hidden assembly line: quant minds make it add up, narrative minds make it land, MDs sell it. Interns sit in the middle and serve both ends.

      <h2 id="s4">Elevator gossip and the insider-trading lesson</h2>
