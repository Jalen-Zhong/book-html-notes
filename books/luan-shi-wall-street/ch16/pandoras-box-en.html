CHAPTER SIXTEEN · Pandora’s Box

Largest bankruptcy: liquidity vs insolvency

After the failed red-line weekend, the United States recorded what many called its largest bankruptcy: an investment bank with nearly $700 billion in book assets, more than $100 billion in bonds outstanding, and roughly 1.5 million derivative contracts—collapsed at the core of the dealer and counterparty web.¹

The narrator unpacks “bankruptcy” in trader English: not a poetic fall, but the inability to pay principal and interest as promised, followed by a court filing for bankruptcy protection and an orderly wind-down or restructuring under the code. Firms usually fail in two ways—“sudden” and “broke.” Lehman’s first shove was sudden: assets on paper might still exceed liabilities, yet the firm lived on overnight and short-term funding. When capital markets cut the milk, it could not stand. Fire-selling $700 billion of assets would crush prices; at roughly thirty-times leverage, the equity cushion vanished and creditors took the hit—sudden became broke.

Nine-cent recovery: creditors at the heart

Weeks later, a CDS auction set Lehman’s recovery rate near nine cents on the dollar for senior unsecured debt.² Where did the money go? A key mechanism was collateral on derivatives: when marks moved against you, you posted more. Counterparties, seeing Lehman wobble, hiked demands—and after the filing, posted collateral typically stayed with them. Insiders took the “meat”; holders of unsecured commercial paper and ordinary bonds often got the “bones.”³

From U.S. money-market funds to overseas pensions and retail note holders, many outsiders learned that a glittering name is not a seatbelt for unsecured creditors. Equity wiped out in a Bear-style rescue is skin damage; deep creditor losses hit the load-bearing wall—because modern banking rolls on debt confidence that funds the real economy.

Equity losses are cuts in the skin; creditor losses crack the beam—that is when the building sways.
Section notes
² CDS auction recovery near 8.625–9 cents Under the October 2008 ISDA auction protocol for Lehman CDS, the publicly reported final recovery was about 8.625 cents per dollar (often rounded to “nine cents” in popular accounts). The print cemented the story that unsecured creditors were also crushed.
Why it matters: pins an abstract “loss” to a verifiable number readers remember.
Sources: Contemporary ISDA/Creditex auction reports; crisis chronologies
³ Derivatives collateral and “meat first” OTC derivatives typically require mark-to-market margin. Before Lehman’s filing, counterparties raised initial and variation margin, accelerating the liquidity drain; after filing, pledged collateral often satisfied derivative counterparties ahead of unsecured creditors. The Valukas examiner report and crisis literature stress this structure.
Why it matters: explains how a firm can look asset-rich while unsecured debt trades like nine cents.
Sources: Valukas examiner-report summaries; FCIC; derivatives-margin practice reviews

The implicit-guarantee seal: Pandora’s Box

For years, investors half-believed the U.S. government would not let a true load-bearing wall of the financial system fall—from LTCM to Bear Stearns to the GSEs, last-minute rescues worked like a seal holding runs, contagion, and systemic liquidation inside “Pandora’s Box.”⁴

As bailout costs rose, so did moral-hazard fury. Paulson drew a line with Wall Street and let Lehman go—perhaps to teach discipline. Once the seal tore, what leapt out was not only one bank’s funeral but a re-pricing of the entire credit chain.

The seal whispered “too big to fail.” The moment it tore, everyone asked: then who is still allowed to fall?
Section notes
⁴ Implicit guarantees, TBTF, and the Pandora metaphor Crisis literature discusses how Too-Big-To-Fail expectations lowered large firms’ funding costs. Leaving Lehman unsaved broke some investors’ linear extrapolation of that guarantee; the money-fund, commercial-paper, and AIG cascade then shoved policymakers from “exemplary punishment” toward “system stabilization.” The book’s “Pandora’s Box” is a literary name for that institutional turn.
Why it matters: gives the chapter title its policy meaning—from saving one firm to asking what else is in the box.
Sources: FCIC Final Report; Bernanke/Paulson retrospectives; public chronologies

Monday on the floor: noise as a signal

On Monday, 15 September, Yuyang reached Lucy Bank’s trading floor before 7:30. The more chaotic the market, the louder the floor—and this morning’s shouting and flashing screens were unlike anything in his career: equity futures plunging, Treasury yields diving (flight to safety), interbank rates spiking, moves several to ten times a normal day. The mess pointed one way: investors fleeing anything with credit risk, treating bank credit like plague.⁵

The building swayed. The question was no longer “how do we trade Lehman,” but: what jumps out of the box next?

The red and green on the screens are not the noise; the noise itself is the tape—trust evaporating by the decibel.
Section notes
⁵ 15 September risk-off and funding stress Market data and chronologies show risk assets sold hard on Lehman’s filing day, while U.S. Treasuries bid up and bank spreads/CDS blew out. Dealer and counterparty risk systems refreshed limits repeatedly—the book’s “blacklist” motif matches that operational reality.
Why it matters: lands the macro shock in a trader’s bodily sense of the floor.
Sources: FRASER / St. Louis Fed crisis timeline; contemporary market reports

Reserve Primary breaks the buck

The next day, headlines named a fund most traders had barely heard of: the Reserve Primary Fund. One of the oldest money-market funds, with on the order of $60 billion in assets, it was supposed to behave like enhanced cash—short, safe paper, daily liquidity, and the long myth of a stable $1.00 NAV.⁶

It held about $785 million face of Lehman commercial paper—roughly 1% of assets. For a “principal won’t be lost” money fund, even a tiny mark can trigger a run on the redemption option. Within hours, liquidity failed: short paper could not be turned into cash fast enough. Then NAV fell below $1.00—breaking the buck. Panic upgraded: if even this was unsafe, what besides Treasuries remained? Equities, credit, CP, repo, money funds—non-Treasury funding channels bled together. The first plague out of the box was bankers’ oldest nightmare: a system-wide run.⁷

When the boring product breaks, fear peaks—because people had treated it as money itself.
Section notes
⁶ Reserve Primary Fund and breaking the buck After Lehman’s filing, Reserve Primary faced massive redemptions; once ~$785 million of Lehman debt was written down, the fund reported a NAV near $0.97 on 16 September—the first major retail money-market fund to break the buck. SEC and court files document tens of billions in redemption pressure.
Why it matters: moves contagion from “peer dealers” to “cash substitutes ordinary people trusted.”
Sources: SEC/court summaries; Yale Journal of Financial Crises (Reserve Primary); FRASER timeline
⁷ Commercial paper and the money-fund run Money funds are major buyers of commercial paper. Breaking the buck and redemptions crushed CP demand, impairing rollover funding for financial and nonfinancial issuers—a shadow-banking run. Treasury later launched temporary guarantees (touched here; expanded in later policy chapters).
Why it matters: shows why a dealer bankruptcy can freeze seemingly unrelated corporate short-term funding.
Sources: FCIC; Federal Reserve/Treasury September 2008 policy summaries

AIG on the blacklist: the AAA no-collateral trap

For leveraged Wall Street, the run felt like a stack of death warrants. Lucy Bank’s risk desk refreshed the blacklist several times in a day—and American International Group (AIG) appeared. Built on traditional insurance, AIG had plunged into financial products; in the CDS market its AAA rating once bought a privilege: by market custom, ultra-high-grade names often posted little or no initial collateral, collecting premium on a golden balance-sheet brand.⁸

It insured vast notionals of highly rated structured bonds (including subprime-linked). After 2008 losses and problem-asset exposures surfaced, the stock slid; the parent’s commercial-paper funding tightened—regulated insurance subsidiaries could not casually upstream cash. After the GSE conservatorship and Lehman’s death spiral, a downgrade rumor alone could kill: lower ratings instantly demanded mountains of collateral.

Section notes
⁸ AIG Financial Products and AAA “no-collateral” custom AIGFP sold large volumes of CDS; its high rating secured favorable collateral terms on many contracts. As housing and structured prices worsened—and ratings fell—enormous margin calls arrived. Public records stress the coupling of parent liquidity with derivative collateral terms, not a cartoon of “the whole insurer was insolvent overnight.”
Why it matters: clarifies how an insurance giant could fail like a dealer.
Sources: FCIC ch. 19; New York Fed AIG actions page; Congressional/GAO after-action summaries

Tuesday downgrade and the $85 billion rescue

During the Lehman weekend, AIG also hunted capital while officials still preferred a private fix. Bankers who opened the books found marks on similar problem assets far more optimistic than Lehman’s—the hole was deeper. Failed fundraising plus Lehman’s filing: on Tuesday the stock was cut again; Moody’s, S&P, and Fitch downgraded the same afternoon, the last straw for liquidity—public narratives put incremental collateral needs in the tens of billions.⁹

When private plans with funds and JPMorgan collapsed, the math said roughly $85 billion—only Uncle Sam could write that check. Under emergency Section 13(3) authority, the New York Fed extended up to $85 billion of credit, taking about 79.9% equity for a Treasury-benefit trust, at roughly LIBOR + 8.5%: the firm lived; old shareholders were nearly drained. Paulson had just refused Lehman and was forced to save AIG—because an insurer’s failure would hammer CDS-buying banks and risk runs on policyholders far beyond Wall Street.

The red line would not save an investment bank; once the box opened, an insurer had to be saved—moral-hazard debate met a countdown to system failure.
Section notes
⁹ Key terms of the 16 September 2008 AIG facility The Federal Reserve Board authorized the New York Fed to lend AIG up to $85 billion under Section 13(3), initially often described as three-month LIBOR plus 850 basis points, with about 79.9% equity interest transferred to a trust for the U.S. Treasury; the CEO was replaced. Later restructurings (TARP preferred, Maiden Lane vehicles) followed—this chapter focuses on the first-night political and market shock.
Why it matters: the terms explain why markets called it a blood-draining rescue and why equity felt sacrificed.
Sources: Federal Reserve / New York Fed releases; FRASER timeline; FCIC

NYSE and the short ban: a mad week

On Wednesday, Yuyang escorted a visiting Chinese delegation onto the NYSE floor—louder than Lucy Bank’s own pit. Shortly after the open the Dow plunged; bank stocks cratered. Goldman and Morgan Stanley sold off hard, and the risk blacklist updated again: the last two standalone investment banks were on it. Hedge-fund “vultures” shorted stock and bought CDS; banks from the U.K. to Belgium to Washington Mutual were shoved toward the cliff.¹⁰

From Thursday, the U.K. temporarily banned short sales in financials; the U.S. SEC followed; other countries copied—an administrative emergency brake under a free-market banner. Shorts covered; equities ripped higher Thursday and Friday. By Friday evening the floor quieted and autumn light fell across the desks: versus the Friday close before Lehman’s filing, the S&P and some Treasury yields were almost back where they started—a visitor from Mars might think nothing had happened. In seven days, Wall Street had changed worlds.

Section notes
¹⁰ September 2008 financial short-sale bans Around 18 September, the U.K. FSA temporarily restricted short sales in financial stocks; the U.S. SEC imposed an emergency ban on a basket of financials (later extended/adjusted). Contemporary coverage and later evaluations debate effectiveness, but the tool is a standard part of the crisis-week record. Banks including HBOS, RBS, Fortis, and Washington Mutual were under acute pressure that week.
Why it matters: closes the “mad week”—the ironic turn from market freedom to administrative short limits.
Sources: SEC/FSA September 2008 releases; FT/WSJ contemporary digests; crisis chronologies

Back near the origin: toward the Paulson Plan

Prices near the prior Friday do not mean risk is. Creditors burned, the money-fund myth cracked, AIG effectively majority-owned by the state, remaining dealers trembling on blacklists—the box was open. The next chapter enters the Paulson Plan: Treasury goes to Congress for authority to buy troubled assets and inject capital at scale (later TARP), trying to force the plagues back under a larger institutional lid.¹¹

Closing his trade blotter, Yuyang knew: chaos was not one candlestick—it was the week when trust, collateral, and political red lines were rewritten together.

Prices can return to the origin; Pandora’s Box does not close so easily.
Section notes
¹¹ Toward TARP / the “Paulson Plan” In late September 2008, Paulson’s team asked Congress for a large troubled-asset program; after political combat it became the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) under the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act. Book §17 “Paulson Plan” covers that fight; this chapter treats AIG and money-fund shocks as the legislative foreshock.
Why it matters: hands the reader from a trading week to the next institutional climax—so the short ban is not mistaken for the ending.
Sources: U.S. Treasury/Congress public timelines; FCIC; public TOC (§17)
