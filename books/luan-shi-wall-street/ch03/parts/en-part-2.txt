ften meant: not a pure Goldman/Morgan Stanley brand, but a balance-sheet giant — half a rung lower on the prestige ladder in a classmate’s ear.
          <div class="why">Why it matters: Frank’s shock is the chapter’s first drumbeat of prestige versus a real seat.</div>

          <strong>² Lehman’s halo in 2005–06</strong>

For graduates then, “I’m going to Lehman” was the explainable Wall Street sentence; “a commercial bank” was harder to sell at dinner.

        <div class="fn-item" id="fn3">
          <strong>³ Pork-belly futures: unglamorous, real trading</strong>
          CME launched frozen pork-belly futures in 1961 — among the earliest frozen-meat storage contracts — roughly 40,000 pounds per contract, long a hedge-and-speculation arena for the bacon chain. As the industry shifted to fresh processing and volume collapsed, CME delisted the contract on 15 July 2011. In trader folklore, “pork bellies” became shorthand for getting on a tape that actually moves — wet-market branding, pit-desk reality.
          <div class="why">Why it matters: “I’d rather trade pork bellies” is not a joke; it is the extreme form of “give me a seat, not a staff job.”</div>

      <h2 id="s2">Interview marathon: Mark, free salad, “40 under 40”</h2>
      <p>Lucy Bank’s interviews felt like an endurance event. Rates boss Mark walked in and took a free salad — the casual perk of Wall Street interview rooms, and a quiet status test. After the résumé, his warmth cooled: almost no traditional finance work experience. Contests and models could not erase the frown of “you haven’t lived in the money pile.”</p>
      <p>The author scrambled with flattery: he’d heard Mark had made a “40 under 40” style list — the media badge that packages sub-forty rising stars as industry royalty<a class="fn-link" href="#fn4"><sup class="fn">⁴</sup></a>. The room thawed a notch. Mark tossed off his own story: roughly $5 million of first-year trading profit against a bonus expectation that might have been around $50,000 — a slap of gap numbers that doubled as a recruiting poster.</p>

      <div class="scene">
        Free salad, résumé frown, “40 under 40” flattery, five million versus fifty thousand — power was on the table before munis were.

      <h2 id="s3">Apple guy and the French quant boss</h2>
      <p>Next came the swap trader colleagues called “Apple guy” — he grabbed a free apple, then lobbed math questions while chewing. Interest-rate swaps are the vernacular of rates derivatives: one side pays fixed, the other floating, to hedge or express a view on the path of rates<a class="fn-link" href="#fn5"><sup class="fn">⁵</sup></a>. The questions were not sadistic, but the tone was lukewarm — filtering out people who cannot compute, not hunting a partner.</p>
      <p>Then the French quant boss: a joking Bonjour, a Mandarin pun landing like “stupid pig” (笨猪), then hard quant problems. The author felt outmatched — not because he could not do arithmetic, but because the density and desk context yanked him from “exam ace” back to “hasn’t lived on the curve yet.”</p>
