CHAPTER TWENTY-FOUR · Unite and Divide · Part 4 The Road Turns

The cycle maxim: New Year 2010 & policy wealth

The Times Square ball drops again; New Year bells ring. Early 2010, Wall Street looks festive. After §23—nets lubricated, distribution humming daily—this chapter zooms out: above personal guanxi, whole markets swing between union and division. ¹

The Romance of the Three Kingdoms opens with “long divided must unite; long united must divide.” Yuyang lifts that maxim onto Wall Street cycles: banks consolidate and break up, bubbles gather and burst, regulation loosens and tightens. Yet the post-tsunami rebound still startles—fiscal stimulus and Fed ease were meant to shield Main Street; Main Street barely stirs, while Wall Street, nearer the spigot, books a fat round of policy wealth. ²

Golden-age echo and Greece's discordant note

The financial “golden age” is back; “collecting” is fashionable again. Liquidity tides push Wall Street “collectors” worldwide; hot money floods emerging markets and reheats assets—global markets look uncomfortably like 2006. Yet in the new gold rush a discordant note rises: Greek sovereign debt surfaces. ³

When the boom echo is loudest, peripheral sovereign cracks begin to speak.
Section notes
³ Greek sovereign debt crisis: public timeline (primer) Greece enjoyed cheaper funding as a euro-area member; after the global crisis, capital fled, deficit and statistics controversies erupted, and sovereign funding seized up. Spring 2010 saw escalation, EU/IMF support talks, and austerity politics. Public economic history treats this as a fuse of the euro periphery crisis. The story uses “discordant note” as mood; the footnote supplies a checkable calendar.
Why it matters: nails the boom echo to a real geo-financial event, not abstract bubble rhetoric.
Source: EU/IMF Greece-aid reporting; euro-debt crisis chronologies

PIGS: eurozone debt logic

Everyone wants “work less, earn more”—the trial read contrasts earlier retirement and generous pensions to show comfortable living long financed by large fiscal deficits: a nation living on tomorrow’s grain. Before the euro, currency depreciation could still digest debt; after joining, foreign inflows and the “euro country” badge compressed yields, and the good life continued a few more years. When capital left and accounting tricks showed, bonds stopped selling. ⁴

Greece is not alone: Portugal, Spain, and Italy rhyme—living standards ahead of productive capacity, deficits too large. Wags stitch the English initials into “PIGS,” a dark twin of “BRICs” (media later often wrote PIIGS, adding Ireland).

A gold-letter badge can compress yields; it cannot cancel the repayment date.
Section notes
⁴ PIGS / PIIGS: media shorthand and the euro periphery Post-crisis media used PIGS (Portugal, Italy, Greece, Spain) or PIIGS (plus Ireland) for fiscally stressed euro members. The acronym is pejorative; research prefers neutral “periphery / sovereign spread” language. The institutional point is public: inside a monetary union, lost independent monetary policy and devaluation make fiscal imbalance more likely to appear as debt crisis and austerity conflict.
Why it matters: behind the joke label sits the constraint that makes “be careful with euro assets” a trading discipline.
Source: euro-debt media/economic primers; public trial-read PIGS beat (rewritten)

Careful sailing: cut risk, prefer liquidity

In spring 2010 PIGS worsen, yet markets are not yet violent: investors grow cautious on euro assets while staying warm elsewhere. Yuyang recalls early-2007 subprime: a chill—trades must continue, but “careful sailing lasts ten thousand years.” He cuts bond risk, shrinks “collectibles,” and tilts toward high-quality liquid bonds, keeping a runway.

Boom playbooks can wait; the retreat pontoon must be checked first.
Section notes
⁵ Liquidity preference: trading discipline at early-warning stages When uncertainty rises, fixed-income desks commonly shorten duration, cut illiquid “collectible” risk, and raise share of high-quality quick-to-sell assets—risk-management primer, not hindsight. Public retrospectives often pair early-2007 “contained subprime” narratives with early-2010 “euro debt still ring-fenceable” narratives as kin underestimation errors.
Why it matters: pulls literary “collecting” back into operable liquidity management.
Source: fixed-income risk primers; contagion case reviews; public trial-read de-risk beat (rewritten)

May 6 Flash Crash: a coffee-cup demon

From late April, euro debt owns the headlines: Greece seeks EU/IMF help, rushes deficit cuts (pay cuts, later retirement), and large protests erupt. Markets darken.

May 6, an ordinary Thursday. CNBC loops Greek riots; equities bleed red. After 2 p.m., the bond session is winding down, the floor quiet; Yuyang ducks out for coffee—walking back with the cup, noise detonates: in the time it took to brew, the Dow has gone from roughly −200 to −500; minutes later it approaches −1,000, then rockets back hundreds of points. Experts argue software glitches and fat-finger trades… His mind holds two characters: demon. ⁶

He recalls §11’s Kerviel episode: without a market already at the cliff, one scandal rarely lights a global equity fire. Likewise, the May 6 Flash Crash sits atop euro-debt anxiety already at a critical point. ⁷

Even as stocks rebound in following days, he cuts the trading book by about two-thirds without hesitation.

The Flash Crash does not teach second-guessing the next tick; it teaches position language at critical points.
Section notes
⁶ May 6, 2010 Flash Crash (public reports) Major U.S. equity indexes plunged and rebounded within roughly tens of minutes; the Dow’s intraday point drop approached the thousand-point scale. SEC/CFTC staff reports describe a morning already unsettled by European sovereign concerns, then a brief collapse in liquidity supply interacting with automated selling and HFT dynamics. The footnote anchors “backdrop + microstructure accelerator,” not any non-public blotter.
Why it matters: gives the coffee-cup scene a verifiable historical coordinate.
Source: SEC/CFTC Findings on May 6, 2010; Reuters same-day coverage
⁷ Kerviel and the “critical point” analogy (back to §11) In January 2008 Société Générale’s Jérôme Kerviel unauthorized-positions scandal jolted European equities; public narratives often stress that a single fraud hits harder when markets are already fragile. This chapter analogies the Flash Crash the same way—micro trigger × macro fragility. Details live in the §11 guide.
Why it matters: pulls the crash out of single-cause “computer glitch” talk into a fragility × trigger frame.
Source: SocGen 2008 public reporting; §11 guide continuity; FX110 analogy beat (rewritten)

De-risk, World Cup, temporary lid

Markets then clearly show international capital retreating: equities, Treasuries, commodities, AUD… needles point to de-leveraging and de-risking. June stays chaotic; only the South Africa World Cup offers brief amnesia. In a desk chat about favorites, someone drops a dark theory: a Spanish title would be best for the world economy—among PIGS Spain is the big one; a true debt crisis might exceed Germany’s rescue capacity; the government must tighten belts, and if citizens riot like Greece it gets harder; decades of waiting for the Cup might help people accept lean years. ⁸

Spain lifts the trophy; the euro debt crisis is temporarily suppressed in summer 2010 by euro-area states, the ECB, the IMF, and related force. A squall—or the foreshock of a larger quake?

A temporary lid ≠ a solved problem; it is only an intermission in the next unite/divide turn.
Section notes
⁸ 2010 South Africa World Cup and Spain’s title (public fact) The 2010 FIFA World Cup was hosted in South Africa; Spain won its first title. The book’s “title helps austerity politics” line is trading-floor black humor, not a causal econometric claim—the footnote verifies the tournament and champion only, and labels the joke.
Why it matters: keeps the chapter’s human texture without letting readers treat the gag as a macro model.
Source: FIFA 2010 public results; FX110 chat beat (rewritten)

Credit cards maxed: sovereign debt risk

The euro crisis makes plain: debt built by long Western borrow-and-spend is among the world’s largest safety risks. Especially the U.S.—how long can living on tomorrow’s grain last? Does AAA hide an unprecedented fiscal crack? Image: mortgages as households’ maxed cards, the banking crisis as Wall Street’s maxed cards, rescues as moving private debt onto the public tab; if one day the government’s card maxes too, then what? ⁹

Near term, America can still draw overseas capital via the dollar’s reserve status; medium term it must raise taxes and cut spending, accepting a temporary living-standard hit to ease the burden. If more borrowing is used as a higher drug dose for a short high, the debt problem will eventually detonate. 2010’s euro episode may be a rehearsal.

The next station of the cycle may not be bank balance sheets alone—it may be the sovereign itself.
Section notes
⁹ Private leverage → public leverage: post-crisis debt migration Public macro discussion often notes that 2008 responses shifted part of private losses and contingent liabilities onto government and central-bank balance sheets (rescues, guarantees, asset purchases). That is not a prophecy of U.S. default; it is a reminder that sovereign credit is not infinitely elastic—ratings and funding terms track fiscal paths. The chapter’s “credit card” image is teaching rhetoric; the footnote supplies the migration logic.
Why it matters: expands unite/divide from Street firms to sovereign credit cycles.
Source: post-crisis fiscal/central-bank balance-sheet discussions; sovereign-risk primers

Autumn 2010: Re-REMIC & retreat pontoon → epilogue

Blink, and it is autumn 2010; a year of writing is nearly done. Chaos’s chill seems far; boom’s glitter is within reach. Under multi-country easing, liquidity tides return: on Bloomberg, stocks and Treasuries rise together, oil and gold rise, AUD and EM FX rise, only the dollar (funding currency) falls as usual. Hong Kong auctions print insane prices; CNBC debates investing in aged wine; M&A and LBOs reappear; junk becomes scarce; structured products quietly revive—CDOs rebranded as Re-REMICs, same song under a new name… Is this 2010, or 2006? ¹⁰

Wall Street lives in the present: when markets are good, run the boom playbook—add risk, raise leverage, push the “collectible” frontier, dust off relative-value models. Profits roll in; unease stays: is the crisis truly over? Fundamentals unsolved, when the medicine wears off—deflation or inflation, euro relapse or a U.S. debt fuse—paths are hard to forecast.

But a surfer need not predict every wave, only borrow force and remember “when affairs change, prepare to change,” riding the beat. If he must forecast one thing with confidence: human greed and fear barely change, and markets barely escape the unite/divide rise-and-fall cycle. ¹¹

As 2010’s curtain falls, markets look like peacetime prosperity again. He can only go with the flow and add P&L—while, advancing, he keeps checking the retreat pontoon behind him, because he vaguely feels: chaos will return someday. Numbered chapters end here; next is the epilogue.

Union is liquidity’s union; division is fear’s division. Check the pontoon—then write the epilogue.
Section notes
¹⁰ Re-REMIC: structured products in new clothes A REMIC (Real Estate Mortgage Investment Conduit) is a U.S. mortgage-securitization vehicle; after the crisis, markets saw Re-REMIC and similar resecuritizations that re-tranche existing RMBS/CDO-like exposures to adjust ratings, capital, or investor taste. Public critics often treat the rename as a symbol of “sell it again”—the footnote explains the acronym, not any specific deal’s legality.
Why it matters: docks “CDO under a new name” to a searchable product family and strengthens the product face of the cycle.
Source: REMIC / securitization primers; post-crisis Re-REMIC market coverage; FX110 closing beat (rewritten)
¹¹ “When affairs change, prepare to change” and the epilogue bridge The classical tag (通识化用 from Legalist phrasing such as in Han Feizi) means: when conditions shift, shift strategy. Public trial reads close on that: paths cannot be precision-forecast; what can is human nature and cycles.
