          <em>Agency</em>: mostly match clients, earn commission or sales credit, limited inventory. <em>Market-making</em>: two-way quotes, earn spread, manage book risk. <em>Prop</em>: firm capital takes directional or relative-value risk for P&amp;L. Pre-crisis banks often ran all three; cheap funding plus weak flow pushed some toward prop. The Volcker Rule later crushed much bank prop — but 2006 Lucy still lived in the can-do-prop era.
          <div class="why">Why it matters: same job title, different daily physics on a Lucy muni prop desk versus a franchise flow shop.</div>
          <div class="src">Sources: eFinancialCareers prop-culture reporting; Volcker Rule contrast</div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <hr class="divider" />

      <h2 id="s8">Vince’s free-lunch stakeout &amp; IDBs</h2>
      <p>Vince assigned a “recon” job: stake out the free lunch — who shows, who grabs, what the vibe is. It sounded like a joke; it was observation school. Power and information on a floor often cling to the smallest rituals.</p>
      <p>He also taught inter-dealer brokers: dealers do not always trade face-to-face; IDBs match them anonymously or semi-anonymously, protecting intent and pooling liquidity<a class="fn-link" href="#fn9"><sup class="fn">⁹</sup></a>. In a fragmented muni market, broker channels are like a subway — invisible to tourists, daily rides for the desk.</p>

      <div class="footnotes" id="fn-block-8">
        <div class="fn-head">Section notes</div>
        <div class="fn-item" id="fn9">
          <strong>⁹ Inter-dealer brokers (IDBs)</strong>
          IDBs intermediate dealer-to-dealer trades by voice or screen, often with anonymity that lowers the cost of showing real intent. Treasuries, rate derivatives, and parts of credit lean hard on IDB ecosystems; munis have their own broker networks. For a rookie, learning IDBs is learning where prices are shouted from and where inventory can be unloaded.
          <div class="why">Why it matters: Vince extends “center of the universe” from the physical floor to the invisible broker web.</div>
          <div class="src">Sources: fixed-income market-structure primers; public IDB industry overviews</div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <hr class="divider" />

      <h2 id="s9">Roger, the Chrises &amp; Series 7/63</h2>
      <p>Roger taught the temperament of spot bonds: which names actually trade, which look cheap until you try to exit. On the sales side, a cluster of Chrises became an in-joke — shout “Chris” and half the sales row turns.</p>
      <p>Licensing hit too: Series 7 and 63-type exams; he landed around a comfortable 95% — then laughed that part of entering trading really does feel like standardized tests<a class="fn-link" href="#fn10"><sup class="fn">¹⁰</sup></a>. Passing papers ≠ managing risk; failing them means you cannot legally talk to clients.</p>

        <div class="fn-head">Section notes</div>
        <div class="fn-item" id="fn10">
          <strong>¹⁰ Series 7 and Series 63</strong>
          Series 7 (General Securities Representative) is a core FINRA qualification covering corporate securities, munis, options, governments, and more. Series 63 (Uniform Securities Agent State Law) focuses on state “blue sky” agent registration. Exams gate legal practice; they do not certify a stable P&amp;L — his ~95% joke lands exactly there: standardized ticket, then the real class.

      <h2 id="s10">Teahouse joke: amateur casino to Wall Street casino</h2>
      <p>The chapter’s self-mockery lands like a teahouse gag: leave the amateur casino, enter the Wall Street casino. Light tone, sharp tip — last chapter he insisted on a pilot’s seat; this chapter he has it, and discovers “center of the universe” and “bigger gaming table” can be two signs on the same room.</p>
      <p>The point is not cynicism but clarity: tools can hedge or gamble; a firm can be the universe’s center or a wet market. In the echo of Done!, a rookie has to learn which one he is actually doing.</p>
