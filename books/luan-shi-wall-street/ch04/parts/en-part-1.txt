      <h2 id="s1">April 2006: a seat of his own</h2>
      <p>In April 2006 he joined Lucy Bank’s New York investment-banking unit as a municipal-bond trader — not a visitor’s chair, but his own slot on the floor. Chapter 3’s “I DO” became furniture: screens, phones, risk limits, and colleagues’ eyes turned the pilot metaphor into a workday.</p>
      <p>It was still the late boom before the crisis: cheap leverage, tight credit spreads, structured products selling hard. The crack of 2008 was not yet audible in the floor noise<a class="fn-link" href="#fn1"><sup class="fn">¹</sup></a>. For a new hire the first lesson was not forecasting collapse; it was locating the desk on the map of the firm’s universe.</p>

      <h2 id="s2">Center of the universe — or a wet market?</h2>
      <p>Wall Streeters told themselves the trading floor was the center of the universe. Walk in as an outsider and you might see only rows of desks, blinking screens, and ringing phones — an air-conditioned wet market where the goods happen to be bonds and derivatives<a class="fn-link" href="#fn2"><sup class="fn">²</sup></a>.</p>
      <p>He also reached for a carrier flight-deck metaphor: chaos on the surface, invisible rules underneath — wind, tower, catapult. The floor sounded the same: noise to visitors, rhythm to people who already lived inside it.</p>

      <div class="scene">
        “Center of the universe” is identity myth; “wet market” is the outsider’s eye; “carrier deck” is his third frame — procedure inside the roar.
      </div>

Culture prizes speed, visible P&amp;L, and the nerve to quote out loud — while compliance and limits cage the “wet market.” By 2006 electronic chat already coexisted with the soundscape; the shout still mattered.

      <h2 id="s3">Liu Laolao: four screens, turret, squawk</h2>
      <p>He joked he was Liu Laolao entering the Grand View Garden: everything new. Four monitors showed markets, positions, news, and chat at once; a turret phone put counterparties one button away; a squawk box shared voice across the desk<a class="fn-link" href="#fn3"><sup class="fn">³</sup></a>.</p>
      <p>The gear was not magic; the speed was. Information did not arrive as a leisurely webpage — it hit four screens and a speaker at once. Half a beat late, and you only heard the wash after someone else traded.</p>

      <div class="footnotes" id="fn-block-3">
        <div class="fn-head">Section notes</div>
        <div class="fn-item" id="fn3">
          <strong>³ Turret phones and squawk boxes</strong>
          A turret is a multi-line trading phone: one-touch paths to brokers, sales, or internal desks, often with shared listen. A squawk box broadcasts voice across the pod for prices, fills, and risk alerts. Together they were (and partly remain) the acoustic infrastructure of a desk — one shared brain, not a row of silent PCs.
          <div class="why">Why it matters: turns the Liu Laolao gag into nameable hardware so readers hear why the floor sounds like a battlefield.</div>
