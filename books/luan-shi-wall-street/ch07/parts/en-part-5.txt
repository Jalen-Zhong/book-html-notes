      <p>At a late-2006 party the author ran into Parvin, a Berkeley classmate working on Lehman’s subprime-related desk. Shop talk slid into P&amp;L scale: the other department’s profits were spoken of near the billion-dollar mark, while the author’s municipal book still lived in the tens of millions. The feeling was blunt—peasant laboring at the landlord’s gate.</p>
      <p>Nothing in the room smelled like blood. Subprime still looked like a proven franchise: rising houses, easy refis, open securitization pipes. Under party lights, risk seemed to belong on someone else’s balance sheet.</p>

      <h2 id="s2">From prime success to subprime push</h2>
      <p>Rewind to earlier victories: prime mortgages, the GSEs, and MBS had turned American homeownership into something ratable, tradable, and exportable<a class="fn-link" href="#fn1"><sup class="fn">¹</sup></a><a class="fn-link" href="#fn2"><sup class="fn">²</sup></a>. Once the pipe worked, politics and markets both wanted a wider aperture—thinner credit files, lower incomes—and subprime moved from fringe to entrée.</p>
