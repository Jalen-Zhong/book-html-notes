CHAPTER SEVEN · THE INTERLOCKING STRATAGEM

Berkeley classmate, peasant vs landlord

At a late-2006 party the author ran into Parvin, a Berkeley classmate working on Lehman’s subprime-related desk. Shop talk slid into P&L scale: the other department’s profits were spoken of near the billion-dollar mark, while the author’s municipal book still lived in the tens of millions. The feeling was blunt—peasant laboring at the landlord’s gate.

Nothing in the room smelled like blood. Subprime still looked like a proven franchise: rising houses, easy refis, open securitization pipes. Under party lights, risk seemed to belong on someone else’s balance sheet.

Same campus pedigree, same Street—and a profit scale that felt like a class divide. The interlocking stratagem opens as status whiplash.

From prime success to subprime push

Rewind to earlier victories: prime mortgages, the GSEs, and MBS had turned American homeownership into something ratable, tradable, and exportable¹². Once the pipe worked, politics and markets both wanted a wider aperture—thinner credit files, lower incomes—and subprime moved from fringe to entrée.

Product design echoed hedge-fund leverage in retail clothing: teaser rates made the first payments look affordable; cash-out refinances turned price appreciation into spendable cash. For borrowers it was leveraged consumption and speculation; for Wall Street it was feedstock for the securitization machine.

Footnotes
¹ Subprime vs prime mortgages Prime loans typically go to borrowers with stronger credit, income, and documentation; subprime targets thinner credit, incomplete files, or heavier debt loads, usually at higher rates and fees. Mid-2000s structures—ARMs, teaser periods, second liens—amplified “get in now, refinance later” incentives. When house prices stalled, the refinance exit narrowed and defaults clustered.
Why it matters: shows how the same housing-finance machine slid from sturdy pipe to riskier feedstock.
Sources: public housing-finance histories; standard post-crisis definitions of subprime
² Fannie Mae / Freddie Mac (GSEs) The government-sponsored enterprises long bought or guaranteed conforming mortgages and issued agency MBS, supplying liquidity and standardization to U.S. housing finance. They are not the whole subprime story, but they are core infrastructure for “mortgages as wholesale securities.” Political pressure to widen homeownership often traveled through GSE goals alongside private conduits.
Why it matters: links successful housing securitization to the later private-label overreach.
Sources: public GSE explainers; crisis histories of housing-finance infrastructure

Tranching alchemy: AAA, mezz, equity

When feedstock was flawed, Wall Street’s answer was tranching³: assume pool defaults around a “manageable” number—public narratives often floated something like 5%—then slice senior, mezzanine, and equity. Senior paper was dressed as AAA for pensions and insurers who could only buy “safe”; equity and juicy mezz went to hedge funds and prop books; leftovers that would not clear stayed on investment-bank inventories.

The author likens the table to Wall Street baccarat: the house shuffles, guests pick seats, and sometimes the dealer must sit too. While house prices rose, loss assumptions kept looking “reasonable.”

Footnotes
³ Tranching and CDOs Tranching reallocates a pool’s cash flows by loss-absorption order: equity burns first, seniors last, reshaping heterogeneous risk into differently rated bonds. Collateralized debt obligations (CDOs) often remortgage MBS, loans, or other debt and tranche again. Ratings, models, and “historical default rates” propped up the AAA story for senior slices—until correlated defaults and downgrades arrived together.
Why it matters: technical core of the “interlocking stratagem”—risk is renamed and resold, not erased.
Sources: structured-credit primers; post-crisis public postmortems on tranching/CDOs

Interlocking ships: where is the east wind?

The chapter’s title image comes from the Romance of the Three Kingdoms interlocking-ships stratagem: chain the fleet for steadier decks—and turn a single fire into a systemic blaze. Subprime loans, MBS, CDOs, wrappers, and funding pipes locked together. While prices rose, everyone praised the calm; few urgently asked: when winter comes, where is the east wind?

Systemic risk and contagion were named, then waved away by the house-price trend. The tighter the chains, the harder for any one ship to flee alone.

In markets the “east wind” is not poetry: it is liquidity, refinance exits, and house prices turning at once.

Dose: useful securitization, excess chaining

The author reaches for a dose metaphor: securitization is medicine—useful in moderation to move credit risk to those who can bear it; toxic in excess when the same risk is repackaged again and again. CDOs-squared took already-sliced mezzanine and sliced it once more, farther from the original borrower, while models still printed “acceptable” default correlations⁴.

The chain was institutional as well as product: banks, raters, insurers, money funds, and cross-border buyers on the same deck. Global finance became mutually collateralized planking—admired as engineering until the screws proved shared.

Footnotes
⁴ CDOs-squared (CDO²) CDOs collateralized by other CDO tranches (often mezzanine). They lengthen the distance from end-investor to original mortgage and are hypersensitive to default-correlation assumptions: when underlying defaults become correlated, multiple “senior” stories can fail together. Crisis literature treats them as a specimen of structured-credit over-engineering.
Why it matters: separates useful securitization from excess interlocking—medicine versus overdose.
Sources: structured-credit crisis postmortems; standard definitions of CDO²

Christmas 2006: record bonuses, tiny price dip

Around Christmas 2006 the Street bathed in record-pay headlines: New York State Comptroller estimates put the Wall Street bonus pool near $23.9 billion; Goldman’s full-year compensation and benefits ran about $16.4 billion, roughly $622k per employee; CEO bonus stories refreshed the screens in turn. Fireworks drowned a nearly invisible house-price wiggle—the first tiny dip barely registered as news.

The Gilded Age’s favorite trick: translate “doesn’t hurt yet” into “will never hurt.”

A winter of record bonuses; house prices nod almost imperceptibly—few heard it as the east wind clearing its throat.

Early 2007 sparks: HSBC and New Century

Early 2007, smoke from the corners. HSBC raised impairment charges tied to its U.S. consumer/mortgage franchise (the Household / HSBC Finance lineage), putting subprime on a global bank’s P&L in plain sight⁵. Then independent subprime lender New Century hit fraud probes and a funding squeeze, filing for bankruptcy in April 2007⁶.

Trading-floor complacency sounded like 1998 talk about Russia: a “small market.” If your book did not literally say subprime, the alarm could be filed under next door’s smoke.

Footnotes
⁵ HSBC / Household provisioning alert In February 2007 HSBC issued a trading update on U.S. Mortgage Services, warning loan-impairment charges would run well above consensus, driven by deteriorating subprime and related ARM/second-lien books. Household International, bought in 2003, sat inside that U.S. consumer-finance footprint. Retrospectives often treat the alert as an early public signal that subprime had entered large-bank earnings.
Why it matters: nails party-chat “subprime” to a global bank’s disclosure.
Sources: HSBC 8 Feb 2007 trading update; contemporary Reuters coverage
⁶ New Century Financial Once among the largest independent U.S. subprime originators. Early 2007 brought rising delinquencies, repurchase pressure, and fraud/accounting scrutiny; after warehouse lines tightened it filed for Chapter 11 on 2 April 2007, cut most lending, and became a highly visible break in the originate–securitize–repurchase chain—still easy for core Street desks to dismiss as peripheral.
Why it matters: originators fell first—mine-mouth smoke still labeled a “small market.”
Sources: contemporary Reuters reports; bankruptcy examiner / BIS case studies

Liquidity rotates: energy, EM, mega-LBOs

Money did not vanish; it changed lanes—energy, emerging markets, and bolder leveraged buyouts⁷. Blackstone’s Equity Office takeout, KKR’s TXU-scale ambitions, bridge loans and “Triple Play”–style package financing let deals sprint before permanent capital arrived. China’s forming sovereign investor (later CIC) took roughly $3 billion of non-voting Blackstone IPO-related units—global spare cash with a proper noun⁸.

Irony shared the season: Sam Zell sold Equity Office near the CRE peak to Blackstone, then levered into Tribune—selling peak bricks, buying newspaper futures. In boom time, stories outran risk models.

Footnotes
⁷ LBOs and bridge loans Leveraged buyouts finance acquisitions largely with debt, counting on cash flows and refinancing. Bridge loans are short-term underwriter financing that close a deal before high-yield bonds, loan syndications, or other takeouts land; in 2006–07 mega-PE deals, bridges and packaged commitments (market slang often bundled ideas like a “Triple Play”) parked underwriting risk on Street balance sheets. When bridges fail to take out, inventory and losses surface.
Why it matters: liquidity rotated rather than evaporated—into larger leverage games.
Sources: contemporary coverage of EOP/TXU-scale LBOs; primers on acquisition bridge financing
⁸ Blackstone and the CIC precursor stake In May 2007 China’s forming state FX investment vehicle (later China Investment Corporation) agreed to invest about $3 billion for non-voting Blackstone common units tied to the IPO, keeping the stake under 10% with a multi-year hold. It became a landmark sovereign entry into a U.S. alternative-asset manager’s equity—and sat on the same liquidity map as Blackstone’s real-estate/PE expansion.
Why it matters: turns “too much global money” into a named ticket.
Sources: Blackstone press release; CRS and other public CIC early-investment summaries

Collections in boom, gold in chaos

Desk orders still said grow. The author reaches for a near-proverb: in prosperous times people collect art; in chaotic times they buy gold—chase stories in boom, cling to what stores value and clears in stress. His own moves were concrete: trading muni housing-related bonds, hunting price in the details.

Bear salesman Hu You’s Ohio bond anecdote was small—thin profit, hard to resell. A fallen leaf: not a storm, but a wind direction. Warnings need not roar; sometimes they are just a few basis points of lost liquidity.

A leaf is not a tornado; when leaves drift one way, people in the tree should ask where the wind begins.

Novel floaters: models price risk in bps

Expansion kept inventing product: novel floating-rate structures, Merrill-linked muni CDOs. Models stayed gentle—showing “risk” that looked tiny against a few extra basis points. The author’s choice was distance: not a precise crash calendar, just ugly odds.

Meanwhile government-linked issuers paired floaters with swaps until balance-sheet management looked hedge-fundized. Borrowing was no longer only for bridges and schools; it was also curve and basis. Economy-wide leverage seeped from Street desks into public issuers’ toolkits.

First ship on fire: Bear’s related funds

In June 2007, Bear Stearns–related high-grade structured-credit hedge funds hit the wall: subprime-linked holdings, leverage, and margin calls shredded the “high grade” story⁹. The parent pledged billions to slow a fire sale—on the interlocking fleet, one ship finally burned in public.

Chapter 7’s stratagem-within-stratagem lands cold: tranching does not cancel risk; it only reorders who burns first. When housing, funding, and correlation turn together, chained ships light together.

Footnotes
⁹ Bear Stearns related hedge-fund crisis (June 2007) The Bear Stearns High-Grade Structured Credit Strategies Fund and its Enhanced Leverage sister held large CDO/mortgage structured-credit books funded partly via repo. In June 2007, falling subprime-related prices and creditor margin calls pushed the funds toward collapse; Bear ultimately pledged up to about $3.2 billion to rescue one fund while the other faced deeper liquidation. Histories treat this as a hinge from “originators/small market” stress into core Street funding and fund structures.
Why it matters: chapter landing—the interlocking fleet’s first open fire.
Sources: NYT 23 Jun 2007 and peers; standard crisis narratives of the Bear funds
