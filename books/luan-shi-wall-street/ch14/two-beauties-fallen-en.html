CHAPTER FOURTEEN · Two Beauties in Distress

Little Beauty falls: the IndyMac run

The false spring never really warmed the real economy. In late June 2008, California’s IndyMac Bank seized the headlines: a savings-and-loan that took deposits on one side and originated mostly housing loans on the other, living on the spread. Its specialty was neither pure subprime nor pristine prime, but the middle band — Alt-A mortgages.¹

As Alt-A delinquencies rose with the crisis, news of hole-ridden books triggered the thing bankers fear most: a deposit run. IndyMac could not hold; federal regulators took it over. For trader Yuyang, the signal cut sharper than any index: the subprime “cancer” had metastasized into other mortgage flavors. When “Little Beauty” fell, the tape turned to the two bigger names that also carry “Mae/Mac” — Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac.

Who are the Two Beauties: GSEs and the American Dream

Homeownership sits near the core of the American Dream. For decades U.S. policy preferred financial plumbing over mass public housing: make mortgages easier and cheaper, and more households climb onto the ownership ladder. The government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs) Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were built for that job — buying conforming loans from banks or guaranteeing them, recycling cash so originators could lend again.²

Versus ordinary banks they held a decisive edge: markets believed in an implicit U.S. government backstop, so their funding often priced inside even top private credits. Part of the savings flowed into lower mortgage rates; part became shareholder profit. In booms the design looked like multi-win policy. In busts it exposed a structural conflict: gains private, systemic losses ultimately socialized.

Semi-public credit, fully private shareholders — the Two Beauties were born with an institutional split personality.
Section notes
² GSEs: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac The Federal National Mortgage Association (Fannie Mae, founded 1938, later partly privatized) and the Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation (Freddie Mac, 1970) are the core U.S. housing GSEs. They mainly buy or guarantee conforming mortgages and issue or guarantee mortgage-backed securities (MBS), supplying liquidity and standardization — not primarily lending door-to-door. Chinese market slang calls them “两房”; the book’s “Two Beauties” plays on Mae/Mac naming.
Why it matters: without the buy–guarantee–fund pipe, conservatorship is only a headline.
Source: FHFA / Treasury primers; CRS GSE FAQs

Agency debt and agency MBS: the implicit guarantee

On a bond desk, GSE paper splits into two main stacks. First, agency debt issued by the enterprises themselves — funding operations and portfolio carry, on the order of roughly $1.6 trillion before the climax. Second, agency MBS guaranteed by the GSEs — cash flows from borrowers, with the GSE standing behind timely pay, on the order of roughly $5 trillion.³

Both stacks were treated by global institutions (including foreign central banks) as quasi-sovereign. The more the implicit guarantee was trusted, the tighter the spreads and the higher the tolerable leverage; when trust slipped, equity issuance and debt costs failed together — oxygen cut for a spread-and-leverage machine.⁴

Section notes
³ Agency debt vs agency MBS Agency debt is GSE own funding; agency MBS are mortgage-backed securities with GSE guarantees of timely payment. Pre-conservatorship briefings often paired “~$1.6T debt + ~$5T guarantee/related exposure” to stress systemic scale far beyond a typical dealer balance sheet. Exact totals vary by perimeter; the order of magnitude is stable.
Why it matters: Yuyang’s desk instinct — the GSEs were not two tickers; they were fixed-income nodes with quasi-sovereign status.
Source: Treasury / FHFA 2008 statements; crisis chronologies
⁴ Implicit guarantee and hybrid ownership Legally shareholder-owned; in practice priced as too-big-to-fail. Critics called it privatized gains, socialized risk. Paulson’s July plan and HERA pushed the “implicit” toward executable liquidity and capital authorities — scaffolding for September conservatorship.
Why it matters: explains why equity could die while debt still had to be saved — creditors were underwriting the state, not management skill.
Source: Treasury HP-1079 (13 Jul 2008); policy reviews of GSE hybrid structure

Sixty-times leverage: spoiled quasi-Treasuries

The GSE earnings engine matched banking’s skeleton: spread + leverage. Ordinary large banks walk a tightrope between leverage and ratings. The GSEs, wrapped in quasi-sovereign glow, long enjoyed top-tier ratings and thinner capital constraints. Public figures cited in the chapter’s era put year-end 2007 capital near the $80+ billion range against multi-trillion owned-and-guaranteed books — leverage on the order of ~60×, a scale that dwarfs many dealer and LTCM lore comparisons.⁵

At the desk Yuyang also watched “label arbitrage”: structured notes that might price at 98 under a dealer name could print nearer 100 with a Freddie-like wrapper; the extra points were split — while residual credit risk leaned on the taxpayer’s implicit backstop. Policy indulgence meant to serve housing also invited using special status beyond core mortgage plumbing.

When someone else stamps your paper AAA, the tightrope looks like a sidewalk — until the stamp itself starts to blur.
Section notes
⁵ Capital, leverage, OFHEO/FHFA Pre-crisis oversight sat with OFHEO; HERA replaced and strengthened it as FHFA. “Thin capital / high leverage” dominated summer-2008 stress narratives: further house-price declines would let credit losses devour slim equity. OFHEO still called the firms “adequately capitalized” under its rules even as markets priced distress — that gap itself fed the run on confidence.
Why it matters: separate “adequate on the regulatory spreadsheet” from “will the market keep rolling your debt?”
Source: FHFA Lockhart statements/testimony; FCIC chapters; OFHEO mid-2008 remarks

Chocolate melts: stocks, spreads, and short sellers

The GSEs were not sealed off from non-prime pain; subprime losses had already hit, and 2008’s recession pushed prime delinquencies higher via unemployment. At ~60× leverage, even roughly 1.5% deep credit loss on a multi-trillion book can erase equity — investors who did that arithmetic sold.⁶

In early July 2008 shares collapsed day after day; analyst notes on capital and accounting, plus IndyMac’s failure, shut the equity window while agency spreads widened. Hedge-fund shorts framed a binary: bankruptcy to zero, or a Bear-like rescue that spares creditors and wipes shareholders. Official verbal support bought less and less.

The implicit guarantee was chocolate in high sun — still shaped like a bar until someone squeezed.
Section notes
⁶ July 2008 equity collapse and capital panic Around 7–11 July, Fannie and Freddie common shares posted large double-digit daily drops from already depressed levels. Triggers included worsening housing, fear of huge capital raises, analyst discussion of accounting/capital rules, and press reports of contingency planning. OFHEO and Paulson’s “adequately capitalized” messaging did not stop the selling.
Why it matters: equity markets passed a funding death sentence first — the bazooka and conservatorship answered that verdict in law.
Source: Washington Post / NPR July 2008 coverage; Treasury contemporaneous remarks

July three axes: Treasury, Fed, SEC

Letting the GSEs fail threatened three destruction paths at once: a mortgage-market seizure hitting housing and growth; U.S. banks marked to death on agency holdings; foreign official investors pulling from dollar assets after losses. Around 13 July, federal agencies swung a combo.⁷

Treasury (Paulson): temporarily expand GSE credit lines and seek authority to buy equity if needed — later nicknamed the “bazooka”: if it is big enough, you may never fire it. Fed: authorize the New York Fed to lend to the GSEs as needed. SEC: temporary curbs on naked short sales in selected financials to blunt spiral dumps.⁸ By month-end Congress passed and the President signed HERA, creating a stronger regulator (FHFA) and writing Treasury support powers into statute.

The three axes bought time for a heart that had seized — chronic bleeding would still need a different knife.
Section notes
⁷ Paulson’s 13 Jul 2008 “bazooka” Treasury release HP-1079 proposed three parts: a temporary larger credit line; temporary equity-purchase authority; a Fed consultative role on prudential standards under a reformed regulator. Paulson’s later gloss: a big enough bazooka might never need firing — markets still demanded credible legal authority. The Fed same day said the New York Fed could lend to the GSEs.
Why it matters: the turn from verbal implicit support to executable statutory tools — without it, September conservatorship lacks a toolbox.
Source: U.S. Treasury HP-1079; Federal Reserve 13 Jul 2008 release
⁸ HERA, FHFA, and naked-short limits The Housing and Economic Recovery Act (signed 30 Jul 2008) created FHFA with stronger conservatorship/receivership powers and authorized time-limited Treasury support for the GSEs. The SEC imposed temporary naked-short restrictions on Fannie, Freddie, and other financials in mid/late July (later extended/adjusted). Debt-market calm improved; losses and capital drain did not stop.
Why it matters: legal weapons ≠ crisis over; the book’s line is “infarct treated, still bleeding.”
Source: FHFA conservatorship history; SEC 2008 short-sale orders; Public Law 110-289

September conservatorship: rescue liquidity or insolvency?

July’s medicine steadied the liquidity story without curing house prices or credit losses. Equity markets were effectively shut; the GSEs could not “re-blood” themselves. On 6–7 September 2008, FHFA placed Fannie and Freddie into conservatorship with board consent; Treasury signed preferred-stock purchase agreements committing on the order of $100 billion per enterprise (often summarized in media as ~$200 billion combined), replaced management, and eliminated common and existing preferred dividends.⁹

Creditors (including overseas and Chinese institutions) exhaled: debt and agency MBS credit was lifted toward sovereign standing. Legacy common and preferred holders were nearly wiped — shares that once traded in the tens fell toward spare change, validating the shorts’ second script. Yuyang borrows a line from the film To Live to translate policy philosophy: help the urgent, not the chronically poor. Bear looked more like liquidity urgency; the GSEs, at ~60× with deep credit holes, looked more like insolvency. Governments “shouldn’t” rescue insolvency — yet systemic size forced an expensive exception, igniting the political storm that taxpayers were saving Wall Street and foreign investors.¹⁰

Equity near zero, creditors walking away whole — if a trillion-scale hole never vanished, it only changed seat cards.
Section notes
⁹ 6–7 Sep 2008 conservatorship and SPSPAs FHFA Director James Lockhart announced conservatorship with board consent; Treasury’s Senior Preferred Stock Purchase Agreements (SPSPAs) initially capped support at $100B per GSE (later raised), with dividend rights and warrants for ~79.9% of common. Aims: keep the mortgage pipe open, protect holders of GSE debt/MBS at home and abroad, and put losses on legacy equity. Conservatorship continues; exit remains a live policy debate.
Why it matters: chapter climax — “Two Beauties in distress” becomes institutional fact and loads political pressure into §15’s red line.
Source: FHFA 7 Sep 2008 statement; Treasury preferred-stock fact sheets; CRS R44525
¹⁰ “Urgent vs poor” and the systemic exception Textbook intervention distinguishes illiquidity (solvent but funding-broken) from insolvency. Bear is often narrated as the former; the GSEs, thin-capital and credit-lossy, closer to the latter. The film allusion is emotional translation: rules say don’t save the chronically insolvent; reality said you must — that tear pushes Paulson toward drawing a public red line.
Why it matters: hinge from “save institutions” toward “selective refusal” in the book’s moral-political arc.
Source: public crisis narratives and rewrite guide; Fed/Treasury contemporaneous speeches

A red line in the sand: foreshadowing

Rescuing the GSEs carried a brutal political price. Facing Congress and the press, Paulson needed to show the world that free markets still bite — that the United States would not write a blank check for every Wall Street villain. The chapter closes on a warning: he must draw a clear red line in the sand. The chance would arrive soon; the independent-dealer story was unfinished, and Lehman Brothers was getting hotter on every screen.

For the desk, conservatorship temporarily floored agency debt and MBS — and shoved the harder question downstairs: if creditors always walk, who finally recognizes the hole? The false spring was over; the main quake of the financial tsunami was still ahead.

The Two Beauties’ fall is not the finale — only the expensive “yes” the government had to say before it learned how to say “no.”
