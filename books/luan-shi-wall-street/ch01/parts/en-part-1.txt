      <h2 id="s1">The road to Wall Street begins in a casino</h2>
      <p>Looking back, the author finds an unglamorous starting point: years earlier he was still sitting in American casinos, counting red and green chips for pocket money; years later he was running trading books in the hundreds of millions and generating tens of millions in profit a year for a bank. The span sounds huge; to him it did not feel that far — both sides were gambling, only the stakes had changed<a class="fn-link" href="#fn1"><sup class="fn">¹</sup></a>.</p>
      <p>He jokes about a gambling gene — a great-grandfather who loved to gamble, another who ran a mahjong house. Growing up in New China, that impulse was suppressed for years, which somehow fed a passion for math and a place at Peking University. After four years he had mainly learned one thing: real mathematics was deep; what he was good at was arithmetic. He joined the wave of students going abroad, switched to computer science, aimed at Silicon Valley — and somehow landed in an investment bank’s IT group instead.</p>
      <div class="footnotes" id="fn-block-1">
        <div class="fn-head">Section notes</div>
        <div class="fn-item" id="fn1">
          <strong>¹ From the felt to the trading desk: the same mental model</strong>
          Edward Thorp first showed in <em>Beat the Dealer</em> that card counting can beat blackjack, then carried the same probabilistic thinking into convertible bonds and quantitative trading. Treating the casino as “Wall Street prep” is a personal version of that historical thread: practice expected value and position size in repeatable small games, then migrate the same language to markets.
          <div class="why">Why it matters: “Both sides are gambling” is not cynicism — it seeds later hedge-fund talk of expected value, volatility, and leverage.</div>

      <h2 id="s2">On Wall Street — but not at the table</h2>
      <p>At first he barely knew what an investment bank did — only that living next to markets sounded cool. Once inside, he learned he was in the “back office”<a class="fn-link" href="#fn2"><sup class="fn">²</sup></a>. Front office raises capital and places real bets; back office keeps the machine running. Compensation felt like wartime: the fighters get the glory and the bigger bonus pool; support gets the leftover care packages.</p>
      <p>After a while he wanted the front. Money was one reason; more than that, he wanted the feeling of surfing the market and putting his arithmetic to the edge. Moving from back to front is notoriously hard. A common path is the “second pass”: a brand-name business school, then re-enter for a revenue seat. He had wanted that route early, but a green-card wait dragged on for years. He did not sit idle — he went to the casino and gave himself a course in Wall Street prep.</p>
