      <h2 id="s3">Las Vegas: a week of blackjack “street sweeps”</h2>
      <p>American casinos looked to him like Ali Baba’s cave: chips sliding, slots ringing, money seemingly everywhere. Most games, though, are “play long enough and you lose.” He first mastered one rule: never bet without an edge.</p>
      <p>The crack in the house wall was blackjack<a class="fn-link" href="#fn3"><sup class="fn">³</sup></a>. Skilled counters can beat the casino; many Wall Street elders had done it young. House edge is already small; with basic strategy it is roughly half a percent, and it drifts with the mix of remaining high and low cards. The secret is simple and hard: bet small when the house has the edge, bet big when the player does.</p>
      <figure class="figure">
        <img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/DelLagoCasinoBlackjackTable.jpg" alt="Casino blackjack table" loading="lazy" />
        <figcaption>A casino blackjack table. Counters track remaining-card composition to adjust bets and plays. Source: Wikimedia Commons (DanielPenfield, CC BY-SA 4.0)</figcaption>
      </figure>
      <p>A chance pamphlet on beating blackjack sent him into late-night study. Feeling “ready,” he raided Las Vegas. It was a glorious week: sweeping casino after casino, flying back to New York with hundred-dollar bills stuffed in both pockets. For a moment he thought blackjack could be an ATM — later he understood Lady Luck had merely smiled once.</p>
      <div class="scene">
        Afterward he could still win on average, but the ride was violent; no more win-every-session streak. Mathematically he saw it: risk was huge relative to reward. To make about twenty dollars an hour on average, you might endure swings of roughly a thousand — in market language, a terrible Sharpe ratio<a class="fn-link" href="#fn4"><sup class="fn">⁴</sup></a>. Bet small and you never get rich; bet big and you can go broke. Blackjack became a game that “couldn’t make real money.” Inside those twists already sit the three hedge-fund keywords of later chapters: risk, return, leverage.
      </div>
      <p>When casinos changed the rules, counting blackjack almost died. Texas Hold’em was rising across America, so he switched tables.</p>

      <h2 id="s4">Texas Hold’em: outthinking other people</h2>
      <p>Compared with blackjack’s mechanical tactics, Texas Hold’em is more like chess against other minds<a class="fn-link" href="#fn5"><sup class="fn">⁵</sup></a>. You do not need the best cards — only worse opponents. You cannot stare only at your own hand; you must guess theirs. You cannot always bluff or always play honest — roughly seven parts substance, three parts bluff. Beginners bet their cards; advanced players bet the range and psychology of the opponent.</p>
      <figure class="figure">
        <img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Two_poker_cards_and_poker_chips_20170611.jpg" alt="Poker cards and chips" loading="lazy" />
        <figcaption>Cards and chips — visual shorthand for the mid-2000s online poker boom. Source: Wikimedia Commons (Santeri Viinamäki, CC BY-SA 4.0)</figcaption>
      </figure>

Next came single-table tournaments: ten players, fixed chips, bust and leave, top three paid. That is math.
