      <p>After that history, the professor had little appetite for teaching mid-career “speculators.” Lectures often circled old grievances or aged notes whose freshest examples stopped in 1987. Students skipped; he answered with pop quizzes. Two terms passed in that tug-of-war. Before the final class he kindly warned: there <em>would</em> be a quiz next time. Bleary classmates arrived on time. No quiz. When they accused him of breaking his word, he smiled and delivered the most useful line of both semesters:</p>
      <div class="scene">
        “The last promise, is the only one you can break.”
      </div>
      <p>It hit hard: on the bright side, business needs trust — break it once and people walk; on the dark side, someone who spends years building credibility for a final grab… he had heard Chinese merchants burn that way too. Worth remembering.</p>

      <h2 id="s7">A cold start — and a headhunter’s ice water</h2>
      <p>Most classmates came to Berkeley to land jobs. The most beloved figure was Debbie, who ran financial-engineering placement: tireless, treating employment as priority one, even carving three internship months into a one-year calendar. She networked hard; students trusted she could package them into Wall Street.</p>
      <p>One day she invited a senior headhunter who had traded at Salomon Brothers<a class="fn-link" href="#fn9"><sup class="fn">⁹</sup></a> — catchphrase: “when I was at Salomon…” After the talk, classmates lined up for résumé reviews. The author’s turn: zero relevant finance experience, big talk about becoming a trader. She left one line — it would take many years before anyone let him touch money. The tone felt like old privilege dismissing an upstart.</p>
      <p>It proved true: seven or eight banks and funds recruited on campus; none invited him to interview. Discouraged, he complained in Debbie’s office and begged for any placement. She was more confident than he was, eyes bright: opportunities come — when one does, grab it.</p>

      <h2 id="s8">Goldman arrives: open-net interviews</h2>
      <p>The first campus interview invite came from — of all places — Goldman Sachs<a class="fn-link" href="#fn10"><sup class="fn">¹⁰</sup></a>. He thought it was a mistake. Why would the firm care about someone with no experience? Debbie explained: two people from Goldman strategies would interview nearly thirty classmates — almost a dragnet. Only later did he grasp Goldman’s approach: cast a wide net. Give a chance to anyone with a spark; whether an offer follows is another question.</p>
      <p>He went last that day. Interviewer Keen, a Harvard grad, had already grilled people for seven or eight hours and looked exhausted. Half an hour of math, stats, finance, coding, and puzzles. Finally a thin smile: you were the only one who got every question right — wait for the phone round.</p>
