hourly rate, and harvested before the crowd leveled up.</p>
      <p>He also trained himself to read people: recreational players with strong hands often play too tight, terrified of letting a cooked duck fly; when losing, they press bets trying to get even, ignoring stops. He forced the opposite: never risk what he could not afford to lose; leave at a loss limit; judge process over single outcomes; trust skill, probability, and risk control — not luck.</p>
      <p>After enough gambling — and enough biographies of star traders and fund managers — he concluded: people who can truly beat casinos should usually walk away. The same brain, applied to a real profession, usually builds more. The highest form of gambling is not gambling. His green card finally arrived; he washed his hands and applied to business school.</p>

      <h2 id="s5">Berkeley: a one-year financial engineering master’s</h2>
      <p>Those years, mathematical models were fashionable in finance; “financial engineering” became the crossroads of finance, math, and computing. UC Berkeley’s program billed itself as the nation’s best, a master’s in one year — ideal for career switchers<a class="fn-link" href="#fn6"><sup class="fn">⁶</sup></a>. It was his first choice.</p>
      <p>The degree sat in the business school, so the GMAT mattered<a class="fn-link" href="#fn7"><sup class="fn">⁷</sup></a>. Standardized tests suited him: one option is right; combine context with the examiner’s psychology — easier than Hold’em. After the last question, the screen showed 790. Stacked with CFA Level III already passed, he felt Berkeley was nearly certain.</p>

The business school was the suit-and-tie enclave — future mainstream professionals rushing past with notebooks and buzzwords. He studied hard between classrooms and the library.</p>

      <h2 id="s6">Professor Rubinstein — and the last promise</h2>
      <p>Finance opened by putting star professor Mark Rubinstein on “financial products.” Young, he had helped popularize binomial option pricing; without a mid-career commercial stumble, a Nobel conversation might have been closer. In the mid-1980s Wall Street raced through innovation. Rubinstein and two other professors founded a firm and sold “portfolio insurance” rooted in option theory<a class="fn-link" href="#fn8"><sup class="fn">⁸</sup></a>: use index futures to synthesize a protective put — ride bulls without missing them, cap losses in bears. The strategy boomed. Then came October 1987’s Black Monday: the Dow fell about 22% in a day; believers dumped futures into the slide and amplified the crash. Congressional scrutiny followed.
