CHAPTER TWELVE · Warlords of Chaos

Three empires: Citi, JPMorgan, BofA

Writing as a bond trader, author Yuyang looks back at late 2007 through spring 2008: Wall Street’s lights still blazed, but survival was decided on three giant commercial-bank balance sheets — Citigroup, JPMorgan Chase, and Bank of America.¹ Broker-dealers could lose repo counterparties overnight; these three were the warlords of the age — holders of deposit franchises, clearing rails, and central-bank access, and also of toxic exposures large enough to wobble half the market.

If the previous chapter, “Achilles’ Heel,” was about addiction to short-term funding, this one asks: when the heel tears, who can still stand, who consolidates in the smoke, and who is already bleeding inside.

The Big Three were not a beauty contest of market caps — they were the shortlist of who could still refinance, and who might be asked to “collect the body.”
Footnotes
¹ U.S. banking “Big Three” context (2007–08) On the eve of the crisis, Citigroup, JPMorgan Chase, and Bank of America were routinely treated as the first tier of U.S. commercial banking by size and systemic importance, while also deep in wholesale funding, securitization, and investment-banking lines. Pure investment banks leaned harder on overnight repo and customer balances; commercial banks had deposits and the discount window in theory — yet housing-related on- and off-balance-sheet exposures could erase that comfort.
Why it matters: the narrative shifts from products and short funding to faces and fiefdoms — systemic risk ends up owned by a few people and a few statements.
Sources: standard crisis histories of large-bank roles; public filings and regulatory literature

Weill and Dimon: partnership and rupture

Rewind a decade-plus. Sandy Weill and Jamie Dimon built side by side: from Commercial Credit through Primerica and Travelers, then Salomon, culminating in the 1998 Travelers–Citicorp combination that created Citigroup — a “financial supermarket” cheered by the era’s deregulatory mood.²

Dimon was the heir apparent. Then, just as the merger landed, the court shook: clashes involving figures such as Deryck Maughan, culture collision, and Weill’s grip on the empire tangled into one knot. In early November 1998, Citigroup announced a reorganization and Dimon resigned as president — master and apprentice split, and Wall Street gasped.³

Dimon did not vanish. He later ran Bank One; when JPMorgan Chase merged with Bank One in 2004 he became president and COO, and on 1 January 2006 he became JPMorgan Chase CEO as agreed.⁴ The expelled heir became lord of another fief.

Partnership can build an empire; the day the empire is finished, succession is often more dangerous than the balance sheet.
Footnotes
² The Weill–Dimon axis and Citigroup’s birth (to 1998) Public biographies and contemporary reporting describe Dimon from the 1980s as Weill’s chief lieutenant through serial acquisitions, ending in the 1998 Travelers–Citicorp merger that formed Citigroup — emblem of one-stop financial conglomeration as Glass–Steagall norms eroded.
Why it matters: without the partnership there is no “betrayal” arc, and no later story of Dimon rebuilding power as an outsider at JPMorgan.
Sources: public biography summaries (e.g. Duff McDonald); Washington Post 1998 merger coverage
³ November 1998: Dimon pushed out of Citigroup Around 1–2 November 1998, Citigroup announced a major reorganization and Dimon’s resignation as president. Reporting cited conflict with co-management (including Deryck Maughan), Travelers–Citi culture clash, and an architecture set by Weill and John Reed that sidelined Dimon from the combined corporate/investment bank. Many had assumed he would be the next emperor — which made the exit theatrical.
Why it matters: the warlord arc begins here — the man expelled from the old empire later watches it bleed from another deck.
Sources: Washington Post 1998-11-02/03; Fortune/CNN Money retrospectives on the split
⁴ Bank One → JPMorgan Chase: Dimon’s ascent Dimon became Bank One chairman and CEO in 2000; the JPMorgan Chase–Bank One merger was announced 14 January 2004 and closed 1 July 2004, with Dimon as president and COO and William Harrison as chairman and CEO, Dimon to succeed as CEO in 2006 (firm biography: CEO from 1 January 2006; chairman the following year).
Why it matters: dates when the warlord truly held the army — without the 2006 CEO seat there is no 2008 mandate to buy Bear.
Sources: JPMorgan Chase leadership biography; 2004-01-14 merger press release

Prince inherits: subprime and resignation

After Weill stepped back from day-to-day control, lawyer-turned-executive Charles “Chuck” Prince became Citi’s chief. The empire was vast; risk culture lagged. While housing securities and CDOs still looked like spread machines, Citi stacked enormous exposures across conduits, the banking book, and the trading book.

Autumn 2007 brought writedowns and collapsing earnings. After a brutal quarter in mid-October, an emergency board weekend produced the 4 November announcement: Prince would leave as chairman and CEO, and Citi warned of roughly $8–11 billion further fair-value reductions tied to subprime. Robert Rubin became chairman; Win Bischoff acting CEO.⁵ Prince said that given the size of mortgage-securities losses, stepping down was “the only honorable course.”

In the author’s telling, Citi was a gold-lettered hull taking on water — while the man once cast out stood on a relatively drier deck.

When the “keep dancing” era ends, the first to fall are often those wearing the largest shoes on the floor.
Footnotes
⁵ Charles Prince resigns (4 Nov 2007) Citigroup’s 4 November 2007 release: Prince retires as chairman and CEO; Rubin becomes chairman; Bischoff acting CEO. The firm also disclosed a possible $8–11 billion reduction in fair value of subprime-related exposures and created a dedicated unit to manage them. Mid-October results had already shown a sharp profit drop on writedowns.
Why it matters: pins Citi’s internal wound to a verifiable public day — the first great fief’s crack becomes visible to the world.
Sources: Citigroup 2007-11-04 press release / SEC exhibit; Reuters, CNN, BBC same-day coverage

BofA swallows Countrywide: indigestion

Another warlord was hardly comfortable. Under CEO Ken Lewis, Bank of America favored acquisition-led growth. In January 2008 it announced an all-stock purchase of Countrywide Financial — one of the nation’s largest mortgage machines — for about $4 billion, billed as a once-in-a-lifetime mortgage-platform opportunity.⁶

To the author the meaning was plain: BofA was hauling a feverish housing beast into its own camp. Short-term it looked like a bargain; medium-term it was a pit of capital, litigation, and reputation. Among the three, Citi was bleeding, BofA was force-feeding itself, and JPMorgan looked comparatively restrained — Dimon, in the public narrative, counting bullets and waiting for terms.

In chaotic times the dearest cost is not the asset — it is the second you believe you bought it cheap.
Footnotes
⁶ BofA acquires Countrywide (announced Jan 2008) On 11 January 2008, Bank of America announced an all-stock deal to buy Countrywide Financial for about $4 billion. Lewis called it a rare chance to deepen the consumer mortgage franchise; closing followed later in 2008. Ex-post losses, settlements, and stigma dwarfed the headline price — a classic crisis “bargain that swallowed the buyer” tale.
Why it matters: contrasts with JPMorgan’s cold watch — three strategies side by side: expand, stanch, or wait.
Sources: Bank of America 2008-01-11 release / SEC exhibit; Reuters; later crisis retrospectives

Warlord of chaos: Dimon watches coldly

By early 2008 JPMorgan Chase looked relatively healthy: capital, liquidity, and a public risk-management image made it one of the few banks regulators could still imagine as a buyer. Dimon had survived Citi’s palace politics and a Midwestern bank rebuild; now he stood like a warlord on high ground — not ignorant of danger, but waiting for price and terms.

The author reaches for Chinese chaos-era imagery: Dimon as a Cao Cao figure — able to fight, able to count, able to say “I’ll take it” when others break. The analogy is not a moral verdict; it is a grammar of power. Chaotic times need someone who can absorb systemic risk — and that someone will demand discount, protection, and a place in the chronicle.

A cold eye is not a cold heart; a cold eye converts other people’s panic into one’s own purchase terms.

Bear Stearns: chestnuts from the fire

In mid-March 2008 Bear Stearns’ liquidity collapsed: hedge-fund clients fled, repo counterparties rejected collateral, price and confidence spiraled. On 14 March the Federal Reserve authorized the New York Fed to extend credit to Bear through JPMorgan Chase Bank; even so, pressure worsened into the weekend.⁷

On 16 March JPMorgan agreed to buy Bear — initially at about $2 a share, a jaw-dropper. To facilitate the deal, the New York Fed created Maiden Lane LLC to purchase roughly $30 billion of Bear’s less-liquid assets, funded by about $29 billion from the New York Fed and a roughly $1 billion subordinated loan from JPMorgan to take first loss.⁸

Shareholder fury and contract mechanics then forced a redo: around 24 March consideration rose to about $10 a share (via a fixed JPMorgan stock exchange ratio), with stronger ownership and guarantee arrangements. A fire sale became a slightly dearer fire sale — the structure unchanged: private buyer plus central-bank backstop of problem assets.

Two dollars is the price of fear; ten dollars is the surcharge of anger and legal risk — neither is the price of prosperity.
Footnotes
⁷ Bear’s liquidity crisis and the bridge (14 Mar 2008) Per Federal Reserve recounting: on 14 March 2008 the Board authorized the New York Fed to extend credit to Bear through JPMorgan Chase Bank so Bear could open. Even with the bridge, pressures intensified; without larger liquidity or a stronger acquirer, Bear likely would have filed on Monday 17 March. JPMorgan emerged as the only viable bidder.
Why it matters: pins the warlord’s move to the Fed timeline — without the bridge and the weekend, there is no purchase story.
Source: Federal Reserve page “Bear Stearns, JPMorgan Chase, and Maiden Lane LLC”
⁸ Maiden Lane and $2 → $10 Maiden Lane LLC bought ~$30B of Bear assets; FRBNY lent ~$29B; JPMorgan’s subordinated loan (~$1B) absorbed first loss (details refined in the renegotiation). The 16 March merger terms implied ~$2/share; the 24 March amendment set 0.21753 JPM shares per Bear share (~$10 based on pre-announcement JPM prices), plus a share exchange giving JPMorgan ~39.5% immediately.
Why it matters: the template of private consolidation plus public backstop — and the institutional backdrop for the author’s warning that the next Bear might not be savable.
Sources: Fed Maiden Lane explainer; FRASER §129 report; SEC/JPMorgan amendment filings; NYT/Forbes contemporaneous coverage

Cao Cao and Zhang Er–Chen Yu: mirrors

The author casts Dimon as a Cao Cao of chaotic times: not “villain,” but a figure who can impose order on disorder and demand matching power. The Weill–Dimon rupture is mirrored onto the Chinese historical pair Zhang Er and Chen Yu — sworn friends until power and survival snapped the bond overnight.⁹

The device is clear: readers who do not know Wall Street’s cast can still read capital empires as familiar power drama. The desk watches basis and haircuts; the author reminds us that behind every haircut sits who hates whom, who owes whom, and who is waiting to watch whom fall.

Historical mirrors are not pedantry — they are a way to read balance sheets as Romance of the Three Kingdoms and the Records of the Grand Historian.
Footnotes
⁹ Zhang Er–Chen Yu and the “master–apprentice rupture” motif Traditional Chinese histories (e.g. Records of the Grand Historian) tell of Zhang Er and Chen Yu as bosom friends who later turned mortal enemies over power in Zhao. Essays often use them for “hardship friends, prosperity foes / old bonds brittle before power.” This chapter maps that onto Weill–Dimon: co-builders → succession clash → rupture → the exile rising in another camp. Rhetorical, not a claim of financial-history fact identity.
Why it matters: explains how the title image “warlords of chaos” lands — financial history remembered as intrigue history.
Sources: Chinese historiographic tradition (Zhang Er / Chen Yu); book rhetoric vs public relationship timeline

The Fed saved one Bear — and the next?

The chapter closes on a cold fear: the Fed can use extraordinary tools and partner with JPMorgan to “process” one Bear Stearns; markets have already learned to ask — what if the next one is fatter, more systemic, and without a buyer? Maiden Lane is not an infinite magazine; political and moral-hazard blowback will come.

For the author on the desk, Bear is not an ending but a blood-colored cover page. The three giants carry wounds or ambitions; the warlord has drawn steel; and Wall Street’s very existence suddenly looks negotiable, discountable, overnight transferable.

Saving one bear does not make the zoo safe — it only means the keepers did not sleep tonight.
Footnotes
¹⁰ Institutional foreshadowing of “the next one” In the public crisis calendar, Bear is followed by the harsher September 2008: Lehman’s bankruptcy, Merrill’s sale, AIG support, money-market “break the buck,” and more. This chapter’s window ends in spring 2008, yet the closing line — the Fed may not save the next — reads afterward like a precise forecast. Footnote as timeline hint only; no spoilers of later book chapters.
Why it matters: rewrites the warlord’s “win” as a system catching its breath — victory local, risk global.
Sources: public crisis chronologies (Fed / FRBNY / press); contrasted with this chapter’s window
