          In 2007–08, media, hearings, and desks labeled hard-to-value, illiquid subprime CDOs, warehouse loans, and structured leftovers as toxic assets / toxic waste. The metaphor became a policy and accounting keyword—how to mark, how to ring-fence, who buys. This chapter treats the slang as linguistic encirclement: once the curse word unifies, patience for price discovery dies with it.

      <h2 id="s4">Hyper Goldman Act II: three endings</h2>
      <p>The author inserts a “Hyper Goldman” allegory—Act II—not a literal biography, but an exaggerated stage play of crisis survival scripts: pipes stuffed with unsold toxic leftovers; accounting tugging between mark-to-market and fair-value tricks—models, liquidity discounts, Level 3 assets, each choice rewriting tomorrow’s capital ratios.</p>
      <p>The escape route is written as a chain: raise Asia and Middle East sovereign capital; bring in Buffett-style preferred as a confidence anchor; add an equity raise; dump the dirtiest residue near 20 cents on the dollar to a distressed fund; finally become a bank holding company (BHC) to reach the Fed’s discount window and a stickier deposit base.<a class="fn-link" href="#fn4"><sup class="fn">⁴</sup></a><a class="fn-link" href="#fn5"><sup class="fn">⁵</sup></a></p>
      <p>The allegory closes on three endings: Goldman-like—finance, transform, live; Bear-like—arranged into a larger buyer; Lehman-like—weekend talks fail, Monday bankruptcy. When the lights come up, the audience sees these are not science fiction—they are the scripts Wall Street will actually stage over the next year.</p>
