CHAPTER TEN · Ambush from Ten Sides

Late-October double top: buyers hibernate

In late October 2007, the chill of the equity double top seeped onto the desk. Screens still looked “healthy,” but the texture of two-way markets had changed: bid lists grew longer while real buyers hibernated—calls went out and came back as watchful silence.

The “lucky” Tennessee housing bond flipped into regret: marks moved the wrong way, and a stop-loss was nothing like cutting Multso rice. Housing credit was thin; counterparties scarce; selling into that void could itself become a second shock.

Colleague Hu You found a bid around 4.70%—about 20 basis points wider than the earlier ~4.50% anchor. A small number, but a thermometer: the Street was re-sticker-pricing the same paper with a harsher yield.

Citi and Merrill: megabank writedowns

What tore open “the crisis seems past” were core-bank writedowns: Citigroup on the order of ~$10 billion and Merrill Lynch ~$9.4 billion in asset hits and loss expectations, dragging the fight from the fringe back into Wall Street’s chest.¹

The transmission path was no longer fuzzy: edge originators and mortgage pipes first, then hedge funds forced to de-lever and dump, then core commercial and investment banks’ warehouses and CDO inventory. Indexes could still pretend calm; P&L and valuation footnotes were already bleeding.

When the periphery burns, the core can still say “contained.” When Citi and Merrill both print ten-digit writedowns, the narrative itself becomes fuel.
Section notes
¹ Citigroup / Merrill late-2007 writedown wave From deep autumn into year-end 2007, Citigroup repeatedly raised expected losses and writedowns tied to subprime and CDO exposures (public reports and filings spanning multi-billion to tens-of-billions adjustments); Merrill, in the same window, kept enlarging FICC/CDO/subprime hits (Q3 already ~$7.9B, later quarters larger still). Markets read simultaneous core-bank “blowups” as crisis wave two—the chapter’s ~$10B / ~$9.4B figures are narrative anchors for that “ten-digit reopen fire” moment, not a single 8-K line item.
Why it matters: snaps the “healthy skin” story back to “organ hemorrhage”—core writedowns are the encirclement signal.
Sources: Citi / Merrill 2007 earnings releases; Reuters and contemporaneous coverage of the writedown wave

Why them: supermarket bank and First Franklin

Why Citi and Merrill first in the public blast radius? Structure: Citi was a financial supermarket—retail, wholesale, and IB pipes in one house—with warehouses of housing-related inventory and CDO exposure. Rewrite the marks, and losses write straight into group P&L.¹

Merrill, around September 2006, bought First Franklin—folding subprime origination into its securitization machine for vertical integration: source loan → package → distribute. In a bull market that is efficiency; in a crash it welds toxin into the balance sheet.²

“Toxic waste” entered everyday speech: not a lab term, but traders’ curse for structured sludge that would not sell, would not mark cleanly, and still sat on books.⁶ Washington Mutual, UBS, RBS and others joined the same casualty list. Merrill-linked CDS was chanted near ~500 bps—insurance cost itself became a fear gauge.³

Supermarket banks fear the warehouse; vertical integrators fear the source—when both blow at once, “toxic” stops being an adjective and becomes an accounting line.
Section notes
² Merrill’s First Franklin deal (Sept 2006 agreement) In September 2006 Merrill agreed to buy National City’s First Franklin origination franchise and related servicing for about $1.3 billion, plus a separate purchase of billions in First Franklin–originated loans—aimed at feeding non-prime product into CDO/securitization lines. The deal closed near the subprime cycle’s crest; origination and warehouse exposure later fed Merrill’s 2007–08 writedowns and shrinkage, and First Franklin was eventually shuttered.
Why it matters: “why Merrill” was not bad luck—it was late-cycle vertical integration welding toxin into its own pipe.
Sources: National City / Merrill 5 Sep 2006 press materials and SEC exhibits; LA Times / HousingWire follow-ons
³ Bank CDS spreads and the ~500 bp fear reading Credit-default-swap spreads price the annualized cost of insuring an issuer’s debt (in basis points). From late 2007 into 2008, large bank and dealer CDS widened sharply; names like Merrill were routinely cited in the hundreds of bps (the book’s ~500 bps as a fear anchor). Spreads fed back into funding costs, counterparty limits, and confidence that short-term debt could still roll.
Why it matters: equities are skin; CDS is closer to whether the market is already pricing organ failure.
Sources: standard crisis histories of bank CDS widening; Bloomberg / contemporaneous credit-market reporting
⁶ “Toxic assets / toxic waste” enters the vernacular In 2007–08, media, hearings, and desks labeled hard-to-value, illiquid subprime CDOs, warehouse loans, and structured leftovers as toxic assets / toxic waste. The metaphor became a policy and accounting keyword—how to mark, how to ring-fence, who buys. This chapter treats the slang as linguistic encirclement: once the curse word unifies, patience for price discovery dies with it.
Why it matters: slang popularity = consensus formation—and consensus accelerates fire-sales and writedowns.
Sources: contemporary financial media usage; post-crisis policy documents retaining “toxic assets”

Hyper Goldman Act II: three endings

The author inserts a “Hyper Goldman” allegory—Act II—not a literal biography, but an exaggerated stage play of crisis survival scripts: pipes stuffed with unsold toxic leftovers; accounting tugging between mark-to-market and fair-value tricks—models, liquidity discounts, Level 3 assets, each choice rewriting tomorrow’s capital ratios.

The escape route is written as a chain: raise Asia and Middle East sovereign capital; bring in Buffett-style preferred as a confidence anchor; add an equity raise; dump the dirtiest residue near 20 cents on the dollar to a distressed fund; finally become a bank holding company (BHC) to reach the Fed’s discount window and a stickier deposit base.⁴⁵

The allegory closes on three endings: Goldman-like—finance, transform, live; Bear-like—arranged into a larger buyer; Lehman-like—weekend talks fail, Monday bankruptcy. When the lights come up, the audience sees these are not science fiction—they are the scripts Wall Street will actually stage over the next year.

Act II’s cruelty: the same toolkit—writedowns, raises, dumps, bank conversion—yields three endings that differ by one step of confidence collapse.
Section notes
⁴ Bank holding company (BHC) and the Fed window In the U.S., BHCs sit under the Bank Holding Company Act and Fed oversight; conversion (or financial-holding status) lets firms operate deposit subsidiaries in a fuller banking frame and, in crisis, reach the discount window, payments rails, and later liquidity facilities more cleanly. In September 2008 Goldman and Morgan Stanley won approval to become BHCs—widely read as the last two large standalone investment banks trading independence for the central-bank safety net.
Why it matters: “become a bank” in the allegory is not rhetoric—it was a real institutional escape hatch.
Sources: Federal Reserve 21 Sep 2008 approvals for Goldman/MS BHC status; contemporaneous reporting
⁵ Buffett preferred capital (Goldman case) In September 2008 Berkshire Hathaway invested about $5 billion in Goldman preferred (roughly 10% dividend, callable at a premium) plus warrants; Goldman also sold common equity. Markets read it as celebrity capital plus hard cash. The chapter’s “Buffett-style preferred” uses such public deals as prototype for how preferred can be both capital and confidence technology in a crunch.
Why it matters: pulls Hyper Goldman Act II from fiction back to checkable crisis finance.
Sources: Berkshire / Goldman Sep 2008 announcements; NBC / Reuters contemporaneous coverage

Dark cave: who will be Cao, Liu, Sun?

Writedowns, CDS, toxic slang, three allegorical deaths—the author feels trapped in a dark cave: exits unclear, the torch lighting only gravel at his feet. The 2008 curtain is rising; Cao Cao, Liu Bei, and Sun Quan’s seats are still empty—who unifies the wreckage, who grinds on, who splits the river will show only after the real ten-sided ambush closes.

Tennessee regret, the 4.70% re-quote, Citi and Merrill’s numbers, and the Hyper Goldman play say one thing together: last chapter’s “crisis seems past” was illusion; this chapter, the ambush has already shut.

The cave’s terror is not darkness—it is thinking you are still walking on open plain until drums sound from ten sides and every exit is sealed.
