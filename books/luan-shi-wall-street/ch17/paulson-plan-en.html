CHAPTER SEVENTEEN · The Paulson Plan

Fire chiefs: Paulson and Bernanke

After Lehman, Wall Street lived weekend to weekend: Fannie and Freddie into conservatorship, Lehman gone, Merrill into Bank of America, AIG rescued on punishing terms. By around September 20, Morgan Stanley, Washington Mutual, even Goldman Sachs looked perched on a ledge. The more fires they fought, the more blazes appeared—Depression-era bank failures leaping from textbooks onto trading screens.¹

Two “fire chiefs” carried the brief: Treasury Secretary Hank Paulson, former head of Goldman Sachs; and Fed Chair Ben Bernanke, a scholar of the 1930s Depression. One knew how markets break; the other knew how breakage becomes catastrophe. From the Lucy Bank desk, Yuyang could see the next act moving from Manhattan to Capitol Hill.

Root disease and complications: two strong doses

The author diagnoses the crisis: bank leverage too high, hard-to-value troubled assets marking down thin capital—the “root disease”; investor flight and aggressive shorting—the deadly “complications.” First control complications, then treat the root.

Washington administered two strong doses: emergency short-sale limits on financials to cool equities; and a Treasury temporary guarantee for money-market funds to stop runs from freezing commercial paper and repo. Complications eased for a moment, but both men knew the root would reignite without a larger net.²

Bernanke’s toolkit was powerful yet incomplete. The Fed can lend and buy Treasuries; it cannot wholesale-purchase toxic assets or inject bank capital—“spending” belongs to Treasury and Congress. Within days of Lehman, Paulson’s public stance swung: from non-intervention rhetoric toward aggressive rescue. Pandora’s box was open; sealing it required a net big enough to hold the plague.

Fever reducers let the patient speak; surgery needs a knife stamped “congressional authority.”
Section notes
² Short restrictions and the money-market guarantee Around 18–19 September 2008, U.K. and U.S. regulators imposed emergency short-sale limits on financial stocks; the U.S. Treasury announced a temporary guarantee for eligible money-market funds to interrupt the “break-the-buck → run → CP freeze” chain. These bought time; they did not refill bank capital.
Why it matters: explains why a brief calm still required a fiscal program on the order of $700 billion.
Source: SEC / U.S. Treasury September 2008 releases; FRASER; FCIC

$700 billion and three pages: to Congress

Around that Friday, Paulson floated the bombshell: authority to buy up to about $700 billion in troubled assets—the seed of the Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP). Markets cheered; but $700 billion is not a secretary’s decree or a president’s pen alone. Under separation of powers, big spending needs Congress.³

Worse was the draft’s shape. Public archives show Treasury’s first legislative proposal ran roughly three pages: authority to buy mortgage-related assets, thin reporting, and language making decisions largely non-reviewable. Ordinary billion-dollar asks come with binders; this blank-check sketch walked onto Capitol Hill—and anger split between the sum and the process.

Three pages were not poetic thrift—they were power and fear compressed. Markets wanted speed; democracy wanted oversight.
Section notes
³ The three-page proposal and $700 billion authority Around 20 September 2008, Treasury released a ~three-page “Legislative Proposal for Treasury Authority to Purchase Mortgage-Related Assets,” seeking up to $700 billion. Non-reviewability clauses drew bipartisan transparency fire. Negotiations later expanded the text into the much longer Emergency Economic Stabilization Act (EESA) framework creating TARP.
Why it matters: nails “the Paulson Plan” to a verifiable document fight, not a slogan.
Source: FRASER / U.S. Treasury three-page text; Yale YPFS; contemporary congressional coverage

Pleasing neither side: the political arithmetic

Democratic progressives distrusted “greedy Wall Street”; Republican conservatives hated sweeping state intervention. The plan pleased nobody. Voters saw a simpler movie: Fannie yesterday, AIG today—taxpayer pits—while the contagion chain was hard to explain. A Goldman alum at Treasury did not help the politics.⁴

The chambers differ. Senators serve six-year terms and can sometimes “take the long view”; House members face voters every two years and track popular anger more tightly. Paulson could not be sure: even members who understood the stakes might refuse to risk constituents for a sector nobody loved.

Finance clocks contagion in hours; politics clocks votes in election cycles—they rarely sync.
Section notes
⁴ Bailout politics: left-right pincer and public rage In late-September 2008 debate, progressives stressed pay limits and taxpayer safeguards; conservatives warned against “socialism.” Hearings and reporting show Paulson and Bernanke repeatedly arguing the freeze already threatened the real economy, not only Street balance sheets.
Why it matters: foreshadows the House “no”—not ignorance, but misaligned incentives versus market time.
Source: Senate Banking hearing coverage; POLITICO / NYT contemporaneous reports; FCIC

Morgan Stanley and Goldman self-rescue

While Congress argued, Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs moved—soft and hard tactics almost together. Soft: apply to become bank holding companies, aligning closer to Fed discount-window and deposit-funding narratives. After Bear Stearns, primary-dealer facilities already existed; renaming did not magically rewrite pipes, but a “commercial bank” label calmed investors and left room to replace overnight wholesale funding with stickier liabilities.⁵

Hard: raise equity. Lehman’s lesson was taped to the wall—cash calms hearts. Morgan Stanley moved fast with Japan’s Mitsubishi UFJ on roughly a $9 billion stake for about 20%; Goldman hunted a domestic buyer who still “had teeth”—Buffett, next.

Renaming is confidence engineering; cash is balance-sheet engineering.
Section notes
⁵ 21 Sep 2008: Goldman and Morgan Stanley become BHCs The Federal Reserve approved Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley as bank holding companies on 21 September 2008, placing them under Fed consolidated supervision and clarifying access to standing liquidity facilities. Morgan Stanley advanced its Mitsubishi UFJ capital deal; Goldman announced Buffett’s preferred investment (next section).
Why it matters: the stand-alone investment-bank model accelerated toward “commercial-bankization” after Lehman.
Source: Federal Reserve 21 Sep 2008 release; firm press; FRASER

Buffett and the peanuts: who still has teeth

Goldman was almost “unfairly” punished: it had cleaned problem assets early and even stayed profitable shorting related credit protection. Post-Lehman panic did not care—twenty-plus turns of leverage still dies in a run. Management had to raise capital.

Yuyang borrows a teahouse lament traders know: when markets are calm you can buy freely but bargains are scarce; at the climax munis are absurdly cheap, yet the bank clamps leverage—“peanuts at last, and no teeth.” Whenever the floor is covered in peanuts, an elder with permanent teeth appears: Warren Buffett, backed by insurance float—premiums in first, claims later, cheap funding only weakly tied to capital-market open windows.⁶

Buffett put about $5 billion into Goldman perpetual preferreds at roughly a 10% coupon, plus warrants to buy another $5 billion of common below the then market—classic crisis value investing. Goldman followed with a ~$5 billion common offering. Two raises beat Lehman’s refusal to sell cheap.

Value investing’s secret is often not a better P/E model—it is still having teeth when others do not.
Section notes
⁶ Buffett–Goldman September 2008 preferred deal Public disclosures: Berkshire invested $5 billion in Goldman perpetual preferred stock with a 10% dividend and warrants to buy $5 billion of common at $115 per share. The deal was read as a confidence signal; Goldman then issued additional common equity. Insurance float helps explain Buffett’s ability to act when wholesale funding freezes.
Why it matters: turns “self-rescue” into terms, price, and funding structure.
Source: Berkshire / Goldman releases and filings summaries; FT/WSJ contemporaneous reports

WaMu and Wachovia: press one gourd, raise another

As Goldman and Morgan Stanley caught breath, Washington Mutual (WaMu) did not. After Lehman it faced a deposit run. Regulators closed the bank subsidiary on 25 September; FDIC sold the deposit franchise to JPMorgan Chase for about $1.9 billion in public reports—vast branches and customers. Depositors were protected; equity and many creditors were not—another hammer on credit confidence after Lehman.⁷

Press one gourd, another rises: larger Wachovia came under siege. Officials brokered emergency marriages—Citigroup’s fire-line plan, then Wells Fargo’s winning bid. Patchwork rescues multiplied until Paulson’s conviction hardened: only a $700 billion “heavy barrage,” not one-off pairings, could hold the field.

Deposit insurance can shield the retail counter; it cannot soothe wholesale creditors’ nerves.
Section notes
⁷ WaMu failure and JPMorgan’s purchase On 25 September 2008, OTS/FDIC closed Washington Mutual Bank and sold banking operations to JPMorgan Chase (about $1.9 billion in contemporary reports)—among the largest U.S. bank failures. Creditor losses repriced financial debt risk; Wachovia’s crisis then drove further merger drama (Wells Fargo prevailing).
Why it matters: shows the autopsy cost of legislative delay while Congress debated.
Source: FDIC releases; FRASER; FCIC / merger reporting

September 29: House rejection and freefall

After weekend talks, the three-page sketch bloated into a thick bill with oversight and pay limits. Leaders, briefly confident, scheduled votes into the trading day hoping for an instant rally—an accidental social experiment that ran in reverse.

On Monday 29 September, the House vote aired live. Investors smelled defeat: indices slid, then collapsed. The House rejected the package about 228–205; the Dow fell roughly 777 points (~7%), with the S&P posting a rare single-day plunge. Funding looked ready to snap; the Fed poured more liquidity to keep patients breathing without curing the disease.⁸

Markets translate roll-call machines: when “no” lands, prices answer in hundreds of points of fear.
Section notes
⁸ 29 Sep 2008 House rejection and ~777-point Dow drop The House rejected the first large rescue package (often tallied 228–205). The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 777.68 points—then among its largest point drops—with the S&P 500 down more than 8%. Days later the Senate passed a revised measure on 1 October; the House approved 263–171 on 3 October; President Bush signed Public Law 110-343 the same day.
Why it matters: the chapter’s climax—political refusal instantly priced on trading floors.
Source: Clerk of the House / Congress.gov; market records; EESA chronologies

Passage and the equity pivot: toward the darkest hour

In the days after rejection, markets re-educated Congress. Paulson and Bernanke lobbied again; on 3 October the revised Emergency Economic Stabilization Act became law and TARP’s frame stood up. With the “bazooka” in hand, Paulson faced a new puzzle: buying mortgage-related assets hits a pricing wall—pay too much and taxpayers lose; pay too little and bank holes deepen. Deleveraging either shrinks assets or grows equity; buying assets is slow, injecting capital is often faster.⁹

Market-leaning by instinct, Paulson hesitated before quasi-nationalization. Yet markets kept convulsing after passage; on 8 October the U.K. led with bank capital and debt guarantees, euro-area peers followed—if the U.S. lagged, funds might flee to guaranteed European banks. Treasury then summoned nine major banks for a combined ~$125 billion capital purchase—little room to opt out.¹⁰

As 13–14 October news hit, U.S. equities surged and bank CDS tightened; Lucy Bank risk even took Goldman and Morgan Stanley off the blacklist—Yuyang felt the muzzle of total collapse lift an inch. But the book’s next title is already written: the darkest hour still waits. Authority is not trust rebuilt; global deleveraging and credit freezes would bite again.¹¹

The bazooka is loaded; whether night yields is for the next chapter—the darkest hour.
Section notes
⁹ EESA signed and the asset-purchase pricing bind On 3 October 2008, EESA became law, authorizing up to $700 billion under TARP. Policy debate then and later stressed auction/purchase pricing and adverse selection; Treasury soon pivoted toward the Capital Purchase Program—preferred equity into banks.
Why it matters: explains why “the plan passed” still required a different playbook.
Source: Public Law 110-343; U.S. Treasury TARP history; FCIC

Collective participation was meant to reduce stigma.
Why it matters: international policy competition forced the U.S. from buying assets toward buying equity.
