      <p>Once the window cracked open and strong hands appeared, the Street’s subtext became: someone will catch the worst—Fed, megabanks, somehow Uncle Sam. Moral hazard was not a textbook abstraction but desk muscle memory: risk appetite crept higher on the belief that “you don’t really die,” and leverage and duration began to sneak back.</p>
      <p>What the author fears is exactly that psychology: the crisis seems past, so take more risk—and that extra risk becomes the next storm’s fuel.</p>

      <div class="scene">
        The clearer the safety net, the farther people walk the wire—until the wire itself is systemic.
      </div>

      <h2 id="s6">October 9: healthy skin, cracked bones</h2>
      <p>On October 9, 2007, the S&amp;P 500 closed near 1565—a high that rhymed with the early-2000s top like a double top on the chart. BRIC decoupling talk ran hot: emerging markets could supposedly grow without sinking with U.S. credit.<a class="fn-link" href="#fn6"><sup class="fn">⁶</sup></a> China A-share lore pushed toward a climax near 6092 (the Shanghai Composite would print its historic peak slightly later in mid-October).</p>
      <p>The author offers a body metaphor: equities are skin, credit is bone, banks are organs. Skin can look flushed and healthy while bones already crack and wholesale/shadow-bank organs bleed inside.<a class="fn-link" href="#fn7"><sup class="fn">⁷</sup></a> Markets “look fine” when eyes habitually check only the skin.</p>
