CHAPTER NINE · THE CRISIS SEEMS PAST

Summer storm = a bank run

Strip Chapter 8’s indexes and acronyms, and the summer storm was a modern bank run: not depositors in a lobby queue, but cash yanking simultaneously out of repo, commercial paper, and shadow-bank conduits. Private balance sheets pledged and haircut each other; once collateral lost trust, liquidity ebbed like a tide.³

This chapter states the blunt rule: only a central bank—the Federal Reserve—can truly inject system-wide liquidity. Strong private hands can catch assets and shout “buying opportunity,” but refilling the pipes still depends on the discount window and open-market operations.

Panic of 1907 and the Fed’s birth

To explain why only the Fed can “add water,” the author rewinds to the Panic of 1907: trust-company runs, a broken clearing chain, and J.P. Morgan locking Wall Street’s chiefs inside his library to improvise a temporary central bank with private capital and personal authority.¹

That library rescue fed the Federal Reserve Act of 1913: a public–private hybrid—a bankers’ bank with what we now call a dual mandate. It is not an ordinary commercial bank, yet it owns the system’s liquidity backstop.²

1907 stitched cracks with private authority; after 1913, stitching was supposed to be institutional—until shadow banking grew cracks outside the charter again.
Section notes
¹ Panic of 1907 In autumn 1907, cascading runs on New York trust companies and brokerages nearly froze equities and money markets. J.P. Morgan convened bankers at his Madison Avenue library, coordinating liquidity pools and stopgaps as a de facto lender of last resort. Reform pressure produced the National Monetary Commission and, ultimately, the 1913 Federal Reserve System.
Why it matters here: welds the “summer storm” to the Fed’s origin myth—private rescue was not enough.
Sources: Federal Reserve History; standard encyclopedic crisis summaries
² Fed structure: public–private hybrid and bankers’ bank The System pairs a Board of Governors in Washington with twelve Reserve Banks; member banks hold Reserve Bank stock, and governance mixes public appointees with banking-sector directors. The Fed supplies the discount window and payment rails and runs monetary policy—hence “bankers’ bank.” The modern dual mandate targets maximum employment and price stability; crisis last-resort lending sits beside that frame.
Why it matters here: private strong hands catching paper still cannot replace the Fed’s systemic liquidity promise.
Sources: federalreserve.gov structure explainers; Fed education materials

August 17: the discount-rate signal

On August 17, 2007, the Fed did not yet cut the federal funds target. It cut the primary credit discount rate 50 basis points (to 5.75%), narrowed the window’s spread over the funds rate, and allowed term financing up to about 30 days—a signal ahead of a full easing cycle, acknowledging market failure without immediately surrendering the inflation-versus-banking-crisis bind.³

Phones lit up: counterparties asked whether anyone had bonds to offer. Liquidity expectations shifted first; sellers’ courage returned a notch. Municipals bounced; crushed credit spreads exhaled.

No immediate funds cut—just a wider crack in the discount door. The Street heard: the central bank sees the run, not that the inflation objective has surrendered.
Section notes
³ Discount rate vs. federal funds rate (17 Aug 2007) The funds target anchors overnight interbank reserves; primary credit at the discount window usually sits above that target as a backup facility. On 17 August 2007 the Board approved a 50bp cut in the primary credit rate to 5¾%, narrowing the spread to the 5.25% funds target to 50bp, and allowed renewable term financing up to 30 days while keeping a broad collateral list. The first funds-rate cut followed on 18 September.
Why it matters here: institutional meaning of “signal, not full easing”—fix the pipes before declaring a cycle.
Source: Federal Reserve press release monetary20070817a (17 Aug 2007)

Strong hands: Citadel, BofA, the bounce

Strong hands showed up. Citadel took nearly all of Sowood’s book after July’s credit smash—capital and nerve buying risk at fire-sale marks.⁵ Bank of America injected about $2 billion of convertible preferred into Countrywide, a balance-sheet sedative for the nation’s largest mortgage lender.⁴

ABX and related subprime gauges bounced; municipal basis tightened from roughly 140 toward 110—not a cured patient, just the sharpest panic blade pulled back. A generation of traders revived the mantra that every crisis is a buying opportunity—S&L, Russia, the dot-com bust—each corpse had minted fortunes in the rubble. This time, they thought the script would rhyme again.

Strong hands turn screens green. Green is not healed bone—only someone willing to warehouse risk others refuse at a lower price.
Section notes
⁴ Bank of America’s $2B Countrywide stake (22 Aug 2007) After Countrywide drew an ~$11.5B credit line amid short-term funding stress mid-August, Bank of America completed a ~$2B strategic equity investment on 22 August: 7.25% Series B non-voting convertible preferred, convertible at about $18/share. CEO Ken Lewis argued markets were underpricing Countrywide’s franchise. It was the corporate-finance version of “strong hands”; a year later BofA would buy Countrywide outright.
Why it matters here: links private rescue to later moral-hazard and organ metaphors.
Sources: Countrywide/BofA 22 Aug 2007 release and 8-K; CNBC/NPR contemporaneous coverage
⁵ Citadel’s purchase of Sowood (30 Jul 2007) Sowood Capital (Jeffrey Larson) Alpha funds lost roughly half their value in July’s credit rout, with failing hedges and looming margin calls. A 30 July investor letter said substantially all of the portfolio had been sold to Citadel Investment Group to avoid forced liquidation; post-deal NAV was still down >50% month-to-date. Ken Griffin called it an orderly transfer of risk—one of summer 2007’s clearest “strong hands” episodes.
Why it matters here: a bounce after someone catches paper ≠ systemic risk resolved.
Sources: Sowood 30 Jul 2007 investor letter (public PDF); Reuters/MarketWatch

Moral hazard: Uncle Sam as backstop

Once the window cracked open and strong hands appeared, the Street’s subtext became: someone will catch the worst—Fed, megabanks, somehow Uncle Sam. Moral hazard was not a textbook abstraction but desk muscle memory: risk appetite crept higher on the belief that “you don’t really die,” and leverage and duration began to sneak back.

What the author fears is exactly that psychology: the crisis seems past, so take more risk—and that extra risk becomes the next storm’s fuel.

The clearer the safety net, the farther people walk the wire—until the wire itself is systemic.

October 9: healthy skin, cracked bones

On October 9, 2007, the S&P 500 closed near 1565—a high that rhymed with the early-2000s top like a double top on the chart. BRIC decoupling talk ran hot: emerging markets could supposedly grow without sinking with U.S. credit.⁶ China A-share lore pushed toward a climax near 6092 (the Shanghai Composite would print its historic peak slightly later in mid-October).

The author offers a body metaphor: equities are skin, credit is bone, banks are organs. Skin can look flushed and healthy while bones already crack and wholesale/shadow-bank organs bleed inside.⁷ Markets “look fine” when eyes habitually check only the skin.

Double tops can lie; decoupling can lie; only when you touch skin, bone, and organs together do you know if the body is faking recovery.
Section notes
⁶ BRIC “decoupling” (2007) Mid-2000s BRIC branding and talk that emerging growth could run relatively independent of advanced economies peaked as U.S. and some EM equities hit highs together in autumn 2007—feeding hopes that U.S. credit stress was “local.” Later synchronized trade, dollar-funding, and risk-appetite collapses showed coupling ran far deeper than the slogan.
Why it matters here: explains why October’s global equity carnival could mask U.S. shadow-bank injury.
Sources: contemporaneous decoupling commentary; post-crisis IMF/academic retrospectives
⁷ Equities vs. credit: surface vs. systemic risk Equity indexes price earnings and risk appetite; credit spreads, AB gauges, bank wholesale funding, and repo haircuts better show whether intermediary chains can still roll. Autumn 2007 often showed “strong stocks / stressed credit”: new equity highs beside pressure in subprime/structured credit, money markets, and interbank spreads. Systemic risk usually writes first on credit and bank organs, then on skin (prices).
Why it matters here: the chapter’s diagnostic tool—don’t let a skin check replace an organ exam.
Sources: crisis histories on ABX / LIBOR–OIS / bank CDS divergences from equities

Tennessee housing bonds: a coin-flip warning

The author’s desk mandate had not flipped to defense: management still wanted the municipal housing book grown. In October, a bid-wanted showed Tennessee AAA housing-related paper around 4.50%. First try felt like a coin flip—he lost. Soon another ~$10 million of similar bonds appeared; he bought.

In hindsight that bond would ride the entire crisis—luck at the time, and also a warning: in the month when “the crisis seemed past,” long-duration housing credit was still being stuffed onto balance sheets, and a buyer’s “good luck” often meant carrying the risk longer.

Lose one coin flip, win the next—on the tape it looks like execution; on the ledger it can look like buying a ticket for the whole crisis early.

Calm after battle = reserves spent

The quiet of late summer into fall felt like the hush after a fight: guns stop not because the enemy is gone, but because the reserves are spent. The author reaches for autumn 1942 at Stalingrad—the alleyway lull while an encirclement is already closing.¹

Discount signal, strong hands, equity highs, housing bonds still scaling: each looks like proof the crisis is past; together they look more like the illusion before the ring closes. The next chapter asks not when skin flushes again, but when bone and organ fracture in public.

Post-battle quiet is the most dangerous: you misread spent ammunition as victory, and encirclement as a truce.
