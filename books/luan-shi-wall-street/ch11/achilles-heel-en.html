CHAPTER ELEVEN · Achilles’ Heel of Wall Street

Achilles’ heel: addiction to short-term funding

The Greek hero Achilles was nearly invulnerable—except at the heel his mother held when she dipped him in the Styx.¹ The author aims that metaphor at Wall Street: Greek-letter risk models glitter on every screen—VaR, correlations, stress tests—yet the lethal addiction is to short-term funding. When overnight and ultra-short cash vanishes, the finest model is only embroidery on armor.

Chapter 10 pushed core-bank writedowns onto the stage; this chapter digs one layer deeper: writedowns wound capital, but the heel wound is whether you can roll funding every morning.

Section notes
¹ Achilles’ heel metaphor In Greek myth, Thetis dipped Achilles in the river Styx to make him invulnerable, holding him by the heel—the one spot left dry. An arrow later found that spot. “Achilles’ heel” became the English idiom for a fatal, hidden weakness in an otherwise strong system. This chapter maps it onto Wall Street: Greek-letter risk models as armor; wholesale short-term funding dependence as the heel.
Why it matters: separates “sophisticated risk management” from “must refinance every day”—the latter snaps first in a crisis.
Sources: classical myth tradition and English idiom usage; crisis literature on wholesale funding fragility

Money markets and repo: the blood circulation

Money-market funds were sold as cash-first products: protect principal, stay liquid, skim a little yield.² They bought commercial paper, repo, and short paper, pumping household and corporate cash into the wholesale machine. Repo was Wall Street’s blood circulation: securities for cash today, reverse tomorrow; the haircut set how much cushion the cash lender demanded.³

On the Lucy Bank desk, a live example: counterparties hiked haircuts and pushed funding toward federal funds plus about 20 basis points. Small number, cold tone—“blank-check” relationships were tightening. The overlooked repo desk that used to joke about “lots of cash on the sidelines” suddenly found no cash on the sidelines.

When the repo desk is ignored, everyone laughs that cash is plentiful; when it matters, the joke has already expired.
Section notes
² Money-market funds Open-end funds that invest mainly in high-quality, short-duration instruments (Treasuries, commercial paper, repo, etc.), aiming for stable NAV, cash-like liquidity, and a yield edge over deposits. In 2007–08 some funds strained on ABCP and related paper; the September 2008 Reserve Primary Fund “break the buck” episode later became a confidence landmark (slightly after this chapter’s core window, same mechanism).
Why it matters: they are the pipes that look like cash—when the pipes clog, wholesale funding loses oxygen.
Sources: SEC / ICI explainers on money-market funds; crisis histories of 2007–08 short-end runs
³ Repo haircuts In a repurchase agreement, the cash lender typically advances less than the collateral’s face or market value; the percentage gap is the haircut. Higher haircuts mean less cash against the same bonds—forced de-leveraging. In the crisis, on-the-run Treasuries often kept easier terms while housing-related collateral saw haircuts explode or bids refused—the same “bloodstream” sorted by collateral quality and expelled the weak.
Why it matters: haircuts are the invisible leverage dial—tightening them kills positions faster than a downgrade.
Sources: New York Fed explainers on tri-party repo and haircuts; crisis accounts of 2007–08 haircut differentiation

Pre-crisis: low haircuts and pass-the-parcel

Before the break, markets were trained on optimism: even weaker collateral could clear at thin haircuts. Structured short-term funding—asset-backed commercial paper (ABCP), structured investment vehicles (SIVs), and municipal tender-option-bond (TOB) structures—worked like repo in spirit: short money rolling long assets, surviving on continuous rollover and the appearance of safety.⁴

The author frames those months as pass-the-parcel: everyone assumed the next hand would take the package; when the music stopped, whoever held the paper discovered they were not a passer-by but an owner.

Low haircuts were the bull market’s hymn; pass-the-parcel was its etiquette—both assumed tomorrow’s liquidity would still show up.
Section notes
⁴ ABCP / SIV / TOB: repo-like structured short funding ABCP is short paper issued by conduits against loans, receivables, or securitized assets; SIVs funded longer credit assets with short debt/ABCP and lived on rollover; TOB structures split long municipal bonds into floating-rate certificates and residual pieces, with the short end dependent on liquidity and bank support. In 2007–08, when buyers stepped away or demanded wider spreads, these pipes contracted and risk returned to sponsor banks’ balance sheets.
Why it matters: proves “short funding long assets” was not only classic repo—the heel wound was a whole short-funding web.
Sources: standard crisis histories of ABCP/SIV runs; municipal-finance explainers on TOB structures

Early 2008: differentiated haircuts and Kerviel

By early 2008, haircuts sorted by pedigree: on-the-run Treasuries still financed relatively easily; MBS worsened; hedge-fund “elephants” might still negotiate ~20% haircuts, while the shrimp were simply refused.

In January, European equities fell on the order of ~6% in a day and S&P futures flirted with limit-down atmosphere; the Fed answered with an emergency cut—the book’s narrative often compresses this to ~50 bp, while the public record shows a 75 bp intermeeting cut on January 22, then another 50 bp at the month-end meeting.⁷

Then came Jérôme Kerviel and Société Générale: unauthorized European index-futures positions on the order of ~€50 billion notional, a net loss near €4.9 billion after unwind.⁵ The author notes that the Fed was briefly fooled by the surface story of a “macro crash”—demons appear when liquidity ebbs.

Haircut tiering is a silent rating; Kerviel reminds us that a “macro” tape can be one hidden ticket tape.
Section notes
⁵ Kerviel / Société Générale (January 2008) In mid-to-late January 2008, Société Générale uncovered trader Jérôme Kerviel’s concealed directional European equity-index futures positions totaling roughly €50 billion notional (hidden via fictitious hedges). The bank unwound around January 21–23 and announced on January 24 a net loss of about €4.9 billion. The unwind overlapped a violent global equity move, making it hard to separate “macro panic” from “one bank’s forced liquidation”—the chapter’s “Fed fooled by appearances” points at that confusion.
Why it matters: grounds “demons appear when liquidity ebbs” in a checkable event—forced selling can masquerade as a macro crash.
Sources: Société Générale official notes and inspection-report summaries; 24 Jan 2008 announcement; contemporaneous Risk.net / financial press
⁷ Fed emergency cut, January 2008 Amid global equity plunges, the FOMC held an emergency call on January 21–22 and on January 22 cut the federal funds target by 75 basis points to 3.5% (discount rate moved in parallel)—the first intermeeting funds-rate move in years. At the January 29–30 scheduled meeting it cut another 50 basis points to 3%. The book’s “emergency ~50 bp” may compress the second cut or the month’s easing; the footnote separates the two public actions.
Why it matters: marks the heel wound traveling from trading desks to the central bank—short end and equities flashing together.
Sources: Federal Reserve FOMC statements 22 Jan and 30 Jan 2008; FRB Monetary Policy Report narrative

Flight to safety: conduit runs onto the balance sheet

Investors fled to safety. When ABCP, SIV, and TOB-style conduits faced runs or failed rollovers, sponsors such as Citigroup and Merrill Lynch had to pull assets back onto their own balance sheets—the off-balance game ended, and the heel wound became open capital burn.

Money-market promises of “cash first” collided, in the same weeks, with wholesale reality of “cash scarce.”

Flight to safety is not a moral choice; it is arithmetic: when the rollover window shuts, conduit assets come home.

ARS freeze: a new poker game and basis 210

In February 2008 the auction-rate securities (ARS) market froze: long-dated munis and similar paper that reset via periodic Dutch auctions, marketed as near-short liquidity. When banks stopped supporting bids, auctions failed en masse; retail holders discovered that “cash-like” product would not cash out.⁶

Lucy Bank saw a game: buy ARS at distressed levels, finance them in repo, and print ROE north of 200%—poker’s “new game.” The author built an ARS book on the order of ~$2 billion. Joy muted fast: related basis blew out toward ~210 basis points—discount capture while the hedge and funding cracks widened.

A new game can pencil >200% ROE; when basis hits 210, the calculator reminds you the heel is still bleeding.
Section notes
⁶ Auction-rate securities (ARS) and Dutch auctions ARS are typically long municipal bonds, corporates, or preferreds whose rates reset in periodic Dutch auctions: buyers bid the lowest rate they will accept; the clearing rate becomes the reset for holders and ownership turns over. Broker-dealers historically backstopped auctions with proprietary bids; from mid-February 2008 major firms withdrew support, auctions failed widely, holders could not exit as expected, and some municipal issuers faced penalty rates. Later buyback settlements with regulators followed.
Why it matters: ARS is the retail version of “long asset pretending to be short cash”—same family as repo and ABCP.
Sources: CRS / Chicago Fed / Wikipedia summaries of the Feb 2008 ARS freeze; SEC and state AG settlement coverage
