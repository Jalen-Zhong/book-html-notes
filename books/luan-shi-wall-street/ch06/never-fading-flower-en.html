CHAPTER SIX · THE NEVER-FADING FLOWER

Cyclical China meets linear U.S. optimism

The author arrives on Wall Street with a Chinese sense of cycles: dynasties rise and fall, bulls and bears rhyme, history bends back to bite its own tail. Many American colleagues default to a different clock — a ray sloping up and to the right, where technology, institutions, and markets push the world forward and drawdowns are mere interludes.

On the surface, 2006 is still calm: tight spreads, loud laughter, bonus stories still writing themselves. Yet he keeps smelling an older weather in the air — not one red blotch on a screen, but “chaos” returning like a tide from far offshore.

Dress rehearsal: LTCM, 1998

To hear 2006 clearly, the tape rewinds to 1998: Long-Term Capital Management as a dress rehearsal for disaster¹. John Meriwether carries the Salomon Brothers arbitrage pedigree; beside him stand Myron Scholes, Robert Merton, and former Fed vice chair David Mullins — a stack of business cards that almost trademarks “reason” itself.

Early returns look surgically bright: volatility seems tamed, spread trades tick like a precision clock. Outsiders treat the firm as proof that markets can be domesticated by equations.

Section notes
¹ LTCM (Long-Term Capital Management) A Connecticut hedge fund founded in 1994, famous for fixed-income relative-value / convergence trades. In 1998 it nearly failed, prompting a Fed-coordinated private rescue among major Wall Street counterparties at the New York Fed. It was not a government equity nationalization — yet it showed regulators and markets that a single fund could become too large to exit cleanly.
Why it matters: this chapter treats LTCM as the historical rhyme for Amaranth in 2006 — the same play in preview.
Sources: public crisis histories; Roger Lowenstein, When Genius Failed, and related secondary narratives

Spreads, leverage, and the “genius” brand

LTCM’s core script is not mystical: bet that spreads between related bonds or instruments will converge, then amplify thin edges with financing and derivatives²³. Success eats itself — copycats crowd in, spreads compress; to keep the same profit, leverage must rise. Public post-mortems often cite balance-sheet leverage around ~25×, with derivatives notionals pushing “invisible leverage” still higher.

The more genius in the room, the less room left; the more confident the model, the thinner the margin for error.

Section notes
² When Genius Failed Roger Lowenstein’s classic narrative of LTCM’s rise and fall: Salomon arb culture, star partners, the Russia shock, correlation breakdown, and the Wall Street rescue. Not a regulatory report, yet it shaped the popular memory that “quant genius” can fail collectively.
Why it matters: the shared “dress-rehearsal script” many readers bring to this chapter arrives through such public storytelling.
Sources: Lowenstein, When Genius Failed (2000); public reviews and crisis-history citations
³ Black–Scholes–Merton option pricing The continuous-time option framework associated with Fischer Black, Myron Scholes, and Robert Merton — work that helped earn Scholes and Merton the 1997 Nobel Prize in Economics. Idealizing assumptions often include continuous trading, lognormal dynamics, and Brownian-like noise. When liquidity snaps and crowds stampede, those assumptions shatter first.
Why it matters: LTCM’s scientific halo sets up the later line that people are not Brownian particles.
Sources: Nobel Prize materials; standard textbook statements of BSM assumptions

Russia default: correlations → 1

In August 1998, Russia’s sovereign debt crisis and ruble turmoil ripped open the word “diversification.”⁴ Positions that looked uncorrelated in peacetime fell the same way in panic — correlations twisted toward one. LTCM was too large to exit cleanly: selling was part of the price.

What followed was a private rescue coordinated among major counterparties at the New York Fed — not mercy for genius, but a firebreak against cascading liquidations. The rehearsal ends with a cruel subtitle: when everyone needs liquidity at once, model “mean reversion” can go offline.

Section notes
⁴ Russia’s 1998 financial crisis In August 1998 Russia devalued the ruble and effectively defaulted on / restructured parts of its domestic debt (including GKOs), triggering violent selling across emerging markets and global risk assets. For funds that lived on financing and convergence trades, the hit was not only “Russia marks” but a simultaneous worsening of spreads, volatility, and funding — the correlation structure rewritten overnight.
Why it matters: the external trigger for LTCM’s collapse and a teaching case of macro shock as black swan for arb books.
Sources: IMF / public economic histories of the 1998 Russia crisis; LTCM literature cross-references

Models fail: people are not Brownian particles

The rehearsal closes on Newton’s almost proverbial sigh in finance: he could calculate the motions of heavenly bodies, but not the madness of people. Market “particles” imitate, panic, and rush the same exit — they are not independent Brownian noise.

The chalkboard lesson: history rhymes. 1998’s rhyme is about to sound again on a natural-gas futures blotter.

Equations treat people as integrable noise; crises restore them as animals who stampede. Curtain. Act two.

History rhymes: the never-fading amaranth

The flower’s name is Amaranth — a plant genus whose etymology carries “unfading.” In 2006, hedge fund Amaranth Advisors managed on the order of $9 billion and became a living irony⁵: a name that promised never to wilt, a book that withered in weeks.

For the author this is not distant gossip but live evidence that chaos is returning: gilded calm can be torn open by one flower’s fall.

Section notes
⁵ Amaranth Advisors / Brian Hunter / Katrina A multi-strategy hedge fund in Greenwich, Connecticut. Energy desk head Brian Hunter (Calgary) booked huge profits around Hurricane Katrina’s 2005 shock to natural gas; in 2006 the book doubled down on directional / calendar-spread bets (often described as March-versus-April spreads). In September 2006 the fund suffered multi-billion-dollar nat-gas derivative losses and large NAV drawdowns, transferring the energy book to parties including JPMorgan and Citadel to ease margin and default risk. The “never-fading flower” name and the ending glare at each other.
Why it matters: the chapter title and core event — dropping 1998’s rhyme onto 2006’s gas market.
Sources: contemporary financial press (WSJ / NGI et al.); public investor-letter paraphrases; later CFTC/FERC enforcement context

Hunter’s natural-gas heroics

The plot is pure Wall Street: first the 2005 hurricane-season windfall memory, then 2006 conviction that seasonal spreads would widen — with March–April often cited in the public telling. Positions stacked higher; when the market reversed, copycats fled while vultures pressed the squeeze. Margin calls became death warrants; exit itself became part of the price.

In the end the energy book was packaged to counterparties better able to digest the risk — public reports name JPMorgan and Citadel. Hero narrative meets forced sale; petals everywhere.

Seventeen hikes: lone leverage on the beach

The backdrop is the Fed’s long hiking scroll: from mid-2004, seventeen consecutive increases took the federal funds target from 1% to 5.25% by mid-2006⁶. Rising rates are a tide going out slowly; the first bodies visible on the sand are often those pinned to a single, highly leveraged bet.

Amaranth’s gas book became a sharp specimen of “who was swimming naked when the tide went out.”

Section notes
⁶ Federal funds path, 2004–2006 Beginning June 30, 2004, the FOMC raised its funds target by 25 bp seventeen times in a row, from 1.00% to 5.25% on June 29, 2006, then held that level for months. It is the textbook “measured tightening” stretch before the crisis: policy rates rose while risk appetite and shadow funding did not cool in lockstep.
Why it matters: explains how 2006 could still feel calm while cheap leverage’s fuel was already being drained.
Sources: Federal Reserve FOMC history; FRED target federal funds series

Vince’s survival style vs. hero narrative

On the desk the contrast is personal: Vince’s natural-gas-related style reads like survivalism — size control, respect for tails, “stay alive” as KPI number one. Hunter’s public image is heroic directional conviction: mythic in a tailwind, fatal to the whole “never-fading” flower in a gale.

The author pairs the two temperaments not for a sermon but for a question: in leveraged markets, are you trading a view — or betting the firm’s survival?

Survival craft rarely looks cool; hero narrative photographs best in a bull tape. When the tide ebbs, looking good and staying solvent are often different jobs.

Base money, multipliers, shadow leverage

Pull the camera back: what a central bank can twist most directly is base money and the policy rate; what really inflates in a boom is the credit multiplier of banks and the shadow system⁷. In repo markets, haircuts act like a lightly regulated leverage dial — loosen the haircut and the same collateral supports a larger notional⁸.

Worse is the lag: after hikes, risk appetite can stay euphoric for a while; liquidity does not feel cold overnight. Policy tightens while markets still add risk — until one lonely leveraged bet blows up and everyone admits the tide already left.

Section notes
⁷ Money multiplier In textbooks, broad money ≈ base money × multiplier; the multiplier captures how the banking system turns reserves into deposits and credit. Crisis research stresses that shadow banks, securitization, and off-balance-sheet pipes complicate the “effective” multiplier — credit can expand in repo and ABCP without sitting at the center of traditional deposit stats.
Why it matters: clarifies the crack between “the Fed is hiking” and “the Street still feels flush.”
Sources: money-and-banking texts; post-crisis discussions of shadow credit expansion
⁸ Shadow banking / repo and haircuts Repo finances positions with securities as collateral; a haircut is the discount between collateral value and cash lent. Lower haircuts mean higher leverage; rising haircuts force de-leveraging. Work by Gorton, Metrick, and others emphasizes that crisis “runs” often hit repo and wholesale funding that behaves like runnable deposits. A hedge fund’s invisible leverage frequently lives in these daily-rolled contracts.
Why it matters: links to the prior chapter’s DuPont/ROE leverage story and explains how one commodity book can drain a whole fund’s liquidity.
Sources: NY Fed repo explainers; Gorton & Metrick on the run on repo

Canary: the gilded age is ending

In the author’s telling, Amaranth is not merely an unlucky fund — it is a canary in the mine: a flower can die first in the gas market while larger cracks in credit and funding pipes still wait. 2006’s calm is not a safety certificate; it is the most deceptive scenery near the end of a gilded age.

Cyclical history stops sounding like nostalgia and starts sounding like a warning: linear optimism still says “never fading,” while petals are already on the floor.
