2006: a book idea at the desk

The idea of writing roughly began in 2006. Newly a trader, he met fresh scenes every day—exactly the Wall Street lore he had once wanted to understand. Why not string those experiences into a book for curious readers? ¹

Markets were still relatively calm then; the draft felt like an insider travelogue. He could not yet see that the next few years would force the travelogue into a crisis chronicle.

Crisis rewrites the narrative

Soon the subprime crisis tore the calm. “Unprecedented” events stacked up; the quiet market became a dangerous battlefield—retreats, counterattacks, tests. Trading forced analysis and decisions; long-foggy questions snapped into focus. The manuscript widened: not only Wall Street anecdotes, but market structure and cycle readings. ²

The aim sounds simple and is brutally hard: weave personal experience with pre-/post-crisis Wall Street into one complete story, yet keep mechanisms plain enough for outsiders. For a first-time author, that meant more than a year of “squeezing toothpaste” and reworking key nodes.

Crisis rewrote balance sheets—and the book the author meant to write.

The “chain stratagem” breakthrough

Writing was not only grind. Public trial reads recall a flash: the subprime section once refused to start; then a structured-products lecture by Professor Zhang Haiyun (UIBE) used a “chain stratagem” metaphor—suddenly clear. CDOs that locked subprime exposures together resembled the Romance of the Three Kingdoms stratagem that chained the ships; falling house prices were the “east wind”; fire along the line followed almost naturally. The “chain stratagem” section wrote itself in one breath. ³

That anecdote is the book’s method in miniature: hang unfamiliar finance on a familiar story skeleton—not to show off, but to make the thick thin.

The best finance metaphors are often old stories that suddenly lock onto new products.

Craft, technique, dao: thick to thin

The book aims for intuitive plainness. Behind that, a belief: under Wall Street’s dazzling stage sit a few through-lines—macro liquidity cycles, leverage versus hedging, the Street’s basic business model. Put a little philosophically: craft and technique can be complex; the dao should be simple. He does not claim enlightenment—only that years of deepening recognition left impressions he tried to put on the page. ⁴

Mathematician Hua Luogeng described reading as first “thin to thick” (accumulate), then “thick to thin” (grasp essence). His own Wall Street path felt similar—curiosity and mystique; then information-gathering and professional fragments without a system (thin→thick); then front-line practice plus watching a full pre-/post-crisis cycle, until fragments gathered into a macro map (thick→thin). He hopes readers leave with a little of that thinning. ⁵

Thickness is necessary inventory; thinness is the map you can carry.

The afterword calls these “dao”—footnote gives public coordinates, not private trading rules.

Source boundaries: media, diary, fiction

The afterword also draws borders: crisis material mainly from Bloomberg, the Wall Street Journal, and similar public reporting; some background from Wikipedia-class resources; his own trading diary as another spine. This is not an academic monograph, and time was tight—facts and figures were not footnoted one by one. ⁶

People and incidents around him were processed; they diverge from reality—so the book can still offer micro stories adapted from real patterns without exposing private lives or firm-sensitive detail.

An honest afterword first tells you which shell is story, which skeleton is checkable.

Thanks, common sense, next cycle

Friends made publication possible. Yang Yuxiang encouraged early drafts and wrote the preface “Respect Common Sense”—summarizing the book’s charm as respect for common sense and for regularities, an echo of the afterword’s claim that dao should be plain. ⁷ Others advised on structure; Heavenly Window and press editors labored. Family support was concrete: parents reading with reading glasses urged him to finish; his wife was first reader, praise and critique both useful; his son’s joy oddly raised efficiency.

Finally he thanks you for finishing patiently; welcomes corrections; and remains open to further exchange on markets.

Closing numbered chapters and afterword, hear the book’s undertone again: crisis lessons are not nostalgia. Public blurbs often warn that after easing and stimulus, new risks may already be seeded. §24 checked the retreat pontoon; the afterword hands it to readers—respect common sense, watch the next unite/divide. Chaos passes; chaos returns. ⁸

The afterword ends; trading does not. Common sense is the pontoon you keep.
