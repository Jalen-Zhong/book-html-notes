CHAPTER 1 · 2/3

On the relative-power chart, the thick lines over ~500 years often track four: Dutch, British, American, Chinese. Skeleton story: China led for centuries, then steep decline from the 1800s; small Netherlands became the reserve empire in the 1600s; the UK followed a similar path, peaking in the 1800s; the US rose over ~150 years, especially during/after WWII; now the US is in relative decline while China rises again. Stretch to ~600 CE and China is usually on top, with Middle Eastern caliphates, France, Mongols, Spain, and Ottomans also in the frame.¹

Eight gauges often averaged into total power: education, competitiveness, innovation/tech, output, world-trade share, military, financial-center strength, reserve-currency status. They typically rise as a chain — education → innovation → trade and military → output → financial center → (lagged) reserve currency — and fade in a similar order. A reserve currency, like a lingua franca, often outlasts the empire’s peak by habit.

Strengths arrive in bundles; weaknesses do too.

The Rise

After a new order: relatively low debts, narrower wealth/values/political gaps, cooperative prosperity, decent education and infrastructure, capable leadership, and a peace policed by one or more dominant powers. Key moves: strong education (including character and work ethic), openness to best practices, civil-military-government coordination, rising competitiveness and trade share, military protection of routes, income funding R&D.²

Capital markets are machine parts, not trivia: the Dutch invented the first publicly listed company (VOC) and a stock market to fund global trade; at their peak they accounted for roughly a quarter of major world inventions. Financial centers migrate: Amsterdam → London → New York, with Shanghai rising fast. When trade clears in the currency and savers want to hold it, reserve status grants cheaper borrowing. Today’s rhyme in the book: US and China are already roughly comparable in output and world-trade share.

The Top

Rewards hide seeds of decline. Labor grows expensive; rivals copy methods — classic lesson: British shipbuilders hired Dutch designers and used cheaper British workers to build better ships, lifting Britain and lowering the Dutch. Wealth brings leisure and refinement; generations slide from builders to inheritors. People lever good times into bubbles; wealth gaps self-reinforce as the rich shape politics and education — “haves” vs “have-nots.” Gaps may not explode while living standards still rise for most.³

Reserve-currency “exorbitant privilege” (the book traces the phrase to French finance minister Giscard d’Estaing) lets the empire borrow more — looking stronger near-term, weaker long-term. When the cost of empire exceeds its take, empire becomes a loss-maker: the British Empire grew massive and bureaucratic as rivals — especially Germany — forced costly arms races and world war. Rich countries borrowing from poorer, higher-saving ones is an early tell — the US from the 1980s, with per-capita income ~40× China’s, began borrowing from Chinese savers who wanted dollars because the dollar was the reserve currency.

The Decline

Decline usually comes from internal economic weakness plus fighting, costly external fighting, or both — gradually, then suddenly. When debts are huge and borrowing cannot roll, printing usually beats hard default: devaluation and inflation follow. Fiscal stress plus gaps fuels left/right populism; capitalism and elites get blamed; taxes and capital flight reinforce each other; capital controls can spark panic.⁴

Productivity falls, the pie shrinks, strongmen promise order — democracy is most stressed here. Civil war/revolution forges a new internal order: Roosevelt-style redistribution was relatively peaceful; 1930s order changes in Germany, Japan, Spain, Russia, and China were far more violent. Only when internal disorder lines up with a capable external challenger can the world order itself flip. Rivals exploit weakness; military bills arrive when finances are worst. Without a world referee, disputes end as tests of power. When debt, civil conflict, foreign war, and lost faith in the currency align, a world-order change is typically at the door.

Building eras often run ~40–80 years; destruction/restructuring ~10–20 — with wide variance.
