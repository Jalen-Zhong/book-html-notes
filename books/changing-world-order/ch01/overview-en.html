CHAPTER 1 · 1/3

Chapter 1 crushes the Dutch, British, and American reserve empires — plus Germany, France, Russia, India, Japan, China, and Chinese dynasties back to the Tang (~600 CE) — into a usable archetype. The point is a measuring stick for where we are in the cycle, not a museum tour.¹

He calls the research audacious, humbling, necessary, and fascinating: archives, scholars, practitioners, drafts, stress tests, redrafts until diminishing returns. He will not swear decimal precision; “mostly right” is enough to put once-in-a-lifetime, historically recurrent events on the radar.

An archetype is an average path; deviations are the doorway to “why this time differs.”

The timeless fight: make, take, distribute

Across time, the hardest plotline is making, taking, and distributing wealth and power; ideology and religion often intertwine. Those with wealth typically own the means of producing it and ally with those who hold political power to set and enforce rules. Class costumes change — landed nobles (when agricultural land was the main wealth source) to capitalists and elected or authoritarian officials — but the cooperate-and-compete structure rhymes.²

The usual movie: a tiny share of people control outsized wealth and power → overreach → hard times hit the least powerful first → conflict becomes revolution/civil war → a new order → the cycle restarts. Not a slogan — Dutch, British, US, and Chinese dynastic runs replay the same film in new costumes.

Evolution, cycles, and bumps

Evolution is the only lasting force: adaptation and learning lift living standards; cycles swing around that path — peace/war, boom/bust, left/right, cohesion/fragmentation. Push past equilibrium and the opposite overshoots. Fear, greed, and jealousy barely change, so cycle shapes stay family-similar.³

Productivity is the main engine of rising wealth and longevity. Long-run GDP-per-person and life-expectancy charts in the book show the trend slope dwarfing the wiggles. The steepening traces to Europe’s Gutenberg press (mid-15th century; printing already older in China), the Renaissance, Scientific Revolution, Enlightenment, capitalism, and Britain’s First Industrial Revolution. Wealth shifted from land to machines and increasingly to data and compute — so fights shift toward who owns data and how it becomes power.

Late-19th-century productivity, entrepreneurship, and capitalism also minted huge wealth gaps and over-indebtedness that fed early-20th-century anti-capitalism, communism, and great conflicts — evolution still climbing while the Big Cycle whipped hard beside it.

Two “small” swings: 1930–45 and 1907–19

On long productivity charts, turbulence looks like a wiggle; to people living it, the sky falls. The book’s 1930–45 case: US stocks down ~85%, economic activity ~10%, then recovery — classic money/credit: too much debt → credit collapse → big government spending/borrowing → central-bank printing. The Roaring-’20s bubble popped in 1929; wealth gaps ignited; countries experimented with redistribution under different systems; rising Germany and Japan challenged Britain and France and pulled the US into WWII. A timing aside: from crash to printing took ~two months in 2008 and only weeks in 2020.⁴

An earlier rhyme: the 1907 US Panic ended a Gilded-Age boom (Belle Époque / late Victorian echoes), with debt bust, redistribution, world war; Spanish flu intensified the stress test; the 1919 Treaty of Versailles declared a new order that set up the 1920s debt boom and the next 1930–45 run.

Destruction/restructuring economic stretches often last roughly two–three years; war and disaster pain ease as people adapt. All three mega-crises — economic, revolution/war, natural disaster — rarely arrive together. “Greatest Generation” stories show hardship forging character; most people stay employed, unharmed in shooting wars, and survive disasters — not to deny suffering, but to trust adaptability more than the bad stuff thrown at us.
