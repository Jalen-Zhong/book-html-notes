CHAPTER 9 · 3/3

Top: good life, lost edge, Anglo-Dutch rivalry

After the Golden Age, attention shifts to living well; wages run above continental peers; VOC is slow on new products like tea; education/tech edge erodes; Industrial Revolution elevates Britain. A vast empire costs too much for a small nation. Naval chain: First Anglo-Dutch War (1652–54, English Navigation Act forcing colonial cargoes onto English ships), Dano-Swedish War, Second/Third Anglo-Dutch and Franco-Dutch wars, then the decisive Fourth.¹

1688 Glorious Revolution helps short-run (William III of Orange on England’s throne) but second-order effects move merchants/capital to London and re-fragment Dutch provinces; when William dies heirless the dynastic link snaps.

Decline: run and reserve handover

From ~1750 Britain and France are stronger; Dutch more indebted, internally conflicted, militarily weak. As foreign earnings fall, rich savers buy British assets (including East India Company stock and London annuities) — yet guilder lags via trade inertia: ~40% of global trade still settles in Amsterdam in bank guilders. Dutch arms sales to American revolutionaries invite British retaliation in the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War (1780–84): 1781 Caribbean blow takes half the ships and key routes; VOC borrows heavily from Bank of Amsterdam.²

Bank balance sheet ~20M bank guilders at war start; wartime loans expand it ~50% — printing paper claims → inadequate metal → bank run. By 1795 deposits trade ~25% below coin; at closure metal covers ~20% of full backing (~80% devaluation). Guilder loses store-of-wealth credibility; pound takes over. Same year: French client state; VOC nationalized, equity wiped.

Reserve status lags other power gauges — until war and a run flip it overnight.
