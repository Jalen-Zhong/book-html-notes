CHAPTER 2 ADDENDUM · B

The multigenerational psychological cycle pairs being poor/rich with feeling poor/rich into five stages — a behavioral read on countries and leaders. Named anchors: from ~40 years ago until ~10–15 years ago, the Asian “Tigers” (Hong Kong, Singapore, Taiwan, South Korea) and then China walked the high-productivity path from poor-feeling-poor to rich-feeling-poor; the US ~1950–1965 sat in rich-feeling-rich “peak health”; China is moving into that stage.¹

  1. Poor and feel poor: low incomes, cash-precious, little credit; culture and capability sort who exits “early emerging” — often about a generation.
  2. Rich but feel poor: still hardworking exporters, high savers, efficient real investment plus reserve assets, heavy education/infrastructure — the most productive stage (“late emerging”); gleaming new cities beside old ones are a visual tell.
  3. Rich and feel rich: high wages but prior capex still pays; priorities shift from precautionary saving to savoring; arts/sciences flourish; stats like a six-to-five-day workweek appear; large countries become economic-military powers (“peak health”). Before mid-20th century, empires grabbed cheap labor and resources abroad; afterward US influence often meant “speak softly and carry a big stick.”
  4. Poorer yet feel rich: debts rise vs income; lifestyle inertia > competitive reality; military spend and twin deficits common; late bubbles (“early declining”).
  5. Poor and feel poor: bust and deleveraging; private shrink, public expand, CB printing; weak currency, falling global weight (“clearly declined”). Return to old peaks is slow: Romans and Greeks never did; the Chinese have several times.
Stage tags are behavior predictors, not moral scores.

Inventiveness, Magna Carta, and 1789

Human problem-solving has outrun problems in aggregate; net knowledge makes the path drift up, with Renaissance-like spurts on Big Cycle upswings. Working impression: innovation + commercial spirit + living capital markets → productivity jumps → wealth and power.²

Class struggle is not soundtrack: almost all recorded societies saw a tiny share control most wealth and power. From the 13th–19th centuries a common order was monarchy + landowning nobles + military; workers were treated as part of the means of production. In England from ~1200, King John wanted more tax, nobles wanted to pay less; civil war followed; nobles won and a “council” became Parliament’s ancestor; the 1215 Magna Carta was written — then fighting over money and power resumed for decades. In France’s ancien régime, clergy and nobility were ~2% of the population yet long outvoted the commoners (~98%) until the Revolution beginning 5 May 1789 overnight. Peaceful reforms tend to come earlier in the cycle; violent revolutions later — logically, when the pie is smaller and trust thinner.

Aristotle’s sting still lands: rich and poor quarrel, and victors often treat supremacy as loot rather than just government. The US imagines itself a melting pot where class labels matter least — yet they still spike under stress tests.
