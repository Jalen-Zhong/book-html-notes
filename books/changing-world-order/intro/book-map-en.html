INTRODUCTION · 3/3

To see the three forces together he zoomed to ~500 years: closest focus on the US, UK, and Dutch reserve-currency empires; secondary focus on Germany, France, Russia, Japan, China, and India; and for China, back to ~600 CE for many dynastic runs. Roughly, great empires last ~250 years (±150) with 50–100-year economic/debt/political subcycles.¹

Averages hide births and deaths — like watching an index without the constituents’ life cycles. Seeing the mega-macro picture sometimes means releasing precision. Even wording stays deliberately loose: countries, kingdoms, and empires are not equivalent around the 17th century, yet he still says “countries” so definition fights do not block pattern recognition.²

Precision is a magnifying glass; pattern recognition needs a step-back telescope.

What he does not know dwarfs what he knows

He feels like an ant studying the universe: more questions than answers. Privilege helps — triangulating with top historians and people who make history, plus Bridgewater’s data machine across major countries and markets. Still, nothing is certain; waiting for satisfaction before acting would strand the learning. Treat the outlook as theory, not fact — and remember diversification above all, because he has been wrong more times than he can count.³

Motive shifted too: silently compounding matters less than passing along a digestible 500-year story of how history rhymes with today, hoping for better shared decisions.

Part III asks what it means for the future. Shorter video versions online; denser charts at economicprinciples.org.⁴
