INTRODUCTION · 1/3

The Introduction opens like an alert: the times ahead will look radically unlike our lived experience, yet similar to many stretches of history. Why say so? Because they always have — for ~50 years, fiduciary duty forced him to learn what makes countries and markets succeed or fail, and to anticipate shocks he had never faced by studying analogous cases and extracting workable principles.¹

He saw forces rare in his lifetime but recurrent in history converge: huge debts and near-zero rates in the three major reserve currencies (dollar, euro, yen), followed by massive money printing; US domestic wealth, political, and values gaps near century highs; and a rising power (China) challenging the incumbent (the US) and the existing order. The closest rhyme: 1930–1945. That worry drove this study.

Mid-study, COVID-19 hit — another once-in-a-lifetime, historically recurrent shock. Folding past pandemics, famines, and floods into the sample showed that rare acts of nature can, by any measure, out-impact even the biggest depressions and wars.

Shocks you have never lived are often shocks history has lived many times — in new clothes.

The Big Cycle: peaceful creation vs destructive reset

History, to him, behaves like an organism’s life cycle: generations hand down assets, liabilities, values, and scars. Empire “powers” — education, productivity, trade, military, currency, markets — cycle and reinforce each other. A successful empire/dynasty may last roughly 200–300 years with readable stage markers.²

The Big Cycle swings between (1) peaceful, creative prosperity that lifts living standards and (2) depression, revolution, and war that destroy what people cherish. Peaceful stretches usually outlast destructive ones (~5:1). Destruction acts like a cleansing storm: it clears weakness and excess (e.g., too much debt) and, painfully, resets fundamentals — then a new monetary, economic, and political bargain: a new world order. Nested inside: ~100-year long debt cycles and ~8-year short ones.

When cycles align, tectonic plates of history shift. Most people still expect a mildly edited recent past — because true boom and bust often arrive about once per lifetime. He notes: it was a rare country in a rare century that had only boom/harmony and never depression/civil war/revolution; expect both.

Swings to extremes are normal; linear extrapolation of the recent past is the rare psychology.

Hard money, soft money, and generational bias

His father’s generation — Depression and WWII — could barely imagine the postwar boom and avoided leveraged speculation. Later cohorts who only knew debt-financed booms treated depression and war as implausible. Money shifted too: relatively “hard” after WWII until the 1970s soft fiat turn to accommodate borrowing and prevent mass default. At the time of writing, many still preferred more borrowing — forgetting how debt booms historically led into busts and conflict.³

Dollar reserve status, stimulative monetary policy, and the prestige of democracy-plus-capitalism felt like defaults in his lifetime. History’s reminder: no system, currency, or empire lasts forever; those who ignore succession often get ruined by it. His job is to preserve wealth in any environment — so he needed a strategy that would have worked through devastating stretches too.
