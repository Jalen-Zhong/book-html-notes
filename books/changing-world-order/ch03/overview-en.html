CHAPTER 3 · 1/3

Chapter 3’s claim: what people and countries want most is wealth and power, and money and credit are among the biggest drivers of their rise and decline. Miss money and credit, and you miss the system — and what is coming at you.¹

Classic chain: Roaring-’20s-style debt bubble → 1930–33 Depression → global fights over the pie → FDR elected 1932, then post-inauguration government+Fed flood of money and credit — akin to peers then and to pandemic-era responses. Without that chain you misread 1933, WWII’s settlement, and the 1945 order; with it, today is more readable. Talking with top historians and current/former heads of state, finance ministers, and central bankers, he found historians lacked the money piece and he lacked the political piece — this chapter supplies the money piece.

Historians often lack the money piece; investors lack the political piece. Ch.3 supplies the money piece.

Income-statement universe

People, firms, nonprofits, and governments share the same arithmetic: income, spending, net income; assets, liabilities, net worth. Overspend and you eat savings or borrow; if income cannot cover ops + debt service, cut, default, or restructure. Sum the books and you get the economy: spending is someone’s income; debt is someone’s asset — austerity and default contagion politicize who gets the shrunken pie.²

Principle: debt eats equity — creditors rank first (miss the mortgage and the house serves the lender first). Looking cash-rich can still mean insolvency if income < expenses and liabilities > assets. Exception: central banks can create money and credit to “feed debt,” so the upgraded line is: debt eats equity, but central banks can print to feed debt. At writing: many people, firms, and governments “look rich” — big spending, plenty of assets, even cash — yet incomes below expenses and liabilities above assets, so projections force spending cuts and asset sales.

Reserve currency: privilege and seeds

Not all printing is equal. Widely accepted reserve currencies (USD-dominant at writing; euro next; yen, RMB, sterling smaller, RMB rising) grant exceptional borrow-and-spend power and geopolitical reach. Privilege also plants endings: overborrowing and printing to service debt cheapen the currency and can cost reserve status — every reserve currency in history has lost the role, often traumatically.³

Non-reserve countries with heavy FX debt and insufficient FX earnings can break in a run. Households and localities face the same liabilities-vs-cashable-assets bind — without a printing press. Central banks can also set rates near 0% and roll debt forever so debtors “never pay back” — still booked as lending, economically near a grant; the cost falls on holders of cash and bonds whose purchasing power is diluted. Post-pandemic versions rhyme with many historical ones.
