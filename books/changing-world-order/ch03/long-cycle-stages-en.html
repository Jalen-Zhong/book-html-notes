CHAPTER 3 · 3/3

The long-term debt cycle’s archetypal six stages (not metronomic, but family-similar):

  1. Hard-money start: debts cleaned by restructuring/monetization; trust thin; money returns to gold/silver-type anchors. Examples: after Weimar’s debt-and-money destruction, money was again backed by gold-denominated assets and land and pegged to the dollar; after Argentina’s late-1980s destruction, money linked to the dollar.
  2. Claims on hard money: notes promise convertibility; banks (and temples in China, etc.) vault metal and issue claims — a “linked currency” system.
  3. Debt expansion: claims are lent; banks lend onward at higher rates; assets and output boom; claims soar vs hard money and goods, eventually far exceeding hard money in the vault.
  4. Crisis, default, devaluation: income cannot service debt, or claims cannot convert as expected → a run; defending the hard link means deflationary depression, so links break, printing and devaluation follow — a “beautiful deleveraging” prints just enough. Private banks cannot print — they default or get bailed out; central banks whose debts are in their own currency can pay back less (e.g., 50–70%); foreign-currency debts can still force default.¹
  5. Fiat and debasement: pure fiat frees printing from metal stocks. Modern classroom: on the evening of 15 August 1971 Nixon announced on TV that the dollar would no longer convert to gold — “guns and butter” spending had piled claims far beyond the vault; astute holders ran first; Dalio lived “default intuition vs equity rally.” Later 2008 (rates already at 0%) and 2020 COVID (Fed plan announced 9 April) again monetized at scale. Four levers: austerity, default/restructuring, redistribution/taxes, print-and-devalue — printing is usually the political path of least resistance because victims (holders of money and debt) are opaque.²
  6. Flight back to hard assets: overprinting sparks selling of debt and money into gold, silver, inflation-hardy equities, foreign assets; Weimar Germany even saw flight into construction rocks. Governments often ban gold, limit FX, impose capital controls. Then often a re-link to hard money or a foreign currency (many weak-currency countries dollarize) to rebuild trust.
Holding debt is like carrying a ticking bomb that pays you while it ticks and explodes when it stops — roughly every 50–75 years; the biblical Jubilee every 50 years calendarized forgiveness.

Three monetary systems and the full loop

Across millennia: (1) hard money (metal coins); (2) paper claims; (3) fiat. Need more money/credit and systems move 1→2→3; overprinting drives a flight back to hard assets and foreign currencies. From China’s Song Dynasty to Weimar Germany, the book names full transitions from constrained systems into fiat and back after hyperinflation.³

Inside each regime there are usually 2–4 big debt crises — bank crises, write-downs, or 30%+ devaluations — that need not smash the whole currency system. The sharp restructuring stretch often lasts months to ~three years (depending on how fast governments act); ripples can last longer — e.g., loss of reserve status. The next chapter digs into why money’s value changes and the risks that follow.
