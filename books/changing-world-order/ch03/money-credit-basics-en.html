CHAPTER 3 · 2/3

Money is a medium of exchange and can be a store of wealth. Barter is inefficient, so societies invent portable, mutually trusted money; people also store buying power in gold, silver, gems, art, property, stocks, bonds. Crucial split: money settles claims; debt is a promise to deliver money.¹

When credit works, borrowers use funds productively, lenders earn interest, society gains; while outstanding, debt is the lender’s asset and borrower’s liability, then clears. Most modern government money has no intrinsic value — journal entries — and the OS that allocates resources periodically breaks, bringing monetization, cheaper currency, and big wealth shifts. Upswings mint joyful abundance; downswings mint painful restructuring.

Financial and real economies are related, not identical: a higher price does not make the thing better.

Financial vs real

Money/credit creation and production of goods, services, and investment assets often reinforce each other and get confused. The real side hits capacity limits; when demand overheats inflation, central banks usually tighten; when demand is weak, they ease. Imperfect control yields the short debt/business cycle of roughly eight years (± a few).²

Money/credit has its own supply-demand: create too much vs demand and the currency falls. If liquidity skips real lending and floods FX and inflation hedges, you can get monetary inflation with real-economy deflation. In crises, governments may temporarily replace markets in allocating buying power — capitalism’s pause button; much of the world did so in the pandemic.

Rising asset marks often create an illusion of richer: same house, different measuring stick and leverage. Money/credit stimulates when issued and depresses when repaid — the cycle’s core. Think of the central bank as holding a bottle of stimulant: inject when weak, taper when hot; limits appear when stimulus no longer lifts real activity.

Short cycles and long cycles

Everyone has lived short debt cycles and often wrongly assumes they will run forever. Long debt cycles typically span ~50–75 years (containing about 6–10 short cycles) and rarely fit one life, so people ignore them until they blow. The present long-cycle frame traces to 1944 Bretton Woods design and the 1945 postwar dollar order.³

Late-cycle picture: debts already high, rates hard to cut further, printing lifts financial assets more than real activity; holders question whether money and debt remain good stores of wealth and flee to alternatives — then the monetary system needs restructuring. History rhymes: central banks and governments print to manufacture monetary inflation that offsets deflationary credit contractions.

Next leaf: six stages from hard money to fiat to the flight back.
