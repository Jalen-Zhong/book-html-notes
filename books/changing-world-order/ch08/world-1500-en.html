CHAPTER 8 · 2/3

1500: families, religion, inequality

No sovereign states as we know them: territories were family dynasties; large enough kingdoms were empires; inheritance and arranged marriage worked like family-firm succession. Religious leaders were far more powerful; science as we know it barely existed. Europe and China had almost no contact yet ran similarly — China’s institutions larger, more developed, less religious.¹

Egalitarian law was thin: inside and between kingdoms, arms ruled. Serfdom had largely faded in Western Europe by the 1300s–1400s, but commoners’ rights stayed weak until the Enlightenment (1700s). Family “firms” were not warm: Chinese dynasties and European courts alike fought succession to the death.

Empire map: Europe–Asia–Americas

Europe: Habsburgs held Spain, its colonies, and much of the Holy Roman Empire (parts of today’s Netherlands, Belgium, Italy, Germany, Austria) — the West’s top power; Valois/Bourbon rivaled them in France; Tudor England was rising but not yet first-tier; Florence, Venice, and Milan drove finance, commerce, and arts — Venice’s doge was chosen through layered lottery committees; Florence’s gold florin became hard money; Venice issued perpetuals (later defaulting after wars around 1500). Papal States and the Church extracted tithes and unpaid labor; Rurik/Romanov Russia was peripheral; Ottomans centered on Constantinople taken in 1453.

Asia: Ming China near its peak — the book cites Kennedy-scale magnitudes: China ~100–130M people in the 15th century vs Europe ~50–55M; canals, exams, navy, ~1M standing army. Neo-Confucian rationalism had helped medieval China lead. Ironically, after Yongle voyages expanded tributary ties, later pullbacks — ending voyages, closing doors, pleasure, eunuch/minister rule, pedantic scholarship — seeded relative decline vs Europe. India and Japan were fragmented; Mughal conquest begins in the 1520s.²

Middle East, Americas, Africa: Ottomans vs Safavid Persia; Aztec capital Tenochtitlán may have outsized any European city, Inca likewise strong — both soon shattered by European contact, planting seeds of what became the US; West Africa’s Songhai was a trade and scholarship hub.

The 1500 map was about to be redrawn by exploration, Reformation, and the invention of capitalism.
