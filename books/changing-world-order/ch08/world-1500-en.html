CHAPTER 8 · 2/3

1500: families, religion, inequality

No sovereign states as we know them: territories were family dynasties; large enough kingdoms were empires; inheritance and arranged marriage worked like family-firm succession. Religious leaders were far more powerful; science as we know it barely existed. Europe and China had almost no contact yet ran similarly — China’s institutions larger, more developed, less religious.¹

Egalitarian law was thin: inside and between kingdoms, arms ruled. Serfdom had largely faded in Western Europe by 1500, but commoners’ rights stayed weak until the Enlightenment.

Empire map

Europe: Habsburgs held Spain, its colonies, and much of the Holy Roman Empire — the West’s top power; Valois/Bourbon rivaled them in France; Tudor England was rising but not yet first-tier; Florence, Venice, and Milan drove finance, commerce, and arts; Papal States and the Church extracted tithes and unpaid labor from peasants; Rurik/Romanov Russia was peripheral; Ottomans centered on Constantinople.

Asia: Ming China near its peak — population, canals, exams, navy, ~1M standing army. Neo-Confucian rationalism had helped medieval China lead. Ironically, vast wealth/power helped later pullbacks: ending voyages, closing doors, pleasure, eunuch/minister rule, pedantic scholarship — seeds of relative decline vs Europe. India and Japan were fragmented, not yet great powers.²

Middle East, Americas, Africa: Ottomans vs Safavid Persia; Aztec and Inca capitals would soon be shattered by European contact; Africa’s Songhai was a trade and scholarship hub among many kingdoms.

The 1500 map was about to be redrawn by exploration, Reformation, and the invention of capitalism.
