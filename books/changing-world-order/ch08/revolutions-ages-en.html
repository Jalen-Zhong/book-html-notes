CHAPTER 8 · 3/3

Revolutions and “ages”: thinking and rules first

What remade history after 1500 were shifts in thinking and rules for sharing wealth and power. Commercial Revolution (Italian city-states, florin, Venetian perpetuals) → Renaissance (reason/evidence, print, Medici-style patronage — four Medici popes; later weak heads plus economic stress brought revolutions and repeated losses of Florence) → Exploration/colonialism: Henry the Navigator–style royal sponsorship, Iberian “sun never sets,” silver and the Price Revolution; after Zheng He, Ming/Japan turned inward; Portuguese reach China in 1513; Europe craves porcelain and silk while China has little reciprocal demand.¹

1517: Luther’s Ninety-Five Theses ignite the Reformation; France’s Wars of Religion kill an estimated ~3 million; the Thirty Years’ War looks Protestant-vs-Catholic on the surface but is a balance-of-power fight over wealth — ending in Westphalia (1648) inventing modern borders and sovereignty, after ~one-quarter of Europe’s population dies from combat, disease, or famine.

Capitalism, science, industry, revolutionary waves

The Dutch invent public equity and the Amsterdam exchange (1602); Bank of Amsterdam supports the guilder as the first non-metal global reserve currency. Bacon pushes scientific method; Copernicus/Galileo/Newton pry knowledge free of medieval locks; Enlightenment extends method into politics and economics. First Industrial Revolution lets Britain catch Dutch living standards ~1800 and approach ~20% of world output by mid-19th century. After Napoleonic Wars (1803–15), Vienna opens a British-led century of relative peace. Western gunboats trigger Meiji success and China’s Century of Humiliation. Second Industrial Revolution and the Gilded Age lift the US. Communism reacts to capitalist gaps and fuels 20th-century revolutions.²

Watch both lines: centuries of evolving thought, and peace/prosperity ↔ depression/war order resets. Next: the Dutch and the guilder.
