CHAPTER 5 · 2/4

① New order and consolidation

After civil war/revolution, winners must mop remaining opposition and fight among themselves — revolutions often come in two acts: topple old leaders, then purge and intra-winner struggle. “Consolidators” must still win at all costs, yet be more politically acute because enemies are less visible. Purge severity tracks conflict depth, fights among new leaders, and maturity of inherited bureaucracies.¹

② Resource systems and bureaucracy

After tearing down the old order, design systems so people row the same way: rules, law, allocation that lifts productivity for most. Best leaders are “civil engineers” — Adenauer, Lee Kuan Yew, Deng versus Churchill/Mao-style “inspirational generals.” Leaders who carry 6→1→2 at scale are rare: Tang Taizong, Augustus, Genghis Khan as yardsticks (including succession-failure warnings).

③ Peace and prosperity (sweet spot)

Broad opportunity, collaboration, high productivity, generational improvement, optimism; wide education and merit placement; entrepreneurs as heroes; debt still funding productivity; income>spending, savings>liabilities. Gladstone, Bismarck, Kennedy’s short agenda, Deng’s pivot illustrate “staying in 3.” Seeds of risk: opportunity/income/wealth/values gaps, elite luxury, productivity slip, excess debt — fail and slide to Stage 4.²

Stage 3’s temptation is believing good times refill themselves.
Section notes
¹ Two-act revolutions Toppling vs purge/infighting need different skill sets; conflating them misreads “the revolution is over.”
Why it matters：Explains post-victory turbulence.
