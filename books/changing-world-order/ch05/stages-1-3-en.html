CHAPTER 5 · 2/4

① New order and consolidation

After civil war/revolution, winners must mop remaining opposition and fight among themselves — revolutions often come in two acts: topple old leaders, then purge and intra-winner struggle. “Consolidators” must still win at all costs, yet be more politically acute because enemies are less visible. Purge severity tracks conflict depth, fights among new leaders, and maturity of inherited bureaucracies.¹

Extreme poles: the French Revolution’s Reign of Terror, Russia’s Red Terror, China’s post-1949 Anti-Rightist Campaign — sometimes one purge, sometimes episodic decades later (Cultural Revolution ~17 years after the CCP took power). Milder poles: reconstruction after the US Civil War; the relatively peaceful Roosevelt “revolution” of the 1930s. Tang Taizong, Augustus, and later the US Founders, Napoleon, and Bismarck illustrate cutting from war to rebuild.

② Resource systems and bureaucracy

After tearing down the old order, design systems so people row the same way: rules, law, allocation that lifts productivity for most — especially a large middle class (Aristotle’s warning: thin middle, excessive poor → fragile state). Case spectrum: early US ~15 years after 1776; early Napoleonic years after the 1799 coup; early Meiji after 1868; late-1940s–1950s rebuild in Japan, Germany, and peers; post-civil-war China; Russia after the Soviet breakup.²

Best leaders are “civil engineers” — Adenauer, Lee Kuan Yew, Deng — versus Churchill/Mao-style “inspirational generals” (strong in Stages 6–1, weak at administrative build). Leaders who carry 6→1→2 at scale are rare: Tang Taizong (~century-and-a-half of Tang flourishing), Augustus (~200 years of relative peace), Genghis Khan (Mongol peak for over a century, but succession failure sparked civil wars soon after).

③ Peace and prosperity (sweet spot)

Broad opportunity, collaboration, high productivity, generational improvement, optimism; wide education and merit placement; entrepreneurs as heroes; debt still funding productivity; income>spending, savings>liabilities. Named stretches: Victorian Britain (Second Industrial Revolution), late-1800s German Empire industrialization, 1960s US (the moon shot as shared mission). Gladstone as “the People’s William” with tight budgets, Bismarck uniting ~39 states and faiths, Lee Kuan Yew as PM 1959–90 shaping culture while avoiding wars, Kennedy’s short moon/civil-rights/poverty agenda with containment, Deng’s “glorious to be rich” / “black cat, white cat” pivot — all “stay in Stage 3” refs.³

Seeds of risk: opportunity/income/wealth/values gaps, elite luxury, productivity slip, excess debt — fail and slide to Stage 4. Empires that stayed long in Stage 3 did so by actively dodging those seeds.

Stage 3’s temptation is believing good times refill themselves.
