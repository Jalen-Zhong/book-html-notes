CHAPTER 6 · 1/3

Chapter 6: internal and external orders rhyme and blend — clear mutually recognized country borders are a late invention. Lawyer–judge–police logic fails between states because supranational bodies rarely out-wealth or out-power the strongest countries. League and UN rule-making mostly failed: if the US, China, or others have more power than the UN, they decide how things go.¹

Five fight types often stack: trade/economic, technology, capital (sanctions/credit cuts), geopolitical, military — cyber cuts across. After survival, wealth and power still drive. All-out wars usually require existential stakes — worth dying for, beyond bargaining. Europe’s 1500–now peace/conflict death cycles: long prosperities (Renaissance, Enlightenment, Industrial) then catastrophic hot wars. Two things one can be most sure of about war: (1) it won’t go as planned; (2) it will be worse than imagined.

Win–win collaboration that grows and shares wealth is cheaper than lose–lose escalation — yet slides into stupid wars are easy.

Forces and principles that rewrite external order

Conflict rises when the dominant power weakens or a rising power nears (or both). Highest military-war risk: roughly comparable force + irreconcilable existential differences (at writing, US–China over Taiwan flagged as a potential flashpoint). Fighting and backing down both cost; prisoner’s dilemma, tit-for-tat, domestic “can’t look weak,” plus emotional propaganda, breed stupid wars.²

Principles in brief: have power, respect power, use power wisely; decide when to deal vs fight; negotiate seeing through the adversary’s eyes and trading what each side truly values; exit lose–lose via separation before hot war when possible; unused needed power still consumes resources, and flashing power often forces rivals to arm — power is usually best as a hidden knife, except when showing it improves the bargain.
