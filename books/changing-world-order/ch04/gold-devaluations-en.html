CHAPTER 4 · 2/3

Versus gold: spot FX of major reserve currencies against gold can be drawn since 1600. Devaluations tend to hit abruptly in debt crises, separated by longer calm — not a smooth grind.¹

Comparing “hold currency (interest-bearing cash/bills)” to “hold gold” requires counting interest. Across ~170 years, six big windows stand out:

  1. 1860s US Civil War: suspend gold convertibility; print “greenbacks” to monetize war debt.
  2. 1870s gold-standard expansion: most currencies relatively stable vs gold (US returned mid-decade); Japan stayed silver-linked into the 1890s and cheapened vs gold as silver fell; Spain often suspended convertibility for fiscal deficits.
  3. WWI: huge deficits funded by CB printing/lending; gold still hard money in international trust gaps. 1919–22 forced prints especially among losers — Weimar mark and mark-debt wiped 1920–23; some winners also had to devalue to restart.
  4. 1930s: post-1929 global debt crisis → print and devalue (US 1933 among them); WWII war debts again; after the war Germany, Japan, Italy, and China saw rapid wipeouts while UK/France among winners still depreciated deeply but more slowly.
  5. 1944–45 Bretton Woods: dollar tied to gold; others to the dollar — lasting into the late 1960s.
  6. 1968–73 (esp. 1971-08-15 Nixon shock): US overspending/debt made gold claims far exceed redeemable gold → fiat dollar era, credit boom and 1970s inflation; after 1980–81 rates far above inflation, currencies strengthened and gold fell until ~2000; then more money/credit and low/negative real rates again cheapened money vs gold — more gradual under floating FX than past cliff breaks.²
In gold-standard booms, holding interest cash often earned positive real returns; in the fiat era the cash-bills real-return center of gravity worsened.

Boom stretch, panics, and the fiat center

1850–1913 roughly tracks the Second Industrial Revolution: most countries kept metal convertibility; lending funded earnings that serviced high rates, so holding currency vs gold was generally profitable — turbulence still hit: a US debt-financed equity boom fed the 1907 Panic amid rising wealth gaps and suffrage/union fights; the Fed and federal income tax arrived in 1913. A world away, China’s rubber-stock bubble (rhyming with 19th-century US railroad bubbles) burst in 1910, sometimes cited in the debt/money/economic downswing that accompanied the end of Imperial China.³

Public rough table (vs CPI, annualized): 1850–present interest cash ~1.2%, gold ~0.9%; about half of countries positive on bills, half negative — Germany shows total wipeouts twice. Since ~1912 (modern fiat): bills ~−0.1%, gold ~+1.6%; France/Italy/Japan worse than ~−2%/yr, Germany ~−18%/yr from hyperinflation. Remember the pattern, not the table: big devaluations are pulsed; war and debt crises are frequent triggers; “earning interest” does not automatically protect buying power.
