§4 · Versus gold: episodic big devaluations

CHAPTER 4 · 2/3

Versus gold: spot FX of major reserve currencies against gold can be drawn since 1600. Devaluations tend to hit abruptly in debt crises, separated by longer calm — not a smooth grind.¹

Comparing “hold currency (interest-bearing cash/bills)” to “hold gold” requires counting interest. Across ~170 years, six big windows stand out:

  1. 1860s US Civil War: suspend gold convertibility; print “greenbacks” to monetize war debt.
  2. 1870s gold-standard expansion: most currencies relatively stable vs gold (US returned mid-decade).
  3. WWI: huge deficits funded by CB printing/lending; 1919–22 forced prints especially among losers — Weimar as the extreme.
  4. 1930s: post-1929 global debt crisis → print and devalue (US 1933 among them).
  5. 1944–45 Bretton Woods: dollar tied to gold; others to the dollar.
  6. 1968–73 (esp. 1971): US overspending/debt breaks the gold link → fiat dollar era, credit boom and 1970s inflation; since 2000, more money/credit and low real rates again cheapen money vs gold.²
In gold-standard booms, holding interest cash often earned positive real returns; in the fiat era the cash-bills real-return center of gravity worsened.

Rough magnitudes (public framing)

1850–present: interest-earning cash ~1.2% average annual real return, a bit above gold’s average real return; about half of countries positive on bills, half negative. Most positives came in gold-standard prosperities. Since ~1912 (modern fiat): bills ~−0.1% real, gold ~+1.6% — few countries steadily beat inflation by holding interest cash.³

Remember the pattern, not the table: big devaluations are pulsed; war and debt crises are frequent triggers; “earning interest” does not automatically protect buying power.
