CHAPTER 4 · 3/3

Is gold the right yardstick? Also watch money versus goods and services (CPI baskets). Public charts show the World Wars were brutal for currency holders, with ~decade-scale swings later; about half of interest-earning cash currencies beat inflation, half did not — holding bills as a store of wealth late in debt cycles has historically been very risky.¹

From 1850 to 1971, gold’s appreciation roughly matched average inflation losses (with big cross-country gaps: Germany saw large gold outperformance; limited-devaluation countries like the US often saw gold lag). After WWII gold held steady in most places while money/credit expanded until the 1971 Type-2→Type-3 fiat shift; unconstrained printing lifted inflation and crushed real rates, so real gold rose into ~1980–81, then high rates pushed it down until ~2000, when rates could not fall further by normal means and printing/buying financial assets again supported gold.

Devaluation ≠ instant loss of reserve status

Cheapening a currency and losing reserve status are related but not identical; both often ride debt crises. Losing reserve status is slower and more political: holders reallocate systemically when economic and political primacy has already shifted to a rival and a credible alternative exists. The shared mechanic is a classic “run”: claims on the central bank’s hard currency exceed what is available — usually gold, though for the UK’s reserve decline the hard unit was the dollar the pound was tied to.²

Net CB reserves often fall before the official devaluation (sometimes years ahead); convertibility may suspend first — the UK in 1947 ahead of the 1949 devaluation; the US in 1971. Runs and devaluations often stack with wartime spending: the Dutch Fourth Anglo-Dutch War, the UK’s world wars, US Vietnam pressure. CBs first lift short rates, then capitulate into printing when the real economy hurts too much; after devaluation they typically cut rates.

Outcomes diverge with remaining economic and military power: the Dutch guilder’s circulation collapsed relatively fast around the end of the Fourth Anglo-Dutch War in 1784, as the Netherlands lost to Britain and later faced French invasion; sterling took multiple devaluations and balance-of-payments strains before fully losing reserve status after Bretton Woods — many holders stayed for political pressure and underperformed US assets; the US saw abrupt 1933/1971 moves and more gradual post-2000 cheapening vs gold without (at writing) losing the reserve role — the self-reinforcing run becomes hard to stop when “rival ready + primacy already lost + CB-monetized giant debts” align.³

Reserve status dies when “rival ready + primacy already lost,” not from one devaluation headline alone.
