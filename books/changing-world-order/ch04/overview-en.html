CHAPTER 4 · 1/3

Chapter 4 grounds Chapter 3’s money-credit frame in cases: the financial economy has its own supply-demand, and currency moves are a core debt-cycle part. The aim is to see how money cheapens versus debt, gold, and goods baskets when printing aims to lighten debt — and when reserve status is actually lost.¹

Of ~750 currencies since 1700, only about 20% still exist — and all have been devalued. Go back to 1850: gulden/thaler in German lands, koban/ryo in Japan, multiple Italian coins, different Spanish and Chinese monies — some wiped after hyperinflation or lost wars, some merged (euro), some surviving but deeply cheaper (sterling, dollar).

Asset marks are visible waves; currency value is the tide underneath.

Devalue against what? First, debt

Printing’s primary goal is lightening debt burdens: raise money relative to debt so debtors can repay. Debt is a promise to deliver money; more money for those who need it softens the promise. Where newly created money and credit flow decides the sequel: into productivity and profits, and real (inflation-adjusted) equity can rise; if it sufficiently hurts actual and prospective returns on cash and debt, flows flee into gold, commodities, inflation-linked bonds, other currencies, and even digital assets — a self-reinforcing cheapening of money.²

When real rates (interest minus inflation) would rise enough to hurt the economy and anger the public, central banks nearly always choose the second path: print and buy cash/debt assets — reinforcing bad returns for holders. The later in the long debt cycle, the higher the odds of currency and monetary-system breakdown: debts and money already too large to become real goods/services; the real rate that saves debtors is below what creditors need to hold debt as a store of wealth; normal rate policy (MP1) and printing to buy high-quality debt (MP2) stop working, and monetary policy becomes a facilitator of uneconomic political allocation.

Some devaluations are systemically “useful” (needed for deleveraging, always costly to money/debt holders); others are wipeout hyperinflations — telling them apart is the reader’s and policymaker’s job.
