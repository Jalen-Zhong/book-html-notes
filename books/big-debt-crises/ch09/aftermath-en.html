5. Aftermath & normalization (2010–mid-2011)

Beautiful deleveraging is not an automatic painless exit. 2010–11 oscillated among a “sweet spot,” more-QE expectations, European debt spillovers, and domestic tightening debates. Declaring victory early remained dangerous; meanwhile the speed of recapitalization, exit from emergency tools, and financial returns on rescue programs became a management-quality scorecard.¹

Regulatory reform (consumer protection, systemic-risk council, and more) tried to pull parts of shadow banking and too-big-to-fail back under the umbrella—perfection impossible, directionally patching holes the post-1930s safety net never covered.²

The back half of deleveraging is a tug-of-war between political patience and monetary policy.

§9 close: classic deflationary big-debt crises can happen in modernity; speed and mix set ugly-phase length; beautiful paths can appear yet still need guardrails against premature tightening and external shocks. We do not start §10–§12 compendium/glossary/appendix here—the case trilogy (Weimar inflation, U.S. 1929, U.S. 2008) pauses at this station.³
