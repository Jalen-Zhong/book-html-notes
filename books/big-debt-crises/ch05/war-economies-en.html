§5 · WAR ECONOMIES · EN

War economies do not speak peacetime accounting. Rising munitions GDP may be destroyed in combat; conscription cuts unemployment; top-down allocation rewrites profits and industry mix; borrowing and capital flows follow different rules. Dalio says this deserves another book; here he flags that sampled big debt crises include war shadows, and another war period would require a new dashboard.¹

1. From economic conflict to military conflict

Domestically, rich/poor and left/right conflicts elevate populist, autocratic, nationalist, militarist leaders; internationally, rising and established powers of comparable strength clash — economics and politics intertwine, raising odds of disruptive conflict. Historians stress geopolitics; markets must add: wars reprice currencies, debts, equities, and the social contract.²

Postwar eras often bring long peace under dominant rule-setters until the next rise. Empire and reserve-currency arcs need ~250-year lenses.³

When rivalries heat, firebrand leaders and war risk rise together — not always destiny, but a classic pattern.

2. Cooperative-competitive vs mutually threatening

Two relationship types: (1) cooperative-competitive — respect what the other truly needs, trade for win-win, negotiate hard like merchants; (2) mutually threatening — trade painful acts to force fear and submission, war not negotiation. Either side can force lose-lose; win-win needs both. Relative power should sit in both minds: do not corner the other on the cooperative path; on the threat path, ability to endure pain ranks with ability to inflict it. When power is untested, cooperation is safer; war reveals dominance through hell.⁴

3. Wartime priority: resources and credit

Priority one: secure financial and real resources for the fight. No country funds both war and tolerable civilian life from current income alone — it must borrow or hold huge reserves. Borrowing capacity hinges on creditworthiness and capital-market depth, especially sound local-currency debt markets; critical materiel and labor matter equally (military spending and soldiers/population can jump ~5×; WWII US shifted ~20% of the workforce into the military).⁵

4. Postwar bills: never be indebted and lose

Winners and losers alike carry debt and must convert from war to civilian economies — military spending cliffs often cause postwar recessions; deleveraging mechanics rhyme with other depressions. Losers face deeper slumps, more printing, reserve drawdowns, higher inflation, sometimes hyperinflation. Dalio's all-caps warning: worst of all is heavy debt and losing a war — Weimar after WWI and Germany/Japan after WWII. Suggested color: Weimar and US Depression cases; winners' US/UK postwar charts; losers' stats often missing because devastation broke measurement.⁶
