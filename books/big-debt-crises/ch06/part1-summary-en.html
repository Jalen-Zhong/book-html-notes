§6 · PART 1 SUMMARY · EN

Part 1 closes on a headline: managing debt crises is about spreading the pain of bad debts — almost always doable in one's own currency; the biggest risks are usually not the debts themselves but policymakers failing from lack of knowledge and/or authority. Foreign-currency debts force harder choices and more pain.¹

1. Knowledge, authority, and politics

Understanding and authority vary widely across countries — and so do outcomes; policymakers often under-react until extremes. Strong checks bring benefits yet can block needed moves that clash with rigid rules. Rules cannot foresee all cases; even the best act in hours inside fuzzy law.²

Checks and balances, meant to prevent concentrated power, can slow decisions and let narrow interests veto. Bold actors are attacked from all sides; politics in debt crises is horrendous, with distortion and misinformation everywhere.³

Over three to ten years, big debt crises can devastate people and countries; over the long run, productivity is more forceful — just less obvious.

2. Productivity, populism, and war's shadow

Political sequels (e.g., rising populism) can matter more than the debt crisis itself. On long real GDP per capita charts, >3% contractions shade the path, yet multi-decade growth rates dwarf the bumps; the biggest bumps often come from wars — which may themselves grow from depression politics.⁴

Looking back across §1–§5: the credit machine, the template, deflationary seven stages, inflationary five stages plus hyper branch, and wartime accounting — all exist to recognize the script faster next time and to spread pain less onto the same people in the same year.⁵
