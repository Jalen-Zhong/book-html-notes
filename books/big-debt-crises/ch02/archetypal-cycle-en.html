§2 · ARCHETYPAL BIG DEBT CYCLE

The template rests on roughly 48 big debt cycles — cases in large countries where real GDP fell more than about 3%. Dalio groups countries into: (1) little foreign-currency debt and no inflationary depression; (2) substantial FX debt and inflationary depression. FX debt and inflation correlated strongly (~75%), so high-FX and inflationary cases share one basket.¹

Classic script: debt and debt-service costs outrun the incomes needed to service them → deleveraging. In ordinary crises, central banks can still cut real and nominal rates; depressions begin when that no longer works. Many short-term debt cycles stack into one long-term cycle — each short peak and trough higher in debt/income — until rate cuts that fueled expansion cannot continue (e.g., rates at 0%). U.S. charts since 1910 often show debt rising while interest outlays stay flat or fall — easing “extends” the boom; when easing is exhausted, deleveraging starts.

Short cycles are bumps; the long cycle is bumps that ratchet leverage higher.

1. The window: before, during, after the depression

The template focuses on the run-up, the depression itself, and the deleveraging after the bottom. Deflationary and inflationary big crises are examined separately. Phase stats average cases from about five years before the depression bottom through about seven years after.²

Long debt cycles resemble amplified short cycles but are more extreme: heavier burdens, less effective conventional monetary policy. Short cycles make mini-booms and recessions; long ones make great booms and busts. The U.S. ran two in a century: 1920s boom → 1930s Depression; early-2000s boom → crisis from 2008.

In the short cycle, spending is constrained mainly by willingness to lend and borrow. Easy credit → expansion; scarce credit → recession. Central banks can usually cut rates to restart. But humans prefer borrowing and spending over paying down, so successive bottoms and tops carry more activity — and more debt — until debt outruns income for good: the long-term debt cycle.

² Two U.S. long-cycle contrasts 1929 and 2008 are paired as domestic-credit excesses under deflationary pressure; differences include policy speed, gold-standard constraints, QE tools, and deposit-insurance legacy.

2. The upswing: confidence, outside intermediaries, false prosperity

On the long upswing, lenders stay easy even as indebtedness rises — self-reinforcement: spending ↑ → incomes and net worth ↑ → borrowing capacity ↑. Risk appetite rises; new intermediaries and instruments outside supervisory umbrellas offer higher leverage and risk for yield. Borrowers spend beyond sustainable levels yet look prosperous; lenders grow complacent. Debts still cannot outrun servicing capacity forever.³

At the limit the process reverses: asset prices fall, debtors struggle, investors refuse roll-overs → liquidity stress → spending cuts. One person’s spending is another’s income. Banks are squeezed; equity markets crash; unemployment and social tension rise — a vicious contraction unlike ordinary recessions, because rates are already near zero and conventional easing cannot restore lending capacity and desire.

Booms invent “better” lending machines; busts reveal they lived off the regulatory radar.

³ Shadow banking and regulatory gaps A recurring pattern: credit innovation escapes bank law — 1920s investment trusts; 2000s shadow banks and structured products. The template treats new intermediaries as systemic features.

3. As old as credit: Jubilee, workers, capitalists

Long debt cycles exist wherever credit does — before Rome; the Old Testament even describes a periodic debt wipe (Jubilee). Like most dramas, causes and scenes recur.⁴

Money is both medium of exchange and store of wealth, serving those who sell time for necessities (workers / “have-nots”) and those whose wealth is tied to money’s value (capitalist-investors / “haves”). Government sets rules. Both groups usually gain from borrowing and lending — until they do not, especially as debtors and creditors.

One person’s financial asset is another’s liability. When claims on money overwhelm available money, a big deleveraging is required: the free-market credit system stalls or runs in reverse; the state intervenes — central bank as buyer/lender of last resort, government as redistributor. Restructuring shrinks claims on future spending relative to money.⁵

That imbalance has always been resolved by some mix of the four levers. The pain can polarize workers and capitalists; historically it even helped brand usury a sin in Christian and Islamic traditions — with civilizational footnotes Dalio flags about medieval lending rules.

4. The fork: deflationary vs inflationary depressions

Deflationary depressions: policymakers cut rates; near 0% that lever fails. Restructuring and austerity dominate without enough stimulation (especially printing and currency depreciation). Debt burdens can rise as incomes fall faster than write-downs. These usually occur where unsustainable debts were financed domestically in local currency: forced selling and defaults, not necessarily an FX/BoP crisis.⁶

Inflationary depressions: classic in countries reliant on foreign capital with heavy FX debt that cannot be monetized. When inflows slow, credit creation flips to contraction; capital withdrawal drains liquidity while currency declines produce inflation. High-FX inflationary deleveragings are especially hard — pain is harder to spread.⁷

Next the narrative enters the deflationary stage play — the “depression” face most reserve-currency readers know best.⁸

Same debt machine; local vs foreign currency decides whether pain skews deflationary or inflationary.
