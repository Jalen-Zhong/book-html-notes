4. New Deal & beautiful deleveraging (1933–1937)

Big Debt Crises §8 · sub-chapter · story rewrite

The day after inauguration Roosevelt declared a bank holiday, suspended gold exports (effectively weakening the dollar–gold link), and raced to restructure banks. The point was not one bill but rebalancing the four levers: restructuring/guarantees to stop runs, monetary devaluation/reflation to lift nominal incomes, fiscal expansion to patch demand—so debt/income improves without pure default liquidation.¹

After leaving gold, deflation became “acceptable inflation” and nominal growth began to run above interest rates—the macro signature of a beautiful deleveraging. Around 1935 a Goldilocks stretch appeared: recovery visible, extreme crisis fear fading.²

Printing and guarantees paired with restructuring are reflation; paired only with austerity dogma they prolong liquidation.

Politically the New Deal was contested and experimental; in debt-cycle language the vector was ugly → beautiful. Investors saw risk assets reprice after a regime shift—not autopilot back to pre-1929.³
