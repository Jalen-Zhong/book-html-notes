2. Top & crash (late 1929)

Big Debt Crises §8 · sub-chapter · story rewrite

Story rewrite of a case study — not verbatim. Full text: Ray Dalio, Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises (Bridgewater, 2018).

Tightening popped the bubble. In 1928 the Fed lifted short rates to slow speculative credit; August 1929 added another hike. The curve flattened/inverted, cash’s relative return rose, and leveraged longs’ funding costs rose. Liquidity left long-duration and risky assets.¹

Then classic forced selling: margin calls, sentiment flip, volume and panic headlines feeding each other. Policy turned quickly—cuts, liquidity, reassurance—but wealth effects and credit contraction were already on; “saving the market” alone does not restore private debt-service capacity.²

The crash is developer fluid for the top; true depression develops slowly in banks, incomes, and trade.

Investor lesson: when the bubble checklist is lit, the last mile of hikes is deadliest; the first post-crash bounce is often misread as another 1907/1920-style ending—fuel for the next act’s false hope.³
