3. Depression deepens (1930–1932)

Big Debt Crises §8 · sub-chapter · story rewrite

Story rewrite of a case study — not verbatim. Full text: Ray Dalio, Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises (Bridgewater, 2018).

Early 1930 markets believed the “50% correction was over” and rallied for months while policy kept stimulating. Then earnings, incomes, and debt pressure developed. Smoot–Hawley raised protectionism and crushed trade; bank runs and failures rewrote a liquidity scare as a solvency crisis.¹

1931 was the year of a global dollar shortage and European debt stress spilling over: optimism yielded to gloom; policy debate jammed; sterling’s strain and gold-exit shock traveled back to the U.S. Failed debt-moratorium diplomacy and reserve drains left the Fed feeling externally constrained amid domestic deflation.²

When most of what people call money is credit, a rush to cash discovers there is not enough cash—unless the central bank prints or defaults clear.

Through 1932 more intervention still struggled to halt collapse; the election put political authority on stage. By pre-inauguration 1933 the banking system neared freeze. The ugly-deleveraging recipe was complete: defaults, austerity, and deflation reinforcing; printing and guarantees slow and twisted.³
