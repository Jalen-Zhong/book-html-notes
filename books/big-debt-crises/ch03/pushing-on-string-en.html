6. Pushing on a String

Big Debt Crises §3 · sub-chapter · story rewrite

Story rewrite of a deflationary-cycle phase — not verbatim. Full text: Ray Dalio, Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises (Bridgewater, 2018).

Late in the long debt cycle, central banks sometimes cannot convert stimulus into spending: rate cuts and bond-buying lose punch; growth and asset returns stay low; policy must get money and credit more directly to spenders. 1930s policymakers called this pushing on a string. Excess printing/monetization and devaluation vs deflationary alternatives can tip into ugly inflationary deleveraging.¹

MP1: interest-rate policy — broadest effects on present values, credit affordability, and debt service. Near 0%, shift to MP2: QE — printing to buy financial assets (often bonds), reshaping saver/investor portfolios. If proceeds fund real spending, the economy is stimulated; if they mainly bid up financial assets, wealth effects trickle slowly and widen inequality.²

QE works best when risk/liquidity premiums are large; once premiums are crushed and prices elevated, reward-to-risk worsens, cash looks relatively better, and QE feels like pushing on a string. Prolonged monetization can threaten money’s store-of-value role and push people toward gold and alternatives.³

MP1 moves borrowers; MP2 moves savers; when the string is soft, MP3 puts money in spenders’ hands.

MP3: put money more directly with spenders (especially higher-MPC households) and incentivize spending — fiscal–monetary coordination, directed credit, helicopter-money variants, large write-downs with money creation (“Jubilee”-like). Coordination usually beats central-bank solo drops; macroprudential tools still steer credit across stages.⁴
