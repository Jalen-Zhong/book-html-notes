4. The Depression

Big Debt Crises §3 · sub-chapter · story rewrite

Story rewrite of a deflationary-cycle phase — not verbatim. Full text: Ray Dalio, Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises (Bridgewater, 2018).

In ordinary recessions, rate cuts can still create wealth effects, stimulate activity, and ease debt service. A depression’s formula: rates near 0% (or floors raised by FX/credit risk), so that tool fails. True in 1930–32 and early 2008–09; better-managed cases like the U.S. in 2007–08 cut fast and then shifted to alternatives — versus slower 1930s easing and even tightening to defend the dollar’s gold link.¹

Defaults avalanche onto leveraged lenders; runs threaten unprotected institutions. Rising credit spreads blunt the help of cutting risk-free rates. Defaults and austerity (deflationary forces) dominate before printing/monetization balances them.²

Liquidity — turning investments into money — becomes the fear: when many rush to cash claims, the central bank must print liquidity or allow mass defaults. Crises mix solvency problems (not enough equity under rules) and cash-flow problems (runs even with adequate capital) — the latter often the immediate trigger.³

It is not mainly “money moving from risky to safe assets”; much of what people call money is credit — appearing in booms, vanishing in busts.

Deleveraging is not primarily psychology: even with amnesia, debtors would still owe more money than they take in. Printing more money cheapens money, validating creditor fears. Policy usually mis-mixes first — taxpayer anger and moral hazard delay support; delay deepens the ugly phase. Eventually guarantees, printing, and monetization arrive — fast and balanced means shorter depression (post-2008); slow means long (1930s, Japan’s lost decade). The biggest blockers remain ignorance and lack of authority.⁴

Four levers in the depression: austerity is tried first but rarely restores ratios alone (incomes fall too); printing stops runs, widens collateral, supports systemic firms; restructuring must be large enough to make debts payable yet devastates leveraged creditors; redistribution is politically common but rarely finishes deleveraging alone without revolutionary confiscation.⁵
