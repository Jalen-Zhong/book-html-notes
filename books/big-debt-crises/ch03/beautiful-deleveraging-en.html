A beautiful deleveraging happens when the four levers move in balance: shocks stay tolerable, growth turns positive, debt burdens fall, inflation stays acceptable. Stimulation (printing/monetization and currency depreciation) must offset austerity/defaults and lift nominal growth above nominal rates — without igniting unacceptable inflation, a currency rout, or a new bubble.¹

Spending is money or credit. When debts overwhelm the money available to service them, incremental spending and service relief must come from more money. Central banks can widen collateral and monetize broader debts. Right doses: (1) neutralize a deflationary credit collapse; (2) keep nominal growth modestly above nominal rates so deleveraging is spread tolerably.²

Key inequality: nominal income growth > nominal interest rate — then burdens start to lighten on their own.

Must printing raise inflation? Not if it merely replaces vanishing credit and stabilizes spending. History shows spending is what matters for prices; a dollar spent with money equals a dollar spent with credit. Tales of collapsing “velocity” often mask credit destruction offset by money creation.³

Stimulus can be abused into an ugly inflationary deleveraging (Weimar; Argentina/Brazil 1980s). High FX debt and foreign ownership make the mix harder. In the end policymakers almost always print: pure austerity hurts too much, big restructurings wipe wealth too fast, transfers are rarely large enough. Rough archetype magnitudes (illustrative): printing ~4% of GDP/year, ~50% initial devaluation vs gold, deficits ~6% of GDP; aggressive stimulus averages 2–3 years into the depression after >50% equity drops — but early and smart beats the average.⁴

Gold or FX pegs force tighter policy; when pain bites, links break and currencies devalue — the U.S. Depression gold break helped mark bottoms; 2008’s fiat regime made purchases and guarantees less legally dramatic.⁵
