2. The Bubble

Bubble stage one: debts rise faster than incomes; asset returns and growth accelerate. Self-reinforcement lifts borrowing capacity as incomes, net worth, and collateral rise together. Unsustainable — yet borrowers feel richer, spend more than they earn, and buy assets at high prices with leverage.¹

Bull markets often start justified: lower rates lift present values; earnings and balance sheets improve. Confidence rises; FOMO arrives; explicit/implicit guarantees encourage reckless lending. Standards fall; banks lever up; lightly regulated shadow banks and financial engineering bloom.²

If central banks target only inflation and growth, they may refuse to tighten during what looks like a productivity boom — Japan late 1980s, late 1920s, mid-2000s. Dalio’s claim: watch whether debt growth can be serviced by future income; do not hide behind “bubbles are undetectable / not our job.”³

When consensus is priced in, extrapolation becomes the expensive habit.

Measurable bubble traits include rich prices vs traditional metrics, priced-in further gains, broad bullishness, high leverage, extended forward purchases, new buyers, and still-stimulative money. More important: the distribution of debt-service capacity across entities — concentrated leverage in key nodes beats a uniform high average.⁴
