3. The Top

Big Debt Crises §3 · sub-chapter · story rewrite

When prices have been driven by leveraged buying and the market is fully long, leveraged, and overpriced, reversal ripens. General principle: when things are so good they cannot get better — yet everyone believes they will — tops are being made.¹

Triggers vary; most often central-bank tightening and rising rates — from the bubble heating growth/inflation, or from external liquidity withdrawal. Rising short rates flatten the curve; cash beats lending; a flat/inverted curve often precedes the pop.²

Early top: parts of credit are hurt while others stay robust; the economy’s turn is unclear; the central bank may still hike. Fastest tightening often ~five months before the equity peak; short-rate peaks a few months before. The more leverage and the higher prices, the less tightening it takes to prick the bubble — and the bigger the bust. Sensitivity and loss cascades matter more than the size of the hike.³

Cruelty of tops: policy still brakes “overheating” while recession seeds sprout in the frothiest pockets.

Right after the pop, wealth effects from asset prices often hit growth harder than monetary policy can immediately offset. Investors misread the decline as a buy-the-dip vs past earnings, underestimating the coming earnings collapse; wealth falls first, incomes later, credit contracts self-reinforce.⁴

Section notes
¹ Top psychology Extreme optimism + fully invested positioning is classic top sociology, paired with “this time is different.”
Why it matters：Spotting emotional extremes.
Sources：Behavioral-finance primers; Dalio top notes
² Curve inversion Treasury term-spread inversion is a well-known (imperfect) recession lead. Dalio stresses how it dulls incentives to lend.
Why it matters：Links money markets to credit supply.
Sources：Yield-curve research primers
³ Sensitivity over magnitude Highly leveraged sectors can break on small hikes; need sector balance-sheet vision.
Why it matters：Operational moral.
Sources：Stress-testing primers
⁴ Wealth effects Asset declines curb spending and collateral — often underestimated early in crises.
Why it matters：Why easing can lag the plunge.
Sources：Wealth-effect literature
