§1 · CREDIT & DEBT

Credit is buying power granted now; debt is the promise to pay it back. Providing credit is often good — without it, development starves. Trouble arrives when debts cannot be paid. Whether rapid credit growth is good or bad hinges on what the credit produces and how the debt is serviced.¹

Many prudent people dislike debt; Dalio admits he preferred saving to borrowing. For society as a whole, “a little debt is always better” is not necessarily true — policymakers hold levers individuals lack. Too little credit can destroy opportunity as harshly as too much destroys balance sheets.

Credit creates spending power and debt together; the test is whether output covers service.

1. Good debt, bad debt: the subway thought experiment

A rough rule: if borrowed funds produce enough income to service the debt, lenders, borrowers, and society can gain; if not, resources were likely misallocated. Count secondary benefits too — refusing obviously productive education or infrastructure finance in the name of fiscal conservatism can be a costly false economy.²

Suppose a $1 billion subway, financed with debt expected to be repaid from revenue, delivers only half the expected cash. A 50% write-down follows. Does that mean it should never have been built? Reframe: is the subway still worth $500 million more than budgeted — or about 2% more per year over a 25-year life? Often having it, dearer beats not having it.

Macro sketch: in very bad cases, ~40% of a loan’s value may not be repaid; bad loans ~20% of all loans → losses ~8% of total debt; if debt ≈ 200% of GDP, the hole ≈ 16% of GDP. Socialized over 15 years ≈ 1%/year — tolerable; taken at once — not. Downside risk of heavy debt depends on willingness and ability to spread losses.³

Two keys recur: (1) is the debt in a currency policymakers control? (2) can they influence how creditors and debtors behave toward each other?

2. Are crises inevitable? Why cycles?

Few well-disciplined countries avoid debt crises entirely. Lending is imperfect; cycle psychology breeds bubbles and busts; policymakers often err loose for near-term growth and political ease. That is the main reason big debt cycles recur.⁴

Talk of long cycles raises eyebrows — as if astrology. Dalio insists: cycles are only logically driven sequences that recur, not destiny clocks. In market economies, credit expansions and contractions drive ups and downs for clear reasons.

Ultra-simple: whenever you borrow, you create a cycle — spending more than you earn now means spending less than you earn later to repay. True for a person; true for a nation. Borrowing sets a mechanical series of events in motion.

Borrowing is not a free lunch; it relocates spending along the timeline.

⁴ Political ease and bubble fuel Electoral cycles, growth targets, and expanding guarantees often loosen credit standards in booms. Minsky-style instability and “this time is different” narratives rhyme with Dalio’s psychology–credit feedback.

3. The Monopoly metaphor

Early on, cash is plentiful and property scarce — converting cash into property pays. Later, rents devour cash and forced sales appear. Early “property is king,” later “cash is king.” Skill is holding the right mix as the board fills.⁵

Add a bank that lends and takes deposits, plus player-to-player credit: debt-financed hotel spending multiplies far beyond cash in existence. Debtors short cash; banks face withdrawals and missed payments. Without intervention, bankruptcy and contraction follow. Repeated mini-cycles fertilize a long-term debt crisis.

Upside: lending supports spending and investment → incomes and asset prices → more borrowing — lifting activity above sustainable productivity growth. Near the peak, loans assume above-trend growth forever. It cannot. When debt service exceeds what can still be borrowed to fund spending, the cycle reverses.

Debt-financed fixed investment, real estate, and infrastructure are especially cyclical — once built, the same build-rate is not needed. Emerging economies often add competitiveness swings as wages rise (Japan’s postwar decades are a frequent long sample). A classic bubble warning: more money borrowed merely to service existing debt.⁶

4. Tops, contagion, and two lasting scars

When money/credit growth is curtailed or standards tighten, spending slows and service problems surface; central-bank tightening often accelerates the slide. Debtors miss payments; lenders miss theirs — policymakers must stabilize lending institutions first. The most leveraged, concentrated intermediaries hurt worst: classically banks; in dynamic systems also insurers, broker-dealers, SPVs.⁷

Two lasting problems: (1) losses when expected service is not paid (smaller coupons or principal write-downs); (2) even after resolution, over-indebted spenders rarely restore prior spending levels — demand can permanently downshift.

⁷ Systemically important intermediaries and shadow banks 2008 showed epicenters often sit outside classic deposit-insurance umbrellas, in wholesale funding chains. Later deflationary chapters return to shadow banking and new products.

5. Can crises be managed? Four levers and beautiful deleveraging

This study examines extremes: cases in the last 100 years with real GDP declines over ~3%. Dalio’s claim: when debt is mostly in a country’s own currency, policymakers can usually spread consequences so big debt problems need not be catastrophes. The worst damage often precedes the right adjustments — even the 1930s was traversed once levers were used well. Biggest risks: (1) ignorance or lack of authority; (2) political fallout from hurting some to help others.⁸

Foreign-currency debt narrows the room; “manageable” never means “costless.”

Four levers to bring debt and debt service down relative to incomes: (1) austerity; (2) defaults/restructurings; (3) central-bank “printing” and purchases/guarantees; (4) transfers from those with surplus to those in need. Some are deflationary; money printing is stimulative/inflationary. A beautiful deleveraging balances them so debt/income falls while growth and inflation stay acceptable and nominal income growth returns above nominal rates.

Each lever rewrites who gains, who loses, and over what horizon — which is why even successful policymakers rarely get applause.
