3. Hyperinflation (c. 1922–mid-1923)

Early 1922 still featured talks of a reparations moratorium and an international gold loan. Creditors wanted a Germany that could pay; debtors wanted a burden they could live with. When settlement hopes collapsed—with French hard lines and political shocks such as Rathenau’s assassination—the external-finance story failed and true hyperinflation ignited.¹

The mechanism stopped being abstract: depreciation → import and expected inflation → wage/contract indexation → real fiscal revenue collapse → more printing; the public shortened deposits; lenders shortened duration; flows rushed into FX, gold, and goods. Denominations entered astronomical narratives; money’s three roles (store, unit, medium) failed one by one.²

After the Ruhr occupation, “passive resistance” floored the fiscal/printing accelerator—external defiance and domestic monetary death can arrive together.

In January 1923 France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr. Passive resistance accelerated fiscal collapse; the Reichsbank briefly borrowed FX and raised rates to “defend” the mark, but versus astronomical inflation rates were symbolic and borrowed FX burned fast. By late summer society barely functioned: producers refused marks; barter and foreign pricing spread.³
