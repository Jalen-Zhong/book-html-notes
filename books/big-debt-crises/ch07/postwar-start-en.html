1. Postwar start (c. 1914–1920)

The story begins with war finance, not 1923 wallpaper. After 1914 Germany left gold-standard practice; gaps were met with domestic debt and growing monetization. Prewar memory of a short Franco-Prussian war plus victor indemnities misread a long war. Germany collected no indemnity—it owed one: the extreme FX-debt case.¹

After the November 1918 armistice, political turmoil and capital flight ran together. Negotiators briefly hoped reparations would be limited to occupied-area damage, paid in goods, with territorial self-determination. The final Versailles terms crushed that hope: hard-currency obligations, territorial/capacity losses, pride and solvency hit at once. The first large mark and price swings followed.²

“Can fight” and “can pay” are not the same sentence—victors wanted Germany weak and yet strong enough to pay.

Policy legacy of this act: wartime printing habits plus postwar hard external constraints. Any relative calm was an intermission before the next reparations hammer. For investors, local cash and long bonds were questioned first; gold and FX became insurance.³
