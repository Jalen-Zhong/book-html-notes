§7 is the extreme textbook of the inflationary-depression template (§4): wartime defeat, huge foreign-currency reparations, a broken gold link, and chronic money printing to fund fiscal and external gaps until the mark dies as money. Dalio contrasts it with deflationary classics (1930s, 2008) to show how FX debt + forced outflows + failed FX defense rewrite a depression as inflation.¹

Compress the timeline into five acts: postwar start under Versailles → reparations shock and spiral → hyperinflation peak → Rentenmark/new-money reset with negotiating room → aftermath (political polarization that sets the 1930s stage). Each act maps back to the template: debts are someone else’s assets; a central bank prints only its own money; depreciation that outruns rate compensation becomes self-reinforcing.²

Reparations are “almost non-defaultable FX debt”—if the gap cannot close, printing becomes a substitute for external finance.

Read as both policymaker and investor: the former walks a wire between vengeance and solvency; the latter re-ranks paper, gold, and real goods. Sub-chapters follow the phases; expand §7 on the home index.³
