4. Stabilization & the Rentenmark (late 1923–1924)

Hyperinflation opened a political window of “cannot delay.” Allies also concluded that without real reparation relief German policy could barely stop total collapse. Payments paused and restructuring talks reopened—oxygen for domestic reform.¹

Stabilization was a chain, not a single spell: make the external burden manageable (debt service down toward a small share of GDP) → introduce the Rentenmark with an asset-backed credibility story and strict issuance caps → cut old-mark fiscal printing → fiscal consolidation and a more independent monetary setup → re-anchor FX and price expectations.²

A new money works first because the old printing story stops—the peg/anchor is what makes people believe the stop.

Arrangements such as the Dawes Plan then layered loans and scheduled reparations, extending stability from money into the balance of payments. Inflation expectations can collapse faster than money-stock stories alone predict—what matters is rule credibility and whether the external gap truly closed.³
