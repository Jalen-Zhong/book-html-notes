2. Reparations spiral (c. 1920–1921)

From spring 1920 into spring 1921 Germany saw a “relative stabilization”: FX and prices less mad. Globally, US/UK tightening drove severe contraction; German discount rates stayed very easy and the Reichsbank bought bills whenever credit tightened—domestic ease under a hard external constraint.¹

The turn was the May 1921 London Ultimatum: reparations size and structure overwhelmed markets, with a design that felt like “improve and you owe more.” Hard-currency debt versus GDP towered over other inflationary-depression samples. After the announcement the mark sold off hard and inflation returned.²

The spiral still looked “contained” while foreigners bought German assets—when that bid left, the slope cliffed.

In H2 1921 classic inflationary dynamics locked in: reparation outflows + local flight → FX↓ → prices↑ → scramble into goods. Political hits such as Upper Silesia compounded it; retail “strength” was savings fleeing into stuff. Negotiators chased moratoria and loans, but creditor splits (French hard line) blocked lasting relief.³
