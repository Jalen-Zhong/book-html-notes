Many inflationary depressions are transitory BoP crises that close gaps and cool inflation; a subset spiral into hyperinflation — prices more than doubling yearly, with extreme wealth loss and hardship. The tell: the gap among external income, external spending, and debt service is not closed, yet large-scale printing keeps funding external spending for years.¹

Sometimes involuntary — Weimar reparations were largely non-defaultable forced outflows. Sometimes chosen — propping growth instead of aligning spending with income. Stopping the press amid outflows means extreme illiquidity; the longer it runs, the harder to stop. By late Weimar, the entire 1913 money stock barely bought a kilo of rye bread — stopping printing meant commerce halting.²

Spiral: devalue → flight → devalue → inflate → print. Wage–cost spirals (including indexation) institutionalize inflation. Depositors shorten tenors; lenders shorten or stop; shorting cash is cheap. Cash shortages hit banks and firms; the central bank prints faster and lends directly — more funds chase gold, FX, and real hedges than productive spend. FX-debt burdens rise as stimulus fades. Tax hikes spur evasion and more flight. GDP accounting distorts: gold, factories, even rocks bought as stores of value.³

Money loses store, unit, and medium roles; producers refuse local currency for FX or barter. Printing cannot fix FX-shortage illiquidity. Financial wealth is redistributed by inflation; politics, strikes, and violence peak. Investing simplifies: short the currency, get capital out, own commodities and resource industries; equities diverge from the exchange rate; bonds are wiped. Classic exit: retire the old currency and issue a new one with hard backing.⁴

Hyperinflation is not linear 'print a bit more' — it is trust collapse that locks policy on the accelerator.
