Unlike a household, a country can change the quantity and value of money. A 30% devaluation is a price cut to foreigners while domestic wage accounts can stay nominally stable — external relative-price adjustment instead of domestic deflation.¹

After reserves, hikes, vows, and controls, policymakers usually stop fighting and let the currency fall (often trying to smooth it). Typical: ~30% real drop initially; shorts do not fully offset losses (~30% year-one currency loss); another ~10% reserves may still be spent 'managing' the fall.²

Devaluation is a double-edged sword: it lifts local-currency asset prices and exports, compresses imports, and helps the BoP — and it raises import inflation. Gradual repeated falls nurture 'more to come' expectations, flight, and inflation psychology; a large one-off move to a two-way market, preferably as a surprise, usually works better.³

Inflows can fall >5pp of GDP in under a year; outflows continue; printing stays limited (it funds exit); short rates may jump ~20pp with an inverted curve; equities ~−50% locally, worse in FX terms. FX-debt burdens rise ~20pp of GDP. Inflation often +15pp, peaking near 30%, elevated ~two years. GDP gap can fall ~8pp to a trough near −4%; activity bottoms ~a year in — 'hitting bottom' as catharsis that forces price and policy metamorphosis.⁴

Yesterday's 'treasure' trade becomes today's trash — surplus sellers, vanished buyers.
