CHAPTER ONE · LOCAL GOVERNMENT POWER AND AFFAIRS · ASSIGNMENT BASICS

Classroom analogies stick: treat Guangdong or Jiangsu as if they sat among the world’s larger economies; match a central province to some mid-sized country’s nominal GDP. The point is not a precision contest of rankings. It is a governance warning: China is vast and internally diverse; day-to-day rule cannot be only “the center decides everything.”¹

Chapter 1 asks a plain question: which level of government should do what, and how are borders drawn? The author’s answer folds into three interlocking principles — externalities and the benefit area, information complexity, and incentive compatibility — then uses investment promotion (zhaoshang yínzi) to show that localities do far more than supply textbook “public goods.” They participate deeply in production and distribution.²

Expenditure assignment before revenue: first clarify “what to do,” then “what money to use.”

1. Features of governance

On the everyday administrative map, a five-rung chain is familiar: center — province — prefecture-level city — county (district) — township. History once looked closer to a three-rung “center — province — prefecture/county” stack. After industrialization and urbanization, “city leads county” spread, prefectures became cities, and urban units multiplied. Sparse frontier areas still keep “prefecture / league” labels; villages below the township rely mainly on villager self-governance — otherwise the fiscally supported headcount would explode again.³

Even within one “level,” discretion differs: provincial capitals versus ordinary prefecture-level cities; county-level cities versus counties versus urban districts. Recent campaigns — turning counties into cities or districts, province-directly-governing counties, Yangtze River Delta integration, the Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area — keep rewriting horizontal power and resources.

Center–local relations run through the book: unity needs central authority; sheer size makes localities the daily operators. The constitutional formula states the tension in abstract language — under unified leadership, give full play to local initiative. Long-run tallies of “unity versus fragmentation” simply stretch the same problem across dynasties.⁴

Party and government overlap heavily in organization and personnel. For economic development, the chapter deliberately does not treat “party versus government” as the main axis: local party secretaries are still local officials; the same information and incentive constraints that shape government assignment also shape party work division; day-to-day economic tasks are pushed by government departments.

Tiao–kuai segmentation is the operational signature: party committees, governments, people’s congresses, and political consultative conferences replicate down the ladder; ministries have matching bureaus. Vertical “lines” (tiao) guide professional work; horizontal “blocks” (kuai) often control appointments — so a county education bureau answers both the city education bureau and the county party–government leadership.

Five rungs are the skeleton; tiao–kuai and personnel are the nerves that actually fire.

2. Externalities and scale economies

Local power is first framed by administrative borders. Under territorial management, ask: does this decision’s impact stop inside our city?

An externality is when your action affects others. Second-hand smoke is a negative case; vaccination that lowers others’ infection risk is a positive one. A primary school mainly serving local children can often be a city call; factory pollution drifting next door needs the province; cross-provincial smog may require the center. Borders that are too small breed endless spillover coordination; borders that are too large glue together people with mismatched preferences.⁵

Scale economies pull the other way: for defense or big infrastructure, more users can thin fixed costs. Most services also congest — overcrowded parks, distant school districts. Administrative design is a tightrope between spreading costs and respecting access costs and preference diversity. School-catchment lines, multi-district cities, and imperial debates over how far conquest should go all fit the same frame.⁶

Impact area ≈ benefit area ≈ decision tier: draw it wrong, and another meeting will not fix it.
Footnotes
⁵ Externalities and the benefit-area principle When private and social costs or benefits diverge, externalities appear; Pigouvian taxes, Coase bargaining, regulation, and property rights are standard responses. Fiscal federalism translates this into a benefit-area rule: decision and financing should match the geography of beneficiaries. Watershed pollution, regional transit, and joint epidemic control are recurring Chinese cross-border cases.
Why it matters: turns “who is in charge” from power-struggle talk into testable geography.
Source: public-economics externality primers; cross-border pollution / regional coordination cases
⁶ Scale economies and optimal jurisdiction size Scale economies mean average cost falls as output rises; for public goods, more users share fixed costs. Congestion, preference heterogeneity, and monitoring distance raise marginal costs — hence “optimal jurisdiction size.” The Tiebout story imagines residents voting with their feet; hukou and housing constrain mobility in China, but the preference-matching intuition still helps.
Why it matters: explains why bigger is not always better and smaller is not always kinder.

3. Complex information

“The mountains are high and the emperor is far away” is folk information economics: if the court cannot see, it cannot easily control. Gathering and processing information is costly, so the side with an information edge often holds the decision edge.

Superiors may overturn any lower decision on paper — formal authority. They cannot process every detail; inspections still rely on briefings. Without independent information, superiors can be led by the report. Lower levels thus often hold large real authority. Which tier should supervise a state firm also tracks information feasibility: distant, heterogeneous enterprises are easier to devolve; strategic assets stay under close upper watch regardless of distance.⁷

Intervention versus autonomy is a spectrum. If lower officials want something and the upper tier does not clearly object, it often proceeds; if they do not want it, they can stall or kick decisions upstairs to an information-poor superior. Inside organizations the same logic holds: rotating “officials” come and go; local “clerks” stay — information sticks to people.⁸

Formal authority lives in documents; real authority lives with whoever sees the detail first.

Chinese idioms about “the county magistrate being less useful than the person on the spot” and “policies above, countermeasures below” rhyme with the same mechanism.

4. Incentive compatibility

If what one side wants is also what the other side is both willing and able to deliver, call it incentive compatibility. Inside government, superiors and subordinates need it; so do task goals and officials’ own interests (the latter reserved for later chapters on cadre incentives).

Tasks split roughly into two kinds: concrete work with clear rules and measurable results; and broad goals — growth, employment — that require creative mobilization. The first fit vertical management better. Customs is a clean case: performance, promotion, and benefits largely close inside the system, with limited conflict against the host locality.⁹

Other professional agencies are wired into the local economy. Industry-and-commerce once went provincial-vertical to curb local protection; local oversight then weakened and incentive misalignment sharpened. After scandals, management swung back toward “local primary, upper participation,” later folding into market-regulation bureaus. Environmental protection long showed a similar tension — upper focus on quality, lower fear of short-run growth costs — hence monitoring, appraisal, and partial vertical pilots as “micro-adjustments,” not one-shot redesign.

After markets multiplied interests, “unified thinking” still matters but is not enough: the person who evaluates performance, the person who assigns work, and the person most affected by the work should, as far as possible, be the same superior. Otherwise dual leadership becomes “everyone supervises, no one owns.”

Incentive compatibility is not a slogan: align the signer, the doer, and the person who takes the blame.
Footnotes
⁹ Incentive compatibility and mechanism design Incentive compatibility comes from mechanism design and contract theory: under self-interest, rules should make truthful reporting or effort optimal. Hurwicz, Maskin, and Myerson formalized the language. In government organization, expenditure, revenue, personnel, and KPIs must align — or selective implementation follows.
Why it matters: reframes “disobedience” from morals into designable institutions.
Source: mechanism-design primers; vertical vs territorial management policy reviews

5. Investment promotion

After the three principles map “who should do what,” the fifth section zooms in: what do localities actually do for growth? Far more than parks and policing.

Investment promotion is often an all-hands task — not only the investment bureau; education and health agencies may be told to know the policies and watch for leads. Delivery needs industrial parks: land development, “seven connections and one leveling,” planning and project packaging. As owners of urban land, local governments frequently supply industrial plots at preferential prices and finish early infrastructure; for large firms they may take platform equity, help with bank loans, and assist with ministry licenses and sector permits.¹⁰

Fiscal tools stack up: R&D and export subsidies, multi-year corporate-tax holidays, personal-tax rebates for executives, school and hospital access, hiring help and talent housing. Land, capital (state banks and platforms), hukou and public services, public research — key inputs are highly intervenable. The result is a mixed economy: not a clean “government builds the stage, firms perform,” and not the textbook split where government only supplies pure public goods.¹¹

Trying to understand China’s economy without government is like studying fish without water: not a value judgment, an observational premise.

Closing

Scale and borders, information complexity, incentive compatibility — three lenses, one theme: differences and conflicts among groups. Without differences, assignment barely matters; with them, assignment shapes outcomes.

Localities supply services and also enter production and distribution. Assignment of tasks answers “what to do”; the next chapter turns to fiscal powers and resources — “what money to use.” Tax reforms can churn; task-assignment principles stay relatively sticky: geography and culture, information and incentives do not vanish overnight.

See assignment clearly first — or you will read the right Chinese story with the wrong government model.
