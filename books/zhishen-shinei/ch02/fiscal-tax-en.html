CHAPTER TWO · FISCAL TAXATION AND GOVERNMENT BEHAVIOR · FOLLOW THE MONEY

Chinese historical dramas often open with a jolt: famous officials do not declaim; they count. Treasury shortfalls, navy budgets, imperial gardens — conflict hides inside line items. The classroom translation is blunt: files alone do not reveal intent; cash flows do.¹

Chapter 1 assigned “who does what.” This chapter asks what happens when spending mandates outrun own-source revenue. In the post-1994 public picture, localities shoulder most budget outlays (recently around four-fifths) while collecting roughly half of budget revenue — the gap is filled by central transfers and off-budget tools. Rewrite the fiscal rules, and local development strategies rewrite themselves.²

Expenditure assignment says what to do; the fiscal system says what you can still afford — or must invent.

1. The 1994 tax-sharing reform

If one word captures the 1980s Chinese economy, many lectures pick “contracting”: land contracts, enterprise contracts, and fiscal contracts. After 1985, “separate kitchens” spread — provinces bargained remittance bases and sharing rates, keeping upside. Forms varied wildly; coastal remittance burdens could differ by an order of magnitude.³

Contracting lit a fire under local growth: township-village enterprises (TVEs) bloomed; product taxes and remitted profits fed county and township coffers. “Pay the center; keep the rest” sounded like a slogan and behaved like an incentive machine. Then the “two ratios” slid — the center’s share of national budget revenue, and budget revenue as a share of GDP. Fearing the next bargaining round would “whip the fast ox,” localities suppressed on-budget collections and hid revenue off-budget: tax breaks that “enriched enterprises,” then fees and levies that returned cash as exclusive local money.

Contracting-era tension (reader’s compass)

Incentive
Keep the upside → build firms
Cost
Two ratios decline
Game
Hide wealth / off-budget swell
Endgame
Center lacks macro “rice”

Without grain in hand, reform compensation, compulsory schooling floors, regional equalization, and crisis response become empty talk. The 1994 tax-sharing system (TSS) sorted taxes into central, local, and shared; state and local tax bureaus split (merged again in 2018) with more vertical administration to curb verbal tax cuts.⁴

VAT was the pivot: once a major local tax, it became shared — 75% central, 25% local. Tax rebates smoothed the cliff so localities kept base-year levels and shared only increments. Which year counted as the base was itself a bargain — settling on 1993 nationwide triggered a year-end collection rush to inflate future rebates. When enterprise income tax shifted from affiliation-based remittance toward sharing around 2002, the same base-year gaming returned.⁵

Local resistance was real: provinces comfortable under contracting objected. Public memoirs describe senior leaders touring provinces to reconcile ledgers face-to-face — successful policy is negotiated compromise, not purely mechanical command. TSS reversed the two ratios, stabilized the center’s share, rebuilt national revenue capacity for crises and restructuring, and rewrote how localities financed growth.

Tax-sharing was not a homework problem; it re-ranked who held the purse — and who spoke louder.

2. Land finance

TSS did not cancel “economic construction first”; it tightened on-budget cash localities could freely deploy. Transfers can patch budget gaps without funding park grading, investment subsidies, or urban expansion. Localities therefore played two hands: enlarge the tax base — especially heavy manufacturing, which lifts GDP, ties VAT to output, and pulls services — and open an off-budget front around land conveyance and development: “land finance.”⁶

Tax design itself shapes taste: when revenue is collected mainly from firms at the production stage, governments care where plants sit more than where consumers live; racing for projects can soft-pedal environmental enforcement and chase overcapacity. Scientific Development discourse later raised livelihood spending — and with it the local share of national outlays — tightening the eat-versus-build tension.

Land institutions need an honest caveat: urban land is state-owned, rural land collective; converting farmland to construction long required expropriation into state ownership — a path-dependent split evolved since the 1982 Constitution, not a single blueprint. In 1994, conveyance rights and proceeds stayed local, but sales were not yet a windfall: TVEs used collective construction land; city parcels were often discounted for FDI.

The turn came in the late 1990s: ending welfare housing opened the commodity-housing era; a revised Land Administration Law tightened non-agricultural use of collective land and strengthened urban monopolies over construction land. After 2001–2002, auction/listing rules for commercial land spread, and conveyance revenue surged into a genuine “second budget” — plus local land-related taxes and the local shares of VAT and corporate income tax along the real-estate chain.⁷

The mode settled into two hands: plentiful cheap industrial land to buy investment, jobs, and tax bases; tighter commercial/residential supply to monetize rising monopoly rents. “Land finance” is often read as “real-estate finance” — industrial subsidies build manufacturing capacity while constrained housing supply in migrant magnets lifts prices and “land kings.”

Picture the jurisdiction as a mall: shops pay a low entry fee but share turnover with the landlord; star brands may get free rent or cashback to maximize total mall income. Firms can shop for sites, so industrial land prices are hard to jack up; housing serves captive local residents, so monopoly power is stronger. Tax-plus-land tournament competition explains a lot of post-TSS growth — and plants debt seeds: capitalizing future rents into today’s projects under short tenures invites vanity investment.⁸

At the national scale, construction-land quotas rarely flow across provinces toward higher-productivity coastal metros — precious farmland quotas beside idle inland parks. Policy documents that put land-factor marketization first admit the point: if competition cannot move resources to higher uses, it is not the efficiency logic of open markets.

Selling land is not the goal; the goal is to pre-sell the city and the factory with today’s prices and tomorrow’s tax base.

3. Vertical and horizontal imbalance

After TSS, revenue gravity rose while spending stayed local — transfers can close the national gap without closing every rung or every place. Provinces may be flush while townships are not; coastal metros may be flush while inland counties are not. Vertical and horizontal cracks drove a long twenty-first-century reform sequence.⁹

Vertical: revenue up, mandates down. After center–province sharing, provinces share again with cities and counties; authority gradients leave lower tiers thinner cash and thicker tasks. A late-1990s ditty captured the mood: the center thrives, provinces steady, cities wobble, counties cry, townships empty. The coast could “eat” on-budget and “build” with land finance; many inland counties found land cheap and wages unpaid, spawning fees, arrears, and rural tension. “Peasants suffer; villages are poor; agriculture is in danger” became a policy fulcrum.

Responses came in bundles: fold rural compulsory education and rural health/pension schemes into multi-level public finance; add incentive transfers (e.g. “three rewards and one subsidy”) to curb headcount bloat; and flatten fiscal tiers — township finances under county management, empowered counties, and fiscally province-directly-governed counties — aiming to compress five administrative rungs toward a center–province–county fiscal stack.¹⁰

Flattening is not free. Zhejiang’s dense county clusters fit province-direct rule; elsewhere provinces may supervise too many units, land-related graft may rise, or county–city relations may tip from cooperation to rivalry and “island” counties — weak cities lose agglomeration. Studies often warn: more fiscal resources need not mean faster GDP per capita. The live question is not only “does the county have money?” but “is money stuck at the wrong spatial scale?”

Horizontal: regional inequality. From the mid-1990s, export growth pulled manufacturing to the coast for logistics reasons — economic geography first, not only tax reform. Agglomeration then self-reinforces fiscal gaps. Public finance’s redistribution mandate pushed large transfers westward to support education and health per capita; progress is real, yet province, urban–rural, and hukou gaps remain open questions for later chapters.¹¹

National totals can balance while every tier — and every person — still does not. Imbalance is TSS’s long homework.

Closing

To know government, know public finance. The 1994 TSS reversed the two ratios and rebuilt central capacity under real constraints — and pushed localities toward land finance and layered imbalances. Judge reform by what it solved and what new constraints it created, not by abstract philosophy alone.

Conveyance fees are already large, yet alone they struggle to explain a decade of high-speed rail and city-building density. Land’s deeper power often sits in collateral and credit — land finance plugged into capital markets becomes land-backed finance, rolling up debt and macro volatility. That is the next chapter’s stage.

Only after seeing why tax-sharing was necessary and successful can you see which parts now need replacing.
