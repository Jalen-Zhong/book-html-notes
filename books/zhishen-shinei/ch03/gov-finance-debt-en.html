CHAPTER THREE · GOVERNMENT INVESTMENT, FINANCING, AND DEBT · MORTGAGING TOMORROW

Chapter 2 ended on a preview ticket: conveyance fees alone struggle to explain dense high-speed rail and industrial-park buildouts. The deeper leverage is rarely the cash sale of land; it is land as collateral and promise — sealing future rents, tax bases, and urbanization visions into today’s loan contracts.¹

This chapter steps onto the financing stage: how urban investment companies turn land into mobile financial assets; how local debt grew from opaque off-balance-sheet claims into a national audit and policy agenda; and how local officials across the investment-promotion table bind land, credit, and firm location into one game.

Selling land is a cash lesson; mortgaging land, issuing bonds, and signaling guarantees is the finance lesson that folds time into investment.

1. Urban investment companies and land-backed finance

Law long drew a hard line: local governments could not borrow from banks the way firms do; before the 2015 Budget Law took effect, they generally could not issue bonds on their own. Yet cities needed roads, parks needed site preparation, and heritage districts needed resettlement — where would the money come from? The answer was dedicated companies: wholly owned by SASAC or a park administration, often branded “construction investment,” “investment and development,” or “tourism development.” Economists and the press call them Local Government Financing Vehicles (LGFVs); in Chinese conversation they are simply chengtou — urban investment platforms.²

In public accounts these firms usually wear three faces at once: they hold land-use rights injected by government; their profits lean on fiscal subsidies and buybacks; and — because markets believe “the government stands behind them” — they can borrow at scale and relatively low cost. Chengdu cultural-tourism platforms rebuilt districts such as Kuanzhai Alley; Wuhu Construction Investment was once a major Chery shareholder; Shanghai Chengtou and Suzhou Industrial Park’s Zhaorun Group track the standard “primary land development + leverage” script.

“Raw” land becomes auction-ready “serviced” land through primary development: resettlement, compensation, and utilities trunking. Capex is heavy, margins thin, coordination hard — platforms usually do this stage; buildings and industrial operations are secondary development by developers or park operators. In public Suzhou SIP cases, the park injects land capital into Zhaorun, the platform grades the site, the park buys it back and conveys it — while the platform mortgages injected land, pledges future conveyance rights, or issues bonds, repaying from buyback fees and fiscal support.³

That is land finance plugged into capital markets: conveyance fees are current cash; mortgages and revenue-right pledges advance unrealized urbanization premia into construction funding. Land does not walk away or vanish — a natural ballast for collateral — so clumsy physical space becomes abstract capital that can be scheduled across time and space.

Industrial parks and tourism districts share the same skeleton with different products: one sells serviced land plus industrial amenities to manufacturers and operators; the other sells refurbished spatial experience to visitors and merchants. Either way, the core is coupling government credit — explicit or implicit — with land assets. Marketized park operators (public discussions often contrast cases such as China Fortune Land) try to replicate the loop on a corporate balance sheet; without inside-the-system guarantees, funding costs and stress tolerance look very different.⁴

A chengtou is not “one more company” on a chart; it is the legal translation of a government that cannot borrow into a juridical person that can.

2. Local government debt

Platform expansion was not smooth accounting growth; it moved like a tide. In the post-GFC stimulus cycle, the center urged local matching investment; platform headcount and liabilities surged. Infrastructure rose fast — and left many projects with weak operating cash, dependent on refinancing and land receipts.⁵

In the 2010s, the National Audit Office twice mapped “local-government-related debt,” splitting repayment, guarantee, and rescue responsibilities — naming as a governance tool: only named stocks can be capped, swapped, and assigned. Around 2013, cadre evaluation began to signal debt; markets learned to price chengtou bonds with premia for administrative rank and regional fiscal strength.

2014 is the usual watershed year in public narratives: the revised Budget Law allowed provincial governments to issue bonds within ceilings; State Council Document No. 43 sought to bar platforms from illegally raising fiscal debt and localities from improper guarantees. The slogan “open the front door, close the back door” meant channeling needed construction finance into transparent government bonds while compressing hidden off-sheet liabilities.⁶

Debt swaps then replaced some high-yield, short-maturity platform claims with longer, cheaper local-government bonds. Special bonds, PPPs, and government guidance funds arrived as tools between “do not borrow wildly” and “still build.” Public research warns: front-door quotas are finite, project yields uneven, and the back door did not slam shut overnight — hidden debt, arrears, and roll-overs remain long homework.⁷

Reading debt is not only reading totals: watch maturity mismatch (short debt, long assets), asset quality (stable cash or not), and whether collateral and repayment sources shrink together when land markets cool. For investors, chengtou pricing long contained a “faith” component; for officials, debt is both the accelerator that builds the city and the exam left for successors and superiors.

Debt governance is the craft of rewriting invisible promises as visible ceilings and responsibilities.

3. Local officials in investment promotion

Platforms and debt answer “where money comes from”; investment promotion answers “what money buys.” Local officials sit on one side of the table holding land quotas, fiscal subsidies, matching pledges — and sometimes immediately buildable serviced plots and standard factories via chengtou. For firms, site choice compares not only wages and logistics but whether “government can get things done.”⁸

Promotion tournaments make GDP, investment, and tax visible performance; finite tenures bias toward “build what can be seen on my watch.” Land-backed finance matches that bias: borrow today, build tomorrow, sell land later — if successors and superiors catch the baton, skylines and résumés thicken together; if land markets turn, idle parks and debt become the next audit’s material.

Successful promotion is rarely one contract; it is a chain of credible promises: when power arrives, when roads open, how schooling works, whether capacity expansions can be coordinated. An official’s value is packaging scattered approvals and resources into a timetable firms can read. Large projects also reverse-feed political capital — “anchor firms” are tax bases and résumé symbols at once.

Risks are symmetric: over-promising subsidies and guarantees writes soft budget constraints into contracts; chasing investment with inefficient capacity debts environmental and overcapacity bills; treating chengtou as ATMs entangles financial risk with cadre incentives. Public debate therefore stresses putting debt into evaluation, normalizing preferential policies, and commercializing platforms — all ways to rewrite the relative prices officials face.⁹

In investment promotion, officials are not bystander stampers; they are the nodes that translate land-backed finance into firm location contracts.

Closing

Chengtou translate governments that cannot borrow directly into companies that can build and finance; land-backed finance mortgages future urbanization premia into today’s steel and cement; local-debt policy tries to reweave that off-sheet net into the budget’s front door. Officials in promotion play salesperson, coordinator, and contingent guarantor at once — when the triangle tilts, growth stories slide into debt stories.

The next chapter turns from “who finances, who owes” to “how government enters industry directly”: equity stakes, subsidies, and guidance funds rewriting market outcomes in stories such as BOE and photovoltaics. Financing is accelerator and brake; industrial policy is another hand on the wheel.

Only after seeing how land becomes a loan can you see why cities can “pre-spend” their future — and who pays the interest.
