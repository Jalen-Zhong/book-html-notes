PREFACE · Start by Understanding the Status Quo · a ticket into the institution

Open a book on China’s economy and you may expect prices, cycles, and growth accounting. This one centers another character: government and policy. Not because the author prefers “talking politics,” but because in the Chinese setting government shapes how the cake is sliced and how it is baked. Studying the economy while skipping government is like studying fish without water.¹

The preface’s stance is simple: understand the status quo first, then judge. Imported models can be tools; they must not become pre-written verdicts. If reality diverges from the model and we only sneer at reality, we forfeit sympathetic understanding.

Being “inside the institution” is not taking sides; it is seeing how the political-economic organism you inhabit actually runs.

1. Why government is the protagonist

The opening is blunt: boomtown skylines and crushing housing prices, dry official documents and noisy headlines, are facets of one organism. The book’s lead is neither the micro price mechanism nor a macro cycle formula, but how government organizes resources, sets incentives, and reshapes the choice sets of firms and households.

The material grew out of lecture notes at Fudan University and The Chinese University of Hong Kong, Shenzhen. Technical detail is pared back; the core claim is raised: in China you can barely talk about the economy without government; to judge the organism, first learn how it operates. We live inside it, and our development depends on understanding it.²

Political economy is too complex for any single theory or news feed to light every side. Which side is “useful” depends on the reader’s question: practitioners need the institutional weather beyond daily deals; economics students need a bridge between Western textbooks and Chinese facts; citizens want to decode political-economy news. The preface folds all three hopes into one volume.

Three preface promises (reading coordinates)

What
Describe mechanisms as they are
Why
Explain incentives and constraints
What next
Prefer policies already in motion
Stance
Sympathetic understanding · do not force theory onto facts

When the narrative must touch “what should be done,” the author prioritizes explaining what government thinks should be done and is doing — often more useful to readers than a personal prescription. Grasping policy intent and constraint is itself part of reading opportunity and risk.

Ask how the machine turns before asking how you wish it would turn.

2. Three audiences, one discipline

Practitioners in finance, investment, and industry can treat chapters as institutional weather maps outside the deal room: when central–local fiscal rules, land finance, or debt constraints shift, cash flows and valuation logic often shift with them.

Economics students often feel the gap between Western theory texts and Chinese cases. The book deliberately puts Chinese government on stage as a bridge — not to reject theory, but to demand that theory meet observable institutions.

Non-specialists can use it as a decoder for political-economy news: keywords in documents, local investment slogans, and “rebalancing” talk usually wire back to micro mechanisms in Part I.³

One book, three doorways; the shared discipline is facts before verdicts.

3. Micro mechanisms, then macro outcomes

Remember the spine in one line: follow local government investment and financing — mechanisms first, macro consequences second.

Part I asks what localities do, where money comes from, and how risk piles up: assignment of tasks, the tax-sharing reform and land finance, LGFVs and debt, industrial policy and guidance funds. These chapters open the machine — screws of authority, incentives, and funding chains.

Part II reinstalls the screws in a larger picture: urbanization and regional imbalance, housing and household leverage, debt and financial risk, low consumption and excess capacity, internal–external imbalance and rebalancing, ending in a synthesis on government and development.⁴

The prose stays lean: main logic and key facts first; detail deferred to further reading. Even if you only memorize the map, you can enter the chapters oriented.

Micro is how localities finance and build; macro is how the nation reshapes as a result.
Footnotes
⁴ From local mechanisms to macro phenomena Linking local incentives to national imbalance is a standard move in contemporary China research: land finance and urbanization, local debt and financial stability, investment/export drive and weak consumption — a dense public literature. Framing “mechanisms then phenomena” as reading order tells you Part II is not another book; it is Part I under a magnifying glass.
Why it matters: keeps eight chapters from reading like unrelated dossiers.

4. Data and writing conventions

The book uses abundant data. For readability, common series (e.g. China Statistical Yearbook, standard Wind pulls) often lack line-by-line cites; uncommon figures or second-hand research numbers are sourced. That is a compromise between popular readability and lecture notes — not “unsourced.”

The preface also stresses academic norms: recent frontier work is absorbed; the book can serve as course material; companion slides come via the publisher’s channel. Treat “easy to read” and “checkable” as two faces of one volume.⁵

Smooth reading is the experience; traceability is the discipline.
Footnotes
⁵ Public doors to yearbooks and macro series NBS China Statistical Yearbook and provincial yearbooks, Ministry of Finance budget outturns, and central-bank / financial-regulator releases are standard places to verify “common” macro and fiscal series. Commercial terminals help; classrooms still return to official definitions. The preface’s citation strategy admits the trade-off between reading flow and footnote load.
Why it matters: teaches readers how to reproduce “common data” themselves.

5. Acknowledgments as a knowledge supply chain

Read the thanks as a supply-chain list. A decade-plus surge in domestic economics and deepening overseas China research made the citation thickness possible — without that flood of work, the book’s density is hard to imagine.

Fudan’s institutional–historical bent and high-frequency workshops were a starting point; seminars, policy talks, and colleagues’ writing touch nearly every chapter theme. Years in a standing discussion group forged scattered ideas into a frame.⁶

Fieldwork in 2017–2018, plus talks with entrepreneurs, investors, and officials, shifted the lens: the book is not only a literature survey; it carries field texture. Colleagues’ comments, RAs’ data work, student test-readers, and editors at Shanghai People’s Publishing House / Century Wenjing complete the chain — labor made public so readers see that a hard book that still reads smoothly is a community product.

Acknowledgments are not polite padding; they map the social network of being “inside.”

6. Three epigraphs: evidence, wholes, humility

Before the body text, three road signs stand on the page. Wang Chong of the Eastern Han insists that claims need effective verification and arguments need evidence — nailing “understand the status quo” to an empiricist post.

Schumpeter reminds us that “the economy” is a slice analysts extract from a social torrent; nothing is purely economic, and other dimensions are often more important. Putting government center stage admits that politics, organization, and history were never exogenous noise.⁷

Gerschenkron warns that a strict conceptual frame can clarify problems — and also trick us into treating the problem as the answer. Do not underestimate the complexity of economic reality or overestimate the quality of scientific tools. For social science’s itch for universal laws, this is a sobering dose.⁸

Together the three epigraphs are almost the preface’s philosophy in miniature: evidence first, holistic vision, theoretical humility — then on to local governments’ powers and tasks.

Drop the fantasy of one law for all places before you claim to read China’s story carefully.
Footnotes
⁷ Schumpeter on “economy” as abstraction In the development-theory tradition, Joseph Schumpeter stresses that economic phenomena sit inside broader social processes; analytic abstraction is necessary, but forgetting its borders turns a slice into the whole. Citing that intellectual-history coordinate legitimates bringing government and non-economic dimensions on stage — without demanding readers become historians of thought.
Why it matters: explains why China’s story cannot be crushed into pure price-theory drills.
Source: Schumpeter, The Theory of Economic Development — public intellectual-history intros
⁸ Gerschenkron and historical perspective on backwardness Alexander Gerschenkron’s discussions of economic backwardness and substitutive institutional arrangements are staples in development economics: latecomers need not copy early paths, yet overconfident frameworks squash complex history into one formula.
