CHAPTER SEVEN · Domestic and International Imbalances · Part II macro core

Open with two contradictory signs: a Chinese restaurant on the Cayman Islands; a “We do not sell Chinese goods” notice on a Puerto Rico souvenir shop — with Yiwu goods still on the shelves. Globalization put “Made in China” everywhere, and backlash everywhere. After WTO entry in 2001, China became the workshop of the world; by around 2019, manufacturing value-added was on the order of 28% of the global total. Behind the success sit two problems: at home, production and investment outweigh household consumption; abroad, undigested capacity must be exported, so when foreign demand shakes, trade friction arrives.¹

Three movements follow: how underconsumption and overcapacity co-evolve; why the US–China trade conflict is more than tariff arithmetic; and how rebalancing and “dual circulation with the domestic loop as the mainstay” rewrite income distribution, urbanization, and the local state’s role.

Diplomacy extends domestic politics; external trade imbalances extend internal structural imbalances.

1. Underconsumption and overcapacity

The sharpest imprint is weak consumption. In public accounts, China’s household consumption share of GDP runs well below that of the US, Europe, and Japan; total consumption fell for years in the growth era before a partial rebound, while the household disposable-income share of GDP also slid — thrift (high saving) on one side, a thinner income slice on the other. Rising saving rates intertwine with demographic change after the one-child policy, thinner public social spending, and housing/mortgage pressure; falling income shares reinforce a local development model biased toward capital, projects, and firms.²

When more income stays with firms and government, spending tilts toward plants, machines, and infrastructure — a high investment share; a further slice becomes net exports. After WTO entry, investment and net exports surged while consumption’s share contracted: the structural fingerprint of the “workshop.” Early on, shifting resources from consumption into capital accumulation is a common catch-up path; manufacturing’s learning effects are strong. Later, the same mechanism produces four complications: productive investment gets harder; capacity cannot be absorbed and debts deepen; a rising capital share widens inequality; surplus spills abroad and friction rises.³

Two doors into underconsumption

Thrift

Thin slice
Lower household income share
Spend flip

Spillover

Overcapacity can be read through efficiency decline, saving–investment and external imbalance, and lagged upgrading — a supply problem and a demand/incentive problem at once. Around 2007 the structural issue was already named in public; the global financial crisis then forced stimulus-style investment that stabilized growth while deepening the skew. When domestic consumption is weak and foreign demand unsteady, funds drift toward infrastructure and property, locking again onto the debt and housing stories of Chapters 3–6.⁴

Underconsumption is not a moral scolding: it is the fingerprint of income distribution, social insurance, and local incentives on expenditure-side GDP.

2. The US–China trade conflict

In an open world, internal imbalance maps onto the balance of payments: China’s current-account surplus and the US deficit are the starkest pair in the global imbalance map. America’s ability to absorb others’ net exports rests on the dollar’s reserve role — the “exorbitant privilege”; the rising share of the bilateral China deficit in the US deficit structure made China the political “main counterpart.” Trade conflict is rarely a tariff worksheet; it is a pressure cooker of structure, employment politics, and technology rivalry.⁵

Employment shocks are told as “China stole the factories.” Academic “China shock” work (Autor and coauthors) does link heavier import exposure to sharper local manufacturing job losses. Zoom out, though, and US manufacturing employment shares have fallen since the 1970s; fewer workers need not mean a matching collapse in value-added — technology substitution matters. Imports also cut downstream costs and support some jobs. In populist anti-globalization weather, “explanation” often loses to “scapegoat” — protection becomes ballot language.⁶

The longer tension is technological: manufacturing still hosts most R&D and patents. China’s manufacturing value-added relative to the US moved from far behind to ahead; PCT filings and high-quality paper shares climbed fast — strong flow signals, thinner stock still. Latecomers often climb manufacture → patents → basic science; the US fears its market and innovation hub drifting apart. Tech containment can hurt short-run and also force import-substitution loops — if the domestic market truly grows.⁷

A trade war is like smashing the thermometer: the fever reading vanishes for a while; the structural fever remains.

3. Rebalancing and the domestic circulation

In 2020, “dual circulation with the domestic loop as the mainstay and domestic–international loops reinforcing each other” answered internal imbalance and a tougher external environment. The point is not to close the door, but to plug growth more into household income and consumption: supply and demand are one coin — cutting capacity is also adjusting investment; upgrading industry is also upgrading income and consumption structure. Public Central Economic Work Conference language stressed keeping supply-side structural reform as the mainline while managing the demand side and unblocking production–distribution–circulation–consumption bottlenecks.⁸

Raising income and consumption requires urbanization and services: the more automated high-end manufacturing becomes, the less factories can keep expanding mass employment; trade, logistics, care, and digital services need dense cities. Hukou, land, and public services for permanent residents must catch up (Chapter 5 threads). More resources must shift from excessive firm/government retention toward households: curb local investment urges, raise livelihood spending — less wasteful capacity, more human-capital investment, and weaker dependence on land-finance leverage. Transferring SOE capital into social security is one institutional pipe from “firm saving” to “household security.”⁹

Income

Dual circulation also stresses higher-standard opening: exports once created manufacturing jobs; imports can create service jobs in commerce and logistics and raise real purchasing power. A unified national market is not a slogan — scale makes internal connectivity almost a mini-globalization, needing infrastructure and institutional grinding together. Only when local governments shift from production-type to service-type roles, and factor markets keep reforming, can the domestic market carry the “main circulation.”

Rebalancing is an engine swap: not smashing the old motor, but letting income and consumption also pull the car.

Closing note

This chapter flips the “workshop of the world” medal: underconsumption and overcapacity are one coin, trade conflict the external projection, dual circulation both forced and chosen rebalancing. The through-line of the book — local incentives, land finance, investment financing, industrial policy, urbanization, and debt — writes itself, under openness, into current accounts and tariff politics.

The next chapter turns to deeper mechanisms and outlook for government and development: imbalance shows not “whether to open,” but whether, after opening, the domestic circulation can truly stand. To stand inside the story is to see structure — not to shout slogans from the shore.

To read imbalance: see consumption and capacity first, then trade and technology, and only then a dual circulation with a real mainstay.
