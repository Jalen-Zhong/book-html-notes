CHAPTER SIX · Debt and Risk · Part II macro core

At the end of the previous chapter, risk was already at the door: local and household debt stacked together, house prices binding banks, budgets, and families. This chapter invites that risk inside and tells it in four movements — how debt deepens recessions; why Europe and the US built tall debt towers; where China’s leverage sits; and why resolution cannot be a one-shot crash diet.¹

Public primers often start with a mortgaged household: optimism underprices liability; when shocks arrive, room to maneuver narrows. Debt carries three asymmetries — inelastic principal, elastic income, and spending that can jump without notice. Scale those asymmetries to the macro level and you get fire sales, credit crunches, and widening inequality.

Debt is not a numbers game: it nails future promises onto today’s balance sheets — the tighter the nails, the harder it is to turn in a downturn.

1. Debt and recession

With high leverage, a fall in asset prices pushes holders to sell to deleverage; selling pushes prices lower — the spiral tightens. Banks see collateral shrink and bad loans rise, so they cut credit; firms’ funding chains snap, and investment and jobs fall together. If prices then slide, nominal debts do not shrink — real burdens rise. That is Fisher’s debt–deflation loop, the harshest mechanical sound in Depression narratives.²

Japan’s “lost decades” and several post-subprime economies are told by Koo and others as balance-sheet recessions: the private sector prioritizes repairing balance sheets and will not borrow to invest even at very low rates. Macro policy that only watches the policy rate may hit agents who no longer want more leverage.³

Recession also rewrites inequality: law prioritizes creditor claims; debtors — often more leveraged households and smaller firms with thinner buffers — take losses first. Public discussion often cites Spanish mortgages: even after surrendering the home and filing bankruptcy, under some rules principal obligations may linger, lengthening social fractures after the crash.⁴

Debt–deflation is not a mood word: it is the mechanical process by which nominal contracts grow heavier when fire sales, credit, and prices move down together.

2. Why debt piled up: lessons from Europe and the US

The first driver of towering debt is often not mathematics but human nature: the urge to satisfy wants early, plus over-optimism about the future. When assets rise, leverage looks like “smart money”; if supervisors and ratings also swim in procyclical illusion, systemic risk accumulates precisely when “everyone feels safe.”⁵

US subprime sliced housing mortgages into layered securities and derivatives — risk looked dispersed but was chain-amplified. The euro debt crisis reminded another face: uneven fiscal discipline inside a monetary union, banks holding sovereign debt, and one country’s servicing stress turning quickly into union-wide financial shock. The morphologies differ — private shadow-banking explosion versus sovereign–bank “twin crisis” — but share optimism pricing + high leverage + contagion channels.

3. China’s debt and risk

Public figures often set the coordinates: by end-2018 China’s total debt was about 258% of GDP — roughly on par with the US, above many large emerging economies — and debt grew faster than GDP over the decade, so leverage roughly doubled in feel because “debt rose faster than the pie.” External net liabilities are not the main pressure; the weight sits more inside — corporates (especially some SOEs and platform-linked entities), local hidden debt, and rising household leverage stacked together.⁶

The proximate trigger is often dated to the post-2008 “4 trillion” stabilization: when external demand collapsed, investment and credit stepped in fast, holding up a piece of China and the world economy — and writing capacity, leverage, and local financing-platform expansion onto the next stage’s balance sheets. Overcapacity can be read through efficiency decline, external imbalance, and lagged industrial upgrading — a supply-side problem and a demand-structure / incentive problem at once.⁷

One public conclusion matters: China’s debt risk is often not a Wall Street-style speculative blast but the structural bill left by fiscal and resource-allocation mechanisms in a high-growth era — investment finance, land, industrial policy, and soft budget constraints tangled together. Deleveraging therefore rarely finishes with “tighten money” alone.

Low external debt does not mean internal debt is light; what is heavy is often the growth model written onto corporate and local ledgers.

4. Resolving debt risk

The public recipe sounds simple: repay the stock, curb the flow. For individuals that means higher income or lower spending; macro-wise, if everyone cuts spending at once, a deflationary fallacy of composition can appear — money becomes “more valuable,” so owed amounts feel heavier. Deleveraging must therefore be paced and structured, not a simultaneous slam of every brake.⁸

The post-2015 policy mix offers a visible timeline: the new Budget Law and Document 43 “open the front door, block the back door”; swap bonds turned high-interest non-standard stock into more standardized local bonds; supply-side “three cuts, one reduction, one strengthening” and 2018 “structural deleveraging” aimed at local and SOE leverage; hidden-debt governance tried to unify definitions and stop new buildup. The metaphor is vivid: a heavy person must eat less, but brutal starvation can trigger complications — reform must treat soft constraints, capacity, and fiscal incentives, not only spreadsheet numbers.⁹

Section notes
⁸ Stock repayment, flow containment, and the fallacy of composition Micro “tighten belts to repay” is rational; macro simultaneous collapse of consumption and investment can deepen deflation and income declines, making debt ratios look worse. Public macro discussion therefore stresses pairing deleveraging with growth stability, restructuring, and income policies — avoiding everyone deleveraging at once.
Why it matters: explains why policy language keeps saying “prudent” and “structural,” not “slam every brake.”

Hidden-debt governance (including 2018 public discussions of unifying definitions) sought to stop new implicit commitments and illegal guarantees.

Closing note

This chapter scales debt from a household mortgage’s micro unease into a macro story of recession spirals, Western contrasts, China’s structure, and paced resolution. If the essence of risk is fiscal and resource-allocation mechanisms, the remedy cannot be only a few lines from a financial-supervision manual — it necessarily ties to harder budget constraints, local incentives, SOE reform, capacity exit, and whether “people-centered urbanization” can continue.

The next chapter turns the camera toward domestic and international imbalance: low consumption, overcapacity, and external friction are another face of a high-saving, high-investment, high-leverage growth model under openness. Understanding debt is preparation for why rebalancing has to be done.

Reading debt from inside the system: see the spiral first, then the structure, and only then a paced way out.
